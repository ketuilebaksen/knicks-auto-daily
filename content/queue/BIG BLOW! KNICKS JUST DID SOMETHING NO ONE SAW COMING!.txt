What actually happened is that Stefan Bondy of the New York Post wrote a column. In it, he said a consolidating move, maybe even two, like the ones the Knicks made in twenty twenty-three twenty-four, should be appealing to Leon Rose. And then he explained why that’s a lot easier to say than to do. That’s the real story. And it’s a good one, because it’s the most honest description of the Knicks’ trade situation I’ve seen all season. So today, let’s break down what a consolidating trade is, why the Knicks have done it before, why it’s so hard this time, and what the realistic options actually look like.

First, what does consolidating mean? It’s simple. You trade two or three players for one better player. You take depth you don’t need and turn it into quality you do need. Teams do it when they have too many good players at one position and not enough at another. And that’s exactly where the Knicks are right now, and it’s been true since the first week of the season. They have a crowded group of guards and wings: Jalen Brunson, Josh Hart, Mikal Bridges, OG Anunoby, Landry Shamet, Miles McBride, Tyler Kolek, Jose Alvarado and Jordan Clarkson. That’s nine players for maybe six or seven real rotation spots. And at the same time, they’re thin behind Karl-Anthony Towns at center. So the idea writes itself: turn some of those guards into a big man.

And Bondy is right that the Knicks have done this before. In twenty twenty-three twenty-four, they made two big consolidating moves. In December of twenty twenty-three, they traded RJ Barrett and Immanuel Quickley to Toronto for OG Anunoby and Precious Achiuwa. Then at the deadline in February twenty twenty-four, they traded Evan Fournier, Quentin Grimes and a couple of end-of-bench players for Bojan Bogdanović and Alec Burks. Two trades, both turning several pieces into fewer, more useful ones. The Anunoby deal especially changed the franchise. So when Bondy says this kind of move should appeal to Rose, he’s pointing to Rose’s own history.

And there’s a second benefit to consolidating that people forget. It’s not just about getting a better player. It’s about clearing the logjam. Mike Brown said recently that it’s hard to find minutes for Bridges, and even for Hart at times, because Shamet and McBride are playing so well. Alvarado has been out of the rotation some nights. Clarkson didn’t play in the second Cleveland game. When you have nine guards and wings for six or seven spots, somebody good is always sitting. Turning two of those players into one big man would make the rotation simpler, give everyone clearer roles, and fill the biggest hole on the roster at the same time.

It’s also worth remembering what made those twenty twenty-three twenty-four moves work. The Knicks had young players with real value, like RJ Barrett and Immanuel Quickley, and contracts that other teams wanted. They had more first-round picks to play with. And they weren’t up against the second apron the way they are now. Those trades were possible because the Knicks had the assets. Today, they’ve already spent most of those assets. They gave Brooklyn five first-round picks for Bridges. They only have one tradable first-round pick, in twenty thirty-three. So the playbook is the same, but the bank account is a lot smaller. That changes what kind of consolidation is even possible.

Now, here’s where it gets hard. Bondy laid out the problems, and every one of them is real.

Problem number one: the Knicks already tried this summer, and it didn’t work. Bondy wrote that the Knicks searched for backup big men all summer and came up empty. They wanted a young, up-and-coming center who could grow with the team. They ended up signing Andre Drummond to a minimum deal instead. If the right player wasn’t available in the summer, when teams have more flexibility, it’s even harder to find in the middle of the season.

Problem number two: nobody wants Bridges’ contract right now. Bondy has written before that trading Bridges would help clear room under the second apron to keep Towns and Hart. But he also said teams are not eager to take on that contract. Bridges makes about thirty-three and a half million this season, and his deal rises every year after that, with about one hundred sixteen and a half million left over the following three seasons, including a player option at the end. After the start he’s had, other teams know the Knicks would be selling low. Even with his recent improvement, including an eighteen-point game against Indiana, his value is far below what the Knicks paid for him.

Problem number three: trading Hart would hurt the chemistry. Bondy said losing Hart would be a blow to the team’s chemistry, and that’s true. Hart is part of the Villanova core with Brunson and Bridges. After the Knicks beat Cleveland earlier this month, Kenny Atkinson pointed to that exact continuity as one of the reasons the Knicks are so hard to beat. You don’t break that up lightly, especially while Hart is still working his way back from calf and ankle problems.

Problem number four: McBride is a natural trade candidate, but he doesn’t bring much back on his own. Bondy called McBride a natural trade candidate because he’s on an expiring contract. He’s going to be a free agent next summer, and the Knicks may not be able to afford to keep him without crossing the second apron. But here’s the catch. McBride only makes about four million dollars. Because the Knicks are above the first apron, they can’t take back more salary than they send out. So McBride by himself can only bring back a player making about four million or less. That’s not much, and it’s definitely not enough to land a real starting-caliber big. As Bondy put it, trading McBride wouldn’t bring back a significant asset because of his contract.

So the Knicks have a clear need, a history of making this kind of trade, and four big problems standing in the way. What can they actually do?

Let me lay out the realistic menu, from smallest to biggest.

Option one: McBride alone for a pick. The original video asks, trade McBride for what? A few second-round picks? The Knicks already have about twelve of them, according to ESPN. Adding more doesn’t help much. The video says the only version that really helps is if a team gives the Knicks a first-round pick for McBride. And it admits that’s a long shot. I agree. A team would have to really love McBride and want his Bird rights before free agency. It’s possible, but unlikely, especially for a player on an expiring deal.

Option two: McBride plus a small salary for a backup big. This is the one I think makes the most sense. McBride makes about three point nine six million. Pacôme Dadiet makes about two point nine eight million, and he’s on an expiring deal after the Knicks declined his option. Together, that’s about six point nine four million. In a two-for-one trade, after the Knicks sign a minimum player to fill the roster, the player coming back could make roughly five point three million with a veteran minimum refill, or about six point four million with a rookie minimum. That’s enough for a real rotation center. Not a star, but a real player who can protect the rim for fifteen minutes a night.

There’s a timing wrinkle here too. ESPN’s Bobby Marks noted the Knicks can’t sign a new player until February fourteenth, because they’re only about eight hundred nine thousand dollars under the second apron. A prorated minimum contract only fits under that cushion late in the season. And the Knicks can only stay below fourteen players for two weeks. So a two-for-one deal like this would most likely have to happen close to the February eleventh trade deadline.

Option three: McBride, Dadiet and Kolek for a slightly better big. If you add Tyler Kolek’s two point three million, the outgoing salary goes up to about nine point two million. But now it’s three players for one, and the Knicks need two minimum players to refill the roster. That eats up a lot of the room. The incoming player could make roughly five to seven million, depending on the type of minimum contracts. So adding Kolek doesn’t give you as much extra room as you’d think, and you’d be giving up the player who’s taken over the backup point guard job. SNY’s Ian Begley did say recently that Kolek could be part of a deal for a backup center if Drummond isn’t the answer in April. So it’s on the table. But I’d only do it for the right player.

Option four: after December fifteenth, a different mix. On December fifteenth, Jose Alvarado, Jordan Clarkson, Mohamed Diawara and Andre Drummond all become trade-eligible. Alvarado makes about four point four million. Alvarado and McBride together are about eight point four million, and in a two-for-one, the incoming center could make up to about six point eight million with a veteran minimum refill. That’s the biggest number the Knicks can reach without touching a starter, and it keeps Kolek. If you’re looking for the most realistic consolidation trade, that’s it: two guards for one big, after December fifteenth, finalized close to the deadline.

Option five: a starter. Bridges or Hart. This is the big one. And the original video is very clear about it: no starters are going to be traded at the deadline. I mostly agree. The video’s reasoning is that this same starting five won a title last season, and Leon Rose isn’t going to break it up in the middle of a title defense. If a starter gets moved, the video says, it’ll happen next summer. That fits everything we’ve heard from Bondy, ESPN and Begley.

So who could the big man actually be? I want to be careful, because nobody has reported any talks. But a few names have come up in columns over the past month, and some of them fit the math. Charles Bassey of the Warriors has a cap number of about two point four five million, exactly the same as Drummond’s, so a one-for-one swap would match to the dollar. Bassey also signed this summer, so he becomes trade-eligible on December fifteenth, the same day as Drummond. The Clippers have two young six-eleven bigs on cheap deals, Yanic Konan Niederhäuser and Baba Miller, and the Clippers have been struggling badly. Moussa Diabaté in Charlotte and Dylan Cardwell in Sacramento have been mentioned too. None of those players are stars. But they’re the kind of players the Knicks can actually afford, and the kind of players who could protect the rim when Towns sits.

And let’s not forget the other option: that the answer is already on the roster. Andre Drummond has been much better lately. Mike Brown named him the defensive player of the game in the first Cleveland win, when he held Evan Mobley to oh for three. Against Milwaukee, he grabbed fourteen rebounds in sixteen minutes. Begley said Drummond has been good, but he’s not sure he’s the guy in April. That’s fair. But if Drummond keeps improving, the pressure to make a trade gets a lot smaller.

So what does all of this tell us? Here’s the honest summary.

The Knicks need size. Everyone agrees on that. Bondy, ESPN, Begley, and the original video.

The Knicks have too many guards. That’s both the problem and the solution, because those guards are their trade currency.

But the guards they’d most likely trade are on small contracts. And above the first apron, small contracts can only bring back small contracts. That’s why the realistic target is a solid backup big in the five to seven million dollar range, not a star.

And the big contracts, Bridges and Hart, are either hard to move or too important to the chemistry to move right now.

That’s exactly why Bondy’s column is so useful. It says the idea makes sense, and then it explains why the idea is hard. That’s the kind of honesty you don’t usually get in trade coverage. Most videos stop at the first half, the part where a trade sounds exciting, and skip the second half, the part where you find out why it probably won’t happen.

Let me also address one line from the column that’s worth correcting slightly. The video says Towns and Hart are both eligible for extensions, but the Knicks cut off talks because of James Dolan’s second-apron mandate. Here’s the nuance. The deadline for veteran extensions on multi-year contracts passed on October nineteenth. So right now, during the season, neither one can simply be extended unless Towns declines his player option or the Knicks decline Hart’s team option as part of a new deal. Realistically, both of those decisions are coming next summer. And Towns has a sixty-one million dollar player option, while Hart has a team option worth about twenty-two point four million. That’s why the money talk keeps coming back.

There’s also the McBride question, and it’s a tough one emotionally. McBride has been one of the best stories on this team. He came back from core muscle surgery during last season’s title run. He had a plus-minus of plus thirty-nine against Milwaukee. He’s hitting threes, he’s defending the other team’s best guards, and he’s playing for his next contract. Fans love him, and for good reason. But the Knicks lost Mitchell Robinson for nothing this summer, because they couldn’t pay him what Boston did without crossing the second apron. If McBride is likely to leave in free agency for the same reason, the smart move might be to use him now to get the big man they couldn’t get in the summer. It wouldn’t be easy. But it might be the right call.

And the video makes a point I really agree with. The Knicks aren’t going to make a trade just to make a trade. If the player they want isn’t available, or they can’t afford him, they’ll stand pat. That’s how a good front office works. Last season, they made a small, smart deadline deal for Alvarado when McBride needed core muscle surgery, and Alvarado ended up playing real minutes in the Finals. That’s the model. Small, targeted moves that fix a specific need. Not a blockbuster for the sake of headlines, but a trade that solves one problem without creating two new ones.

Now, the video also spends a lot of time on the championship odds from its sponsor. I’m not going to promote betting odds. But the general point is fair. A lot of people outside New York have doubted this team all season. The Knicks are seven and six after starting oh and three, so they’ve gone seven and three since. They just won a close game in Toronto in overtime, after going one and six in clutch games before that. Brunson hit the dagger. Towns had twenty-eight and seventeen. Shamet scored twenty-three. That’s not a team that needs to panic. It’s a team that needs one smart move and a healthy Josh Hart.

And the video’s belief in this team isn’t crazy. The Knicks have a history of flipping a switch in the playoffs. Last season, they came back from double-digit deficits again and again on the way to the title. Brunson led the playoffs in fourth-quarter scoring. So I understand why fans aren’t panicking. I’m not either, not at seven and six. But I also think the regular season matters for seeding, and home-court advantage could matter a lot in May.

Let me give you a simple timeline for how this could play out. Between now and December fifteenth, I don’t expect much. The Knicks will watch Drummond, watch Hart’s health, and watch Bridges to see if his eighteen-point game against Indiana was the start of something. After December fifteenth, more players become trade-eligible, and the menu gets bigger. In January, teams that are struggling, like the Clippers, the Pelicans or the Kings, start deciding whether they’re sellers. And in the days right before February eleventh, that’s when a two-for-one deal is most likely, because the Knicks can sign a replacement a few days after the deadline. If nothing happens by then, the next real window is next summer, when Towns and Hart’s contract decisions come due.

So here’s my verdict on Bondy’s consolidation idea. It’s the right idea. It’s the same kind of move that helped build this championship team. But this time, the pieces are smaller, the rules are tighter, and the best targets are harder to find. If the Knicks do something, I’d expect a two-guards-for-one-big trade, probably McBride with Alvarado or Dadiet, finalized close to the deadline. If the right big isn’t there, I expect them to wait until summer.
