Alright. The name we need to talk about is Bruce Brown.

According to Brett Siegel of ClutchPoints, who discussed this on the Clutch Scoops podcast, Brown could potentially become a target for the Knicks before training camp begins. Arthur Hill of Hoops Rumors highlighted Siegel's comments, with the New York Post also covering the possibility.

And this is one of those names where the fit starts making sense almost immediately.

Bruce Brown is a veteran guard who built his entire reputation on doing a little bit of everything. He defends. He plays physically. He handles different offensive responsibilities. And he gives you that toughness that becomes incredibly valuable when a game gets ugly.

And this isn't theoretical for him. Brown won a championship in Denver being exactly that kind of player — the connective piece who guarded up, cut off the ball, made the extra pass, and did all the things that don't show up in a box score but absolutely show up in a series. He has already proven he can be that guy on a title team. That matters more than any stat line I could read you.

Now, Siegel pointed out a connection that genuinely deserves attention. Brown is a CAA client.

And if you follow this front office at all, you know why that matters. Leon Rose and William Wesley both came out of CAA before running this organization. Those relationships have been a real thread through how New York does business. It doesn't guarantee anything — but when a CAA client hits the market and the Knicks have a need, you pay attention.

But here's the part that really got me. Siegel specifically compared Brown's playing style to Josh Hart.

Come on. You tell a Knicks fan that and we're immediately listening. Because we know exactly what Hart brings. It's not about drawing up plays for him. It's the rebounding. The physicality. The energy. The willingness to do the dirty work. All those possessions where he somehow finds a way to affect the game without the ball ever being called for him.

So when you hear Bruce Brown described in those terms, it's easy to understand why New York could see something.

And I genuinely like the idea of adding another player who doesn't need the basketball to make an impact. When you're building a serious contender expecting to play deep into the postseason, you need guys who can survive different kinds of games.

There are nights where the offense looks beautiful. Ball moving, shots falling, everything easy. Then there are playoff nights where nothing is easy — where every possession is a fistfight and the whistle disappears. That's when physicality, defense, hustle, rebounding, versatility, and experience decide games.

Brown has already shown he can embrace that role at the highest level.

But New York might not be alone here. Siegel also referenced rumors connecting the Los Angeles Lakers to Brown, so there could be real competition if this develops.

The interesting part, though, is that Siegel views New York as the more realistic destination. And that's the detail I'm watching. There's a big difference between saying a team could theoretically use a player and saying that landing spot actually looks realistic.

Now, we shouldn't treat this like a done deal, because it absolutely isn't. Right now this is a possibility being floated before camp.

But if Brown is still available and New York sees a chance to strengthen this roster without disrupting what already works, that conversation is worth having. Because this team does not need somebody coming in trying to reinvent everything. They need complementary players. Guys who understand their role, compete defensively, and don't disappear emotionally when a game turns physical.

That said, let me give you the honest other side, because there is one.

Roster balance is a real question. How many players with overlapping skill sets do you actually need? Look at this guard rotation right now — Brunson, Josh Hart, Jose Alvarado, Landry Shamet, Deuce McBride, Tyler Kolek. That is already crowded. Where do Bruce Brown's minutes come from? Whose do you take?

And there's a version of this where you sign a good player and he simply never gets on the floor, which helps nobody.

The counter is that Brown has genuinely played bigger than his position throughout his career — he's guarded up, he's played in small lineups as a de facto forward. And this roster is far thinner on the wing than it is at guard. If that's how New York would use him, the fit gets a lot cleaner.

But for a team with championship expectations, having too many trustworthy players is a problem I would much rather have than spending April and May wishing we had one more.

So Bruce Brown is absolutely a name to monitor as camp approaches.

Alright, second story. And this one isn't about bringing somebody in. It's about whether the Knicks should keep investing in somebody who's already here.

Pacôme Dadiet. And this is where things get uncomfortable.

Kenny Klusker of Daily Knicks argued that New York has what he called essentially a no-brainer decision coming with Dadiet's contract.

The deadline is October thirty-first. By that date, the Knicks have to decide whether to exercise his fourth-year team option for the 2027-28 season. That option is worth approximately five point four million dollars.

And based on what we've seen, there are legitimate reasons to question whether guaranteeing that money makes sense.

Dadiet was selected twenty-fifth overall in the 2024 draft. He's still only twenty-one. And whenever we talk about somebody that young, I think we have to be careful about acting like development is finished. It isn't.

But at some point, a front office also has to make decisions based on what a player has actually shown. And so far, Dadiet hasn't established himself in the rotation at all. Across two seasons he's appeared in only forty-seven regular season games, and most of those minutes came in garbage time.

That alone creates a difficult situation. But what really raised the concern was Summer League.

Because normally, when a former first-round pick is entering his third season, you want him walking into Summer League looking like he's above that level. You want control. You want confidence. You want him looking like one of the best players on the floor.

Instead, he struggled. Across four appearances in July, the six-nine forward shot just thirty-six point four percent from the field. From three, only sixteen percent. And the number that bothers me most — he finished with more than twice as many turnovers as assists.

That last one is what separates this from a shooting slump. Shooting fluctuates. Four games is a small sample and young players have bad stretches. But when you combine inefficient shooting with turnovers and a general lack of command against that level of competition, you start asking harder questions.

And unfortunately Summer League wasn't the end of it. Dadiet also played for Ivory Coast in FIBA World Cup qualifying at the end of August, and by all accounts had three more underwhelming performances.

Nobody's asking a twenty-one year old to dominate NBA playoff games. But when you're evaluating a first-round pick entering year three, you want to see him separate himself against lesser competition. Those signs haven't been consistent enough.

Now here's where it becomes bigger than whether the Knicks believe he can improve.

New York exercised his third-year option last year at around three million. The fourth-year option is a different level of commitment at five point four. And for a team projecting to operate close to the second apron in future seasons, every dollar and every roster spot matters.

Five point four million doesn't sound massive next to superstar contracts. But under restrictive payroll rules, spending it on a player outside your rotation becomes genuinely costly.

There's also trade flexibility. Dadiet's name is already one of the first mentioned whenever Knicks trade candidates come up. Exercising that option attaches another season of guaranteed money to him — and that makes moving him harder, because teams taking a flier on an uncertain young player want to limit their exposure.

And this is where the Knicks have to separate emotion from asset management.

Yes, he was a first-round pick. Yes, he's twenty-one. And yes, giving up early on a young player always carries the risk that he develops somewhere else and everybody looks back wondering.

But you cannot keep investing in a player simply because you already invested a first-round pick in him. That's sunk cost thinking, and it's how good front offices end up stuck with rosters full of decisions they made three years ago. The only question that matters is what gives this roster the most value going forward.

And New York has cleaner alternatives. We hold three second-round picks in 2027, and any one of those selections could fill that roster spot while counting less against the cap. A veteran minimum signing would cost less than half of what his option would.

Put all of that together and you understand why October thirty-first matters so much.
