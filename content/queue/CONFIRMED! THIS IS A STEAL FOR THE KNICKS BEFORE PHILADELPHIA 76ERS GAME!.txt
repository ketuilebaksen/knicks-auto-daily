Alright, let's get started.

First, the rules, because everything else depends on them and almost nobody explains them properly.

Rule one. The Knicks cannot simply absorb a salary. They're operating right up against the second apron, which means any real contract coming in has to be matched by real contract going out. You don't get to just say yes.

Rule two. Whatever the trade looks like, New York has to still be under that apron when it's finished. Not close to it. Under it. Ownership has made that line very clear all summer.

Rule three, and this one is about the calendar. Players signed as free agents this offseason generally can't be traded until December fifteenth. That covers Andre Drummond and every camp invite on this roster.

So put those three together and you get something important. A Stewart trade in October is nearly impossible. This is a December or February conversation. Anybody selling you an imminent deal is not looking at the rulebook.

Okay. Now let's build.

Package one. Call it the clean version.

Deuce McBride, plus second-round picks.

This is the trade most fans have in their heads, and I understand why. McBride is the most valuable movable piece on this roster after Jalen Brunson, and New York has a genuine stockpile of seconds.

The problem is arithmetic. Stewart is a rotation big man on real money. McBride is a very good player on a very friendly contract. And in trade math, "good player" and "big salary" are not the same thing.

So this package almost certainly doesn't clear the salary bar on its own. It's the version everybody wants and the version that doesn't work.

Grade: a fantasy. Not because Detroit would refuse — because the math would.

And this is the single most common mistake in fan trade proposals, so it's worth saying plainly. When you build a trade, you're not matching talent. You're matching salaries. Two teams can agree that a swap is fair in basketball terms and still be unable to do it, because the collective bargaining agreement doesn't care who the better player is.

That's why cheap contracts — the ones we celebrate all season — are actually the hardest pieces to trade. McBride's contract being a bargain is great for the Knicks' books and genuinely unhelpful in a deal for a bigger salary.

Package two. The realistic version.

McBride plus Pacôme Dadiet, a second-round pick, and a third team to help the money land.

Now we're talking about something that could actually be constructed. Dadiet's contract is exactly the kind of filler this front office has been reportedly trying to move anyway. A third team with space absorbs whatever doesn't fit and takes a sweetener for the trouble.

And look at what that does beyond the salary. It clears Dadiet, which we've said for weeks is the move that opens the second roster spot. So this version solves two problems at once.

Grade: this is the deal. If Detroit says yes to some version of this, I make the call today.

Package three. The overpay.

Everything above, plus the 2033 first-round pick.

And here I'm going to be consistent with what I've said all month, even though it's the less exciting answer. That pick is the last one in the drawer. Every other first this franchise owned went into building the team that just won a championship.

Spending it on a backup big — even one I like this much — is spending your last emergency fund on a want rather than a need.

Grade: no. Unless Detroit throws in something else meaningful, and at that point it's a different trade entirely.

Now let's flip the table, because I think Knicks fans skip this part constantly. What does Detroit actually want?

They're a team whose young center just turned down two hundred million dollars. That's not a franchise looking for win-now veterans. That's a franchise looking for clarity, young players, and draft capital.

And here's the awkward part. Deuce McBride is twenty-five, he's good, and he's in the final year of his contract. For a rebuilding team, an expiring contract isn't a prize — it's a rental.

So the honest version is that Detroit might prefer picks to McBride. Which pushes the price back toward that 2033 first, which is exactly where I said no.

That's the tension in this whole thing. The package the Knicks want to give isn't necessarily the package Detroit wants to receive.

But there's a counterargument, and it's real. Rebuilding teams still need functioning adults. You can't run a locker room full of twenty-two year olds with nobody showing them how professionals prepare. McBride is a championship-level defender who plays hard every night, and Detroit gets an entire season to decide whether they want to keep him.

That's not nothing. It's just not a first-round pick.

Alright, now the part I find most interesting, and I don't think anybody else is asking it. If Stewart arrives, whose minutes does he take?

Because this isn't a team with an open rotation spot waiting for him.

Karl-Anthony Towns starts at center. Andre Drummond is the primary backup, and we've talked about how the front office signalled that by only adding camp bodies behind him. So Stewart isn't walking in to start, and he isn't replacing Drummond on day one.

What he'd actually do is take the third-big minutes — the ones currently earmarked for whoever wins camp — and then absorb some backup power forward minutes next to Towns or Drummond.

And that has a knock-on effect that I think is genuinely important. If you trade for Stewart, the fourteenth roster spot competition suddenly doesn't matter very much. James Wiseman and Drew Eubanks were fighting for the emergency big job. Stewart is a better version of that job.

So this trade wouldn't just add a player. It would quietly resolve the longest-running storyline of our entire offseason.

But there's a fit question I want to raise honestly, because the enthusiasm around this player can drown it out.

Can he share the floor with a non-shooting center?

Because the reason Towns is so valuable is that he pulls the opposing five out of the paint. That's what creates driving room for Brunson. And Mike Brown's design is one real big surrounded by four players who stretch the floor.

If Stewart plays next to Drummond, you have two bigs in the paint at once, and the spacing gets tight in exactly the way this offense hates. So the honest answer is that Stewart's minutes probably need to come alongside Towns, not alongside Drummond — which means Mike Brown has to be careful about how he staggers them.

That's not a reason to avoid the trade. It's a reason to understand that he isn't a free plug-and-play addition. He's a specific tool for specific lineups.

There's also a question I keep coming back to that nobody wants to sit with. What if Detroit just says no?

Because here's the thing about a team in chaos. Chaos doesn't mean they're selling. Sometimes it means the opposite.

Think about it from their side. Their young franchise center just rejected two hundred million dollars. If that relationship deteriorates further, Detroit could wake up next summer without a starting center at all. And in that world, the one big man they absolutely cannot afford to trade is the tough, reliable, already-signed one.

So the same chaos that makes Stewart feel available might be the exact reason Detroit holds onto him.

That's the part I'd want Knicks fans to prepare for. This could easily be a story that generates six weeks of speculation and ends with nothing happening — and that wouldn't mean the front office failed. It would mean the other team had its own problems to solve first.

And one more thing to keep in mind — the Boston factor we covered yesterday. If Boston is genuinely in on Stewart, this stops being a negotiation with Detroit and becomes an auction. Auctions are how teams end up giving away their last first-round pick and calling it aggressive.

So here's where I land, cleanly.

Package two, or nothing. McBride plus Dadiet plus a second, with a third team making the money work. That's a trade that costs the Knicks a good guard they can survive without, clears the salary they've been trying to clear anyway, fills the one hole on the roster, and ends the camp competition permanently.

Anything more than that, and you're paying championship prices for a role player in a year when your own stars still need extensions.

And remember the timeline. None of this happens before December fifteenth. So if you see nothing between now and then, that isn't failure. That's the rulebook.
