Alright, now the money, because this is where everything gets decided and I want to walk through it slowly.

Two open spots sounds like room for two players. It isn't, and here's why. A veteran minimum contract counts against the apron at its full value based on the player's years of service — not at the reduced figure the league reimburses teams for. So a genuine veteran minimum takes a meaningful bite out of three point two million. Two of them cross the line.

That's the whole situation in one sentence. Two spots, enough money for one.

Now let's talk about the part I actually disagree with, because there's a real problem buried in this report.

Trading Deuce McBride for a third-string center might be a bad trade even if it works.

Here's the logic. A third-string center, by definition, plays limited minutes. Most nights he doesn't play at all. He exists as insurance for the night Towns is in foul trouble and Drummond can't go.

Deuce McBride is not insurance. He's a rotation player. Twelve points a game, forty-one percent from three, and genuine point-of-attack defense. You feel his impact the moment he walks onto the floor.

So in the version of this trade where New York sends out a real rotation guard and receives a player who plays eight minutes a night, you have made your bench worse in aggregate while solving a specific emergency. That's a defensible trade if you believe the emergency is likely. It's a bad one if you don't.

And here's the honest counterweight, which is the reason I'd still consider it.

Every opponent in this league has the same plan against us. Attack Towns, get him in foul trouble, then do the same to Drummond, and force the Knicks into small-ball lineups they'd rather not use. That is not a hypothetical emergency. That's the scouting report, and every team in this conference will run it.

So the question isn't "is a third center worth as much as McBride." The question is "how often does that emergency arrive, and what does it cost us when it does in April?"

My answer is that it arrives more often than people think, and that it costs more in the playoffs than any regular-season bench production is worth. Which means I'd make a version of this trade — but not the version where McBride goes out alone for a marginal big.

There's a second consequence here that the reporting makes unavoidable, and we should say it plainly.

If the Knicks are actively shopping McBride, his extension is effectively off the table.

You don't extend a player while trying to trade him. Those two things cannot be true at the same time. And that's a real shift from where we were two weeks ago, when the league raised its cap projection and keeping McBride suddenly looked affordable again.

So follow the sequence. The cap forecast went up, which made an extension realistic. Then the Towns negotiation stalled with the two sides reportedly tens of millions apart per year. And now McBride's name is in active trade conversations.

Those three things are connected. Every dollar the Knicks might have to spend on Towns is a dollar they can't promise McBride. The Towns standoff isn't just a Towns story — it's the reason the McBride story keeps getting worse.

Now let's talk about the basketball reason Mike Brown wants more bigs, because there's more to it than "we need a body."

The first reason is OG Anunoby.

When the Knicks run out of centers, somebody has to play the five, and in Mike Brown's system that has often been OG. Now, he can do it — he's strong, he's smart, and he competes. But think about what you're asking. You're taking your best perimeter defender, the man who guards the other team's best scorer every single night, and you're putting him in the paint to absorb contact from players seventy pounds heavier.

That's not just a tactical downgrade. It's an injury risk. And we've talked about OG's availability history on this channel — twenty-nine, thirty-four, fifteen, and thirty-two games missed in four straight seasons earlier in his career. This is a player this team cannot afford to lose.

Every additional center on the roster is one fewer night where OG Anunoby has to be one.

The second reason is Towns, and here I want to be careful, because there's a tension in the thinking.

A lot of people, including the source of this report, suggest that more centers means Towns could play more power forward. And Towns is noticeably leaner this year, which fuels that idea.

But remember what actually happened last season. Towns said the same thing — he'd slimmed down to play more four. And Mike Brown barely used two traditional bigs together. Towns anchored the middle all year.

So I'd be cautious about expecting that change. Brown's system is one real big surrounded by four players who stretch the floor. Two bigs at once shrinks the floor and this offense hates that. A third center probably doesn't move Towns to the four. It just gives Brown somebody to call on without changing the design.

And the third reason is the simplest, and it's the one Brown himself pointed at. More bigs means more rotations to test.

October is a laboratory. This is the only month of the year where a coach can play a combination for six minutes purely to see what happens, with nothing at stake. The more bodies you have, the more experiments you can run before the answers start to matter.

That's why Bradley exists. Not because the Knicks think Tony Bradley is the solution, but because Mike Brown wants more combinations to look at before he has to commit to one.

Let me lay out the three ways this actually ends, because I think it helps to see the whole board.

Scenario one. Nothing happens. Wiseman takes the fourteenth spot, the fifteenth stays empty, and the Knicks go into the season with Towns, Drummond, and a camp winner. This is the most likely outcome right now, and it's survivable — as long as nobody gets hurt.

Scenario two. The Dadiet salary dump. They move his contract for almost nothing, which opens the second roster spot and the room to fill it. Then they can keep both a big and a wing — Wiseman plus Bruce Brown or Ochai Agbaji, for example. This is the cleanest version and it's been reported as something they're exploring for weeks.

Scenario three. The real trade. McBride and Dadiet go out together, a center comes back, and the bench gets reshaped in the middle of a title defense. This is the version Siegel's reporting points at, and it's the one that makes me most nervous — not because the logic is wrong, but because losing McBride's shooting and defense is a real cost that doesn't show up in a depth chart.

And remember the calendar on all of it. Every player signed as a free agent this offseason, including Drummond and every camp invite, cannot be traded until December fifteenth. So the universe of legal trades right now is small, and it gets much larger in about ten weeks.

Which tells you something. If the Knicks are being honest about wanting this done before the season, they're limited to deals involving players who were already here — which is exactly why you keep hearing the same two names.

So here's what I'd do if it were up to me.

First, move Dadiet. That's the free move. His contract is the obstacle and nobody is pretending otherwise. Clearing it opens the second roster spot and gives you flexibility you don't currently have.

Second, hold McBride until December fifteenth at the earliest. Not because I'm sentimental, but because the market gets better. Right now you're negotiating with a tiny subset of the league. In December, hundreds more contracts become movable and salary matching gets dramatically easier. The same McBride buys you more in winter than he does in October.

Third, be honest about the camp competition. If Wiseman looks healthy and defends, give him the spot and stop looking. If he doesn't, Eubanks is the safer floor and Bradley is the fallback. But make the decision on what you see in October, not on draft pedigree from 2020.

And fourth — and this is the one I keep coming back to — settle the Towns negotiation. Everything else on this list is downstream of it. You cannot plan a bench while the biggest number on your cap sheet is unresolved.

Because here's the thing about this entire story. The center problem isn't really a center problem. It's a math problem wearing a center's jersey.
