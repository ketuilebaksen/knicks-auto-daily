First, Dink Pate, the player.

He's from Dallas, born in March of 2006, six-eight, a long, athletic wing who handles the ball like a guard. He was a highly ranked recruit in high school, and instead of going to college, he turned pro at seventeen with the G League Ignite in 2023, which reportedly made him the youngest known professional basketball player in U.S. history. Then the Ignite shut down. He spent the 2024-25 season with the Mexico City Capitanes and averaged about ten points a game. Even then, he was one of the G League players selected for the Rising Stars event at All-Star weekend in 2025, which tells you how scouts viewed his upside. He went undrafted in 2025, played summer league with the Knicks, and the Knicks kept him in their system in Westchester.

In thirty-six regular-season G League games, he averaged sixteen points, six point seven rebounds and three point six assists, shooting about forty-two percent from the field and almost thirty-seven percent from three. Now, why did a sixteen-point G League scorer turn down the NBA? This is the strange part of the story.

Last January, Pate turned down two-way and ten-day contract offers, reportedly including interest from the Knicks, because he wanted to keep his college eligibility. His agent told ESPN in the spring that he'd turned those deals down to stay eligible, and he committed to Providence. The bet was that because he'd only played in the G League and on an Exhibit 10 deal, and never signed an actual NBA contract, the NCAA would clear him.

It didn't. In late December, NCAA president Charlie Baker had posted that the NCAA would not grant eligibility to players who had signed NBA contracts, including two-way deals. Pate's case was that he never signed one. But according to the Providence Journal, he was never cleared. His case dragged through the summer and into the fall, and he had to sit out a closed scrimmage. Two of his Providence teammates, Joseph Pinion and Derek Simpson, have filed antitrust lawsuits against the NCAA over their own eligibility, with a hearing set for October 19th. So this is bigger than one player.

On October 16th, Pate withdrew from Providence. His coach, Bryan Hodgson, said, "Dink Pate will always be a Friar, and we fully support his decision." And Hodgson said legal action wasn't yet an option, because there were steps that still had to come from the NCAA first. Pate couldn't wait. The G League season was about to start.

So think about the irony. He turned down the NBA to go to college, and six months later the NCAA left him without a team. The Knicks were there the next day.

Now, how the Exhibit 10 works, because "signed and waived" confuses a lot of people.

An Exhibit 10 is a non-guaranteed, one-year minimum contract designed for players headed to the G League. Teams have to sign these before the regular season starts. If the team waives the player before the season, and he then joins the team's G League affiliate and stays there for at least sixty days, he earns a bonus of up to around ninety thousand dollars on top of his G League salary. And the NBA team keeps his G League rights as an affiliate player. The Knicks signed Pate on October 17th, the day after he left Providence, and waived him right away. That's the standard path. It pays him a bonus and puts him back in Westchester.

But here's the detail the "next star" videos skip. Affiliate rights only cover the G League. They don't stop another NBA team from signing Pate to an NBA contract, a standard deal or a two-way. If another team offers him a two-way spot, he can take it. So the Knicks haven't locked him up. And there's no matching right here, like with a restricted free agent. If another team offers him a two-way deal, the Knicks can't simply match it. Their only real protection is to make the offer first. They've given him a home, a bonus and a path. If he plays like he did last season, the Knicks will have to decide whether to give him an NBA contract before someone else does.

And if you think an undrafted G League player can't matter on a title team, look at the Knicks' own roster. Jose Alvarado went undrafted in 2021 and started his NBA career on a two-way contract in New Orleans. He became one of the most important bench players in the Knicks' title run, the kind of player whose energy changes games. Jaden Akins went undrafted too, and he's on a Knicks two-way deal right now. Under the second apron, this is how a contender keeps finding talent. You can't buy it. You have to find it, develop it, and give it a chance.

And think about how Pate's skill set would fit Mike Brown's system. Brown explained this week, when he was talking about Bruce Brown, that his point guard doesn't have to come down and break his man down every play, because the Knicks "play out of concepts," and other players can initiate the offense. A six-eight wing who can bring the ball up, make a read, rebound and push in transition is exactly the kind of player that system rewards. Pate's three-point jump makes it even more interesting. If defenses have to respect his shot, his size and handle become real weapons.

What would he need to show to make that leap? Mostly the things that don't show up in a box score. Defensive discipline against NBA-level wings, decision-making with the ball under pressure, and consistency. A lot of young G League scorers put up big numbers because they're the focal point of the offense. The question is whether they can fit into a smaller role on an NBA team without the ball. For a twenty-year-old, that's the next step, and it's why another full season in Westchester, playing big minutes, is the right place for him right now.

The plan going around is this: trade Dadiet, sign Pate to an NBA deal, and use the savings to bring back Bruce Brown. Let's check it, with exact numbers, because at the second apron, a few thousand dollars can decide everything.

Before signing James Wiseman, the Knicks were three million, two hundred seventy-three thousand, seven hundred sixty-eight dollars under the second apron. Quick note on why Pate's number is different from Wiseman's or Brown's. The league's reimbursement trick, where a veteran's one-year minimum only counts at the two-year amount, applies to players with three or more years of experience. Pate has none. So his minimum counts in full, but his minimum is also much smaller, because rookies have the lowest scale in the league.

Sign Pate to a standard NBA contract. As a player with no NBA experience, his minimum this season is about one million, three hundred fifty-seven thousand dollars, and for a rookie, that counts in full. The cushion drops to about two million, four hundred fifty thousand, two hundred sixty-four.

And then Bruce Brown on a one-year veteran minimum counts two million, four hundred forty-nine thousand, four hundred twenty-one dollars. Subtract that, and you're left with about eight hundred forty dollars under the second apron.

Eight hundred forty dollars. On paper, it fits. By less than the price of a pair of courtside tickets. And it would put the Knicks at fifteen players: Dadiet out, Pate and Bruce Brown in.

There are a few big catches, though. First, someone has to take Dadiet, and last week nobody would, even with picks. Second, Bruce Brown has to still be available and willing to come back. There's also a second version. Put Pate on a two-way contract and convert one of the current two-way players, Tyler Nickel, Kevin McCullar or Jaden Akins, to a standard deal. Two-way players don't count against the cap or the apron, but they can only be active for fifty regular-season games and can't play in the playoffs. So if the Knicks want a player like Nickel available in the playoffs, they'd have to convert him eventually. Swapping Pate into that two-way slot would keep a twenty-year-old prospect in the system at no apron cost. But converting someone to a standard deal still costs apron room, so it's not free.

And the bigger money question is actually next season, not this one. If the Knicks pick up Dadiet's fourth-year option, he'll make about five point three million dollars in 2027-28.
