Every team in this league has been filling out its roster for three months. Two-way contracts, camp deals, G League affiliates — thirty front offices quietly closing doors.

Except one.

The New York Knicks are the only team in the NBA with all three two-way spots still open. And I've heard a lot of people call that indecision.

Then on Thursday night, a former SEC Player of the Year got waived, and suddenly that patience looks like a plan.

Shout out to Jake Fischer for the news. The Los Angeles Clippers have waived Johni Broome, the six-ten forward-center.

And look at the path that got him here, because it explains everything about why he's available and nothing about whether he's good.

Philadelphia took Broome with the thirty-fifth pick last summer. In late July, the Sixers sent him to the Clippers in a salary dump — a move designed purely to create room to sign Kentavious Caldwell-Pope. Then the Clippers ran into their own roster crunch, and Broome was the odd man out again.

Twice in three months, he's been the guy who didn't fit the math. Not the guy who wasn't good enough. The guy who didn't fit the math. There's a difference, and it's the entire reason a champion gets a look at him at all.

Now, who is he actually?

Because there are three completely different versions of Johni Broome, and you need all three to judge this properly.

Version one is the college player, and it's genuinely absurd. At Auburn, Broome became just the fourth player in NCAA Division One history to reach twenty-five hundred career points and fifteen hundred career rebounds. He was SEC Player of the Year. First-team All-American. MVP of the SEC tournament. A Naismith Trophy finalist. And he led Auburn to a Final Four.

That's not a fringe résumé. That's one of the most accomplished college big men of the decade.

Version two is the NBA rookie, and it's the opposite. Eleven games. Five minutes a night. Sixteen point seven percent from the field. Under a point and under two rebounds per game. And his season ended in February with a partially torn meniscus.

But look at why. He was buried behind Joel Embiid, Andre Drummond, and Adem Bona. Those eleven appearances were mostly blowouts. There is nothing to evaluate there, in either direction.

And version three is the G League, which is where he actually played basketball. Twenty-six games with the Delaware Blue Coats — twenty-one point eight points, eight point three rebounds, and one and a half blocks a game. Including one night where he scored fifty points.

Fifty. In a professional game.

So which version is real? Probably none of them, and that's exactly the point. Nobody knows yet — and "nobody knows yet" is precisely what a two-way contract exists for.

Now let me be honest about the concerns, because they're real and they were in his pre-draft reports.

Scouts called him slow, undersized for a center, and short on explosive athleticism. His vertical leap measured twenty-four and a half inches, which means finishing above the rim against NBA length simply isn't part of his game.

And the bigger issue is stylistic. In college he overpowered smaller, weaker opponents with his back to the basket. That skill has almost no market in the modern NBA. Traditional post-ups have basically disappeared.

Philadelphia's staff told him directly that becoming a shooter was essential to his future. And so far? Twenty-seven percent from three in his final Auburn season. Twenty-eight point seven percent in the G League, on five attempts a game. Forty-six point four percent from the field overall, which is low for a big man.

In Summer League he averaged fourteen point seven points and nine rebounds across three games — while going two for fifteen from three.

So the shot isn't there. Not yet.

But here's the part I'd actually pay attention to. The volume. He went from barely shooting threes in college to five attempts a game in the G League and fifteen attempts in three Summer League games.

That's a player who has been told what he needs to fix and is visibly, uncomfortably, publicly trying to fix it — in games, where it costs him. Plenty of players nod at that conversation and then keep doing what's comfortable. He isn't.

I'd rather bet on a twenty-four-year-old shooting badly on purpose than one shooting well by avoiding the attempt.

And that's why the two-way contract is the perfect vehicle here. He'd spend most of the season developing with Westchester, stay available if the frontcourt gets hit by injuries, and cost this team almost nothing against the cap. If the rebounding and the toughness translate even partially, you've turned an empty slot into a rotation body. If not, you've lost nothing.

Now let's zoom out on the patience, because I think this is the underrated story of the whole offseason.

Every other team filled those slots in July, when the options were whoever happened to be available in July. New York didn't. And because they waited, they got to react when a former SEC Player of the Year hit the market the same week camp opened.

That's flexibility used as an asset. And we've watched it work already — the same open-slot pitch is part of why Igor Milicic chose New York.

Alright, now let's talk about the man whose job all of this is protecting, because there's a signal here that almost nobody has decoded properly.

Andre Drummond's role is safe. And you can prove it by what the Knicks did after signing him.

Think about it. Since Drummond arrived, the front office has added Drew Eubanks and James Wiseman — both on camp tryout deals. Not trades. Not real money. Not a single asset spent.

If this front office genuinely doubted Drummond as the primary backup behind Karl-Anthony Towns, they'd be chasing a bigger name and paying a real price for it. Instead, everything around him looks like depth insurance.

So read the message clearly. The backup center job belongs to Andre Drummond. The camp competition is for the spot behind him.

And there are good reasons for that trust.

Start with something past Knicks backup centers simply never offered — a willingness to shoot from outside. Consider this. Nerlens Noel, Isaiah Hartenstein, and Mitchell Robinson combined for twenty-five NBA seasons and attempted twelve three-pointers between them. Total.

Drummond took nine last season alone.

Now, let me be the one to add the caveat that nobody else will. Nine attempts is not a skill. Nine attempts is noise. You cannot conclude anything about a shooter from nine shots, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

But the willingness still matters, even at low volume — because a defender who has to consider stepping out, even occasionally, is a defender who isn't parked in the paint waiting for Jalen Brunson. That's the whole game with spacing. It isn't about makes. It's about what the defense has to respect.

Then the actual reason he's here. Rebounding.

Last season in Philadelphia, his defensive rebounding percentage was in the same range as Karl-Anthony Towns. And on the offensive glass, his rebounding percentage was two-tenths of a point behind the league leader, Donovan Clingan.

Two-tenths of a point off the best in basketball. For three point nine million dollars.

Now, there's a long-running criticism of Drummond worth acknowledging — that some of those numbers are inflated, because at times he's been so focused on securing the board that he'll pass up contesting the shot to get into rebounding position. That criticism has followed him for years and it isn't baseless.

But for a team that led the league in offensive rebounding and built its identity on extra possessions and physicality, those numbers explain exactly why the front office is comfortable.

And here's the detail I find most fun, because it's a perfect mirror of something we talked about last week.

Drummond arrives directly from an Eastern Conference rival. He spent a full year inside Philadelphia's locker room — alongside Tyrese Maxey, Joel Embiid, VJ Edgecombe, and Nick Nurse's staff.

Remember what we said about Ariel Hukporti? He left for Philadelphia knowing our coverages, our rotations, our tendencies, and that's a small real disadvantage for us.

Well, this is the same trade in reverse. Philadelphia got a big man who knows how we play. We got a big man who knows how they play. And we open the season against them.

Nobody wins a game because of inside information. But on opening night, in a building that's about to raise a banner against the team that took our backup center, I'll take every small edge available.

One more thing before we wrap, and I'm flagging it as a watch item rather than news.

Moussa Cisse's situation may be changing. He's the six-eleven big New York signed to a two-year offer sheet this summer before Dallas matched it inside forty-eight hours. If he genuinely becomes available again, remember what that offer sheet told us — this front office already decided what he was worth, and they were willing to pay it.

That's the thing about this whole summer. New York collected a long list of rejections at center. Mitchell Robinson left because ownership wouldn't cross the second apron. Yves Missi has been refused repeatedly. Cisse got matched. Julian Reese chose New Orleans.

But every one of those doors closing is also the reason three two-way slots are still open — the last unspent currency this front office has.

And this week, they finally have something to spend it on.
