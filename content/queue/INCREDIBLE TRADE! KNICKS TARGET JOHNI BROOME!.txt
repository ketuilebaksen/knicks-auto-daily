Shout out to Joseph Randazzo of Knicks on SI for this. The Clippers recently acquired Johni Broome from Philadelphia. And the way it happened tells you everything. The Sixers essentially dumped Broome on LA, with a draft pick attached as payment.

Broome was a second-round pick out of Auburn. And think about the timing. Philadelphia signed Ariel Hukporti away from us this summer — the move that started this entire center crisis. Once they had Hukporti, Broome became expendable. So the team that took our third center just shipped out its own.

Now here's the catch for the Clippers. Los Angeles has seventeen players under contract, and NBA rules only allow fifteen on opening night. Two people have to go.

And on top of that, the Clippers are in turmoil. According to the reporting, they've been stripped of future draft capital after the league handed down its verdict in the Kawhi Leonard investigation. They need draft picks badly.

So Randazzo lays out two paths. The Knicks could offer one of their second-round picks — and this front office has a real stockpile of them — to acquire Broome directly. Or they could sit back and wait for LA to cut him.

And I want to be very clear about which path is better, because this is where most coverage gets it wrong.

We covered this rule earlier this week. Waiver claims go in reverse order of last season's standings. The worst team gets first crack. The defending champions are at the very back of the line.

So if the Clippers waive Broome and any other team wants him, New York doesn't get him. Period.

Which means the second-round pick isn't overpaying. It's buying a way to skip the line. And for a Clippers team that suddenly has no draft capital, a second-rounder is worth more to them than a fifteenth roster spot they can't keep anyway. That's a trade that works for both sides.

And there's a mechanical detail that makes it cleaner. A player on a minimum-level contract can be taken into a trade using the minimum salary exception — so New York doesn't need to send salary back to match.

Now, let's be realistic about what Broome would be. He's not starting over Karl-Anthony Towns. He's not taking Andre Drummond's minutes. He's an insurance policy — someone who can protect the paint and clean up misses on the glass when you need him.

In plain English, third-string center depth.

But here's the part nobody is connecting. Broome would take exactly the same roster spot, and use exactly the same second-apron room, that Drew Eubanks and James Wiseman are already fighting for in camp. He isn't an extra. He's a third candidate for the same job. So if the Knicks go after him, they're telling you they don't love what they've seen from the other two.

There's also one snag. Clippers center Yanic Konan Niederhauser went under the knife again this summer and could miss extended time. That might force Los Angeles to keep Broome for their own center depth.

But if they cut him — or if they'll take a late pick — Leon Rose gets a young center without giving up a single rotation piece.

My verdict? Make the call. Don't wait for the waiver wire, because the waiver wire isn't built for champions. We cannot count on Towns and Drummond staying healthy for eighty-two games and a playoff run.

Story three. A breakout candidate.

Over at Heavy, Michael Kaskey-Blomain named six-nine forward Mohamed Diawara as a prime breakout candidate for 2026-27.

Diawara was a second-round pick who quietly played sixty-nine regular season games for us last year. Three point six points, one point four rebounds, in just nine point two minutes a night.

But here's the number people are passing around. He made fifty-seven point six percent of his corner threes.

Picture it. Brunson drives, the defense collapses, and Diawara is sitting in the deep corner. That's an elite kick-out option.

And Kaskey-Blomain points out that with Diawara sharing second-unit minutes with Drummond instead of Mitchell Robinson, his length and spacing become even more important. A non-shooting center needs shooters around him.

And the expectations go further than that. On WFAN radio, the take was that Diawara isn't just end-of-bench depth. He's expected to be part of Mike Brown's playoff rotation, because his length, athleticism, and quickness matter in high-stakes matchups.
