Official. What does it mean?

Tony Bradley signed an Exhibit 9. And I've said this on the channel before, because almost nobody in the coverage explains it, and it changes how you read everything.

An Exhibit 9 is a camp contract with injury protection. It lets a team bring in a veteran, put him in real practices, and waive him before the regular season without any salary-cap consequences. If the player gets hurt in camp, the team owes only a small one-time payment, which was reported in this case at around fifteen thousand dollars. It does not convert to a two-way contract, and it isn't a roster spot.

That's different from an Exhibit 10, which is a pathway deal. It can turn into a two-way, and if the player is waived and joins the G League team, he gets a bonus. Exhibit 10s are for young players the organisation wants to develop. Exhibit 9s are for veterans the organisation wants to look at.

Why does that distinction matter for a team in this situation? Because the Knicks are pressed up against the second apron, which is the ceiling this ownership has said it won't cross. There is no room for a gamble that costs money. An Exhibit 9 is the only kind of gamble that doesn't.

Now, how did Bradley get his place? He took the tryout spot opened by John Konchar. Konchar had been signed recently, and was waived on Wednesday to deal with personal matters. We wish him well, and it's worth saying that nothing about that move reflects on his basketball.

So that leaves five players on non-guaranteed deals competing for the roster. Bradley, Drew Eubanks and James Wiseman at centre. Ochai Agbaji and Bruce Brown on the wing.

So one of those five camp players gets the spot. Maybe. And since the group is three bigs and two wings, the most likely outcome is that New York keeps a centre. That's the logic, and I think it's right.

Let's talk about the three centres as people, because this is a real competition, and I don't think the result is obvious.

James Wiseman is the one with the most talent and the most history. The second overall pick in twenty twenty, six-foot-eleven or seven foot depending on who is measuring, a player Mike Brown coached in Golden State when Wiseman was a rookie. Drew Eubanks is the physical option. He rolls hard, finishes at the rim, blocks shots, and has the veteran stability a coach can trust at the end of a close game.

And Bradley is the specialist. He doesn't score. He doesn't pretend to. What he does is the thing every coach says they want and few fans notice, which is the boxing out, the angles, the physicality. If you watch the way he guards a rebounder, he plays the old way. Butt in the gut. Make the other guy foul you to get through.

So the competition isn't just about who is best. It's about which flavour of third centre fits the roster. And that comes down to two questions.

Question one is what the other bigs can't do. Drummond is a rebounding machine. Towns is a perimeter shooter. Neither is a rim protector in the way Robinson was. Of the three camp centres, which one best covers that hole? On blocks and length, Eubanks. On pure rebounding and boxing out, Bradley. On upside and potential offence, Wiseman.

Question two is how much offence the team needs from a player who plays ten minutes. The Knicks want a rim-protecting backup who can set screens, rebound, and give you ten minutes a night. That job description is closer to Bradley's profile than to Wiseman's.

I'll give you the honest counterargument too. Bradley's offensive numbers are thin. If a defence can leave him alone on the floor, you've got a four-on-five problem every time he's in the game. Which brings me to Tyler Kolek, because his name is quietly central to that.

Kolek is entering his third NBA season. He's twenty-five, a former Marquette star, and he's in one of the trickiest spots on the roster. The rotation is clear. Brunson is the captain and the Finals MVP. Jose Alvarado is the backup point guard, and he's openly said he wants to compete for Sixth Man of the Year. Behind them there are McBride, Clarkson and Shamet. Alvarado's arrival last February pushed Kolek out of the rotation.

He told the New York Post that he was playing well early last season and that he got too passive as the year went on. His focus now is on looking for his own shot and attacking the paint. I like that. It isn't a complaint. It's a diagnosis. A guard who stops looking for his shot makes every pass harder, because defences sag off him and nobody can run a play against five defenders in the same area.

But think about what it tells you. The Knicks have one position with a surplus of young, cost-controlled players, and one position where they're short. It's the oldest solution in basketball. So Kolek's best move is the one he's making. Be ready. Either he's the insurance behind Brunson and Alvarado, or he's the key piece in a deal that brings back size. Both are good outcomes for a guard who wants a long career.

The Knicks wouldn't send him to an Eastern rival like Boston or Cleveland. Now put all of this together, because I think the connection is the real story.

The Knicks want a better backup centre. They don't have the money to buy one, the trade market has been reluctant to help them, and the one star centre they have is in a contract negotiation that could take months. That is why a six-foot-eleven journeyman on an Exhibit 9 matters more than it looks. Not because Tony Bradley is going to change the season. That is also why the answer to this whole story isn't a trade or a signing. It's a decision. Who gets the one open spot, and what does the front office do about the other one?
