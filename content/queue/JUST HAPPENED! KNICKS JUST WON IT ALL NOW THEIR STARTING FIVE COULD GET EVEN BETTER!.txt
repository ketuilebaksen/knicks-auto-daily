Start with Mikal Bridges, because he came into last season with more doubt on him than anyone, and he answered all of it.

In the playoffs he shot fifty-six percent from the field, thirty-six percent from three, and ninety-two percent from the line, with three assists for every turnover, while playing elite defense. That run ended the argument about what he cost to acquire.

And he hasn't missed a game since high school. So what's left for a player like that?

Honestly? Rest. And I know how strange that sounds.

He's spoken publicly about starters playing too many minutes, so he may actually be open to sitting some regular season nights. And here's the number that makes the case. Despite playing nearly four hundred fewer regular season minutes than the year before, Bridges committed fifty-three percent more shooting fouls.

That's a fatigue number. When a defender is a step slow, he fouls instead of contesting. And Bridges spent about a quarter of the regular season and a third of his playoff minutes at power forward — heavier physical work for his frame.

So if Mike Brown leans on small lineups again because of the size problem behind Towns, managing Bridges' workload stops being a nice idea and becomes a requirement.

Now Josh Hart, who is the proof that rest works.

His minutes dropped thirty-one percent last season, and with fresher legs he shot a career-best forty-one percent from three. That's the cleanest before-and-after you'll find.

And now the Knicks need him somewhere that suddenly looks shaky. Last year New York led the entire league in offensive rebounding and ranked third in total rebounds. But Mitchell Robinson was the team's top offensive rebounder and its second-leading defensive rebounder, and he's in Boston now.

His rim protection can't be replaced. His rebounding might be — but only if everybody chips in.

The complication is that Hart's own rebounding rates dipped last season to their lowest level since he arrived. Hart has always argued that not all rebounds are equal, and he's got a point. But somebody has to replace that production, and a six-four wing who rebounds like a big is the most likely candidate.

Now OG Anunoby, who had a postseason almost nobody predicted.

Fifty-six percent from the field, forty-nine percent from three, eighty-five percent from the line — while guarding the other team's best scorer. His playoff efficiency rating would have placed him among the top ten players in the regular season.

So for OG, this isn't about adding a skill. It's about availability.

Earlier in his career he missed twenty-nine, thirty-four, fifteen, and thirty-two games in four straight seasons. This team cannot absorb a long absence from him — he's as important to what they do as Brunson or Towns.

And there's reason for optimism, because Mike Brown already cut his minutes to their lowest level in years. This group also finished its title run with eight straight road playoff wins, closing out series in Boston, Detroit, Philadelphia, Cleveland, and San Antonio.

Think about what that means. This team doesn't need home court advantage. It's proven it can win anywhere. So there's no reason to grind anybody down in February chasing a seeding line.

Now Jalen Brunson, who has already given this city almost everything.

Captain. Face of the franchise. Only the second Knick ever named Finals MVP, joining Willis Reed, who did it twice. And he left money on the table so the front office could build a champion around him.

So what's left? Two small things, and one of them is subtraction.

First, he took noticeably more shots going left last season, adding another option to an already deep bag. Every new wrinkle makes him harder to plan for.

Second — and this is the one I care about — he could do less of something. Brunson led the entire NBA in charges drawn last season.

Think about what a charge actually is. A thirty-year-old guard standing still and letting a larger athlete run through his chest, on purpose. It's brilliant, winning basketball. It's also a car crash you schedule yourself, dozens of times a year.

He just turned thirty. He played through tendon pain in his left hand and wrist during the playoffs and needed surgery this summer. Taking fewer hits from October through April is how you make sure there's more of him in May and June.

And then Karl-Anthony Towns, who might have the most on his plate of anyone.

Because he and Robinson were a perfect pair — each one covering the other's weakness. The lineup of Brunson, Bridges, Anunoby, Robinson, and Towns outscored opponents by nine point four points per hundred possessions.

That partner is gone. Andre Drummond has the job now, and Towns' responsibilities have grown.

Nobody expects him to become a rim protector. But here's an encouraging detail — in the playoffs his block rate jumped a hundred and fifty percent compared to the regular season, and it came from patience and awareness rather than wild gambling. He did it while playing fewer minutes.

So the ability is there when he locks in. He got married this summer, he's extension-eligible, and if he brings that same calm focus into the season, very few teams have an answer for him.

Alright, section two. And this is the part where I want to make an argument you haven't heard on this channel yet.

If the Knicks are trading Deuce McBride, they should do it now. Not in February.

Leon Rose normally makes his moves at two points in the year — draft night, and around the deadline. The one real exception was the Towns trade, which got finalized just weeks before a season opener. So he's shown he'll break his own pattern when the timing demands it.

And here's why the timing demands it now. Three reasons.

Reason one is injuries, and nobody wants to say it out loud. Right now McBride is the most valuable trade chip this team has. That can change in a single practice. He pulls a hamstring in camp and overnight he goes from trade candidate to untradeable.

And there's a human side too. He's entering his fifth season and he's been one of the most underpaid players in this league his entire career. He understands the business. But rehabbing an injury for a team you know is trying to move you is a miserable place to put anybody.

The risk cuts both ways. Imagine instead that Towns or Drummond gets hurt. Now New York is dangerously thin at center, possibly with Bridges taking minutes at the five. The need becomes desperate, every front office knows it, and prices go up. You'd be negotiating from weakness.

Reason two is the calendar. McBride is an unrestricted free agent next summer. It's already hard to find a team that will give up a good big man for a guard. It's much harder when that guard could walk in nine months. A team acquiring him today gets a full eighty-two games to evaluate him.

Reason three is chemistry. Whoever takes those minutes needs time. If it's Tyler Kolek, you want him getting reps now. If it's Bruce Brown, you want him learning the defensive rotations in October, not on a plane in February.

Now, in a perfect world McBride signs an extension and stays with the only team he's ever known. But ownership wouldn't cross the second apron to keep Mitchell Robinson, and there's little reason to think that changes for McBride.

And there's a basketball case for moving on, even though it's uncomfortable.

McBride's job is to defend and shoot threes, and he does both well — forty-one percent from deep last season. But inside the arc he has struggled his whole career. He made forty-four percent of his two-pointers in the regular season, and that fell to twenty percent in the playoffs.

Twenty percent. That's exactly the kind of weakness a playoff defense finds and attacks. Every other guard on this roster was more efficient on two-pointers, including Tyler Kolek — and he doesn't have Kolek's or Alvarado's ball-handling either.

In eighty-two games you can hide that. In a seven-game series you can't.

And that's really the point. McBride is exactly the player you need to survive a regular season. For years that's what this team needed. But New York is defending a championship now. The playoffs are a sprint, not a marathon.

Last thing, quickly, and it stings a little.

Julian Reese has agreed to a two-way deal with the New Orleans Pelicans, per his agents, as reported by Michael Scotto of HoopsHype.

And Reese looked like an obvious Knicks fit. Young big man, available, wouldn't have cost a standard roster spot, and New York has all three two-way slots open.

His finish to last season was genuinely good — thirteen games with Washington averaging eleven point eight points, ten and a half rebounds, and one point four steals on fifty-three percent shooting, including one night with eighteen points and twenty rebounds.

But let me give you the honest reasons the Knicks may have passed.

Those numbers came on a tanking Wizards team that won one of the thirteen games he played. Producing on a bad team is better than not producing, but it isn't proof you can handle pressure — and as defending champions, pressure is the only environment we have.

Size may have mattered too. Reese is listed at six-nine, and this front office has clearly preferred taller bigs.

And there's the minutes problem, which is probably the real answer. Reese wants to prove he belongs, and behind Towns and Drummond he'd barely play. New Orleans is in transition and can offer him an actual rotation path. That alone might explain his choice, regardless of what New York offered.

One more wrinkle, and it's the part that makes this sting more. There was an argument that the Knicks already had their two-way center in the building — Liam Robbins, a legitimate seven-footer who put up fifteen points, five rebounds, three assists, two steals, and four blocks in a Summer League win. But Robbins was waived on September sixteenth. So if he was the in-house answer, that answer is no longer on the roster, and all three two-way spots are still sitting empty.

So it may be less a missed opportunity than another small sacrifice to keep flexibility. But with opening night getting closer and that center spot still unsettled, the small sacrifices are starting to add up.
