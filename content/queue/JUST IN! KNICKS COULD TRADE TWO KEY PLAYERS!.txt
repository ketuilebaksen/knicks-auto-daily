"The Knicks could trade two key players." You'll see that headline a lot today, the day before banner night. So let me be precise about what's behind it. One of the two stories is about Karl-Anthony Towns, and it's built on trade ideas from a Bleacher Report writer, not on any reported talks. The other is about Tyler Kolek, and it already resolved over the weekend. Kolek wasn't traded. Bruce Brown was waived instead.

So no, the Knicks aren't about to trade two key players. But the Towns question underneath is real, and it's going to follow this team all season. So today, let's do it properly: what's actually been reported about Towns, the three trade ideas being floated and whether any of them make sense, with the math, a word on Josh Hart, and then the Kolek decision and what it says about the age of this roster.

Welcome back to Ketuil KNICKS. Banner night is tomorrow, so there's no better time to clear this up. Let's go.

First, the real reporting on Towns, because it matters to separate it from the ideas.

The Knicks offered Towns about two hundred million dollars over four years. He's eligible for up to about two hundred and seventy-six million. That's a gap of about seventy-six million. On October 9th, the Knicks announced that they're pausing all extension negotiations, Towns, Josh Hart, Deuce McBride and Tyler Kolek, until later in the season. Before that, on October 5th, Shams Charania described the situation as "a growing cloud around the New York Knicks," and said it could heat up around the February trade deadline if nothing changes for Towns and Hart.

And Towns has been unusually open about it. Before the preseason opener in Philadelphia, he told reporters, including Stefan Bondy of the Post: "Simply put, I want to do something that can keep the team together and work with the Knicks and also values me. And none of those things have come close to happening." Asked about a deal before opening night, he said, "It doesn't look good." He also said, "I just won a championship. You'd think it was pretty easy." After the pause was announced, he said he was "very disappointed, to say the least," and that it was "news to me." But he also said his only goal since winning the title is figuring out how to win another one in New York.

Notice what he's saying. He's not asking for a trade. He's saying he wants a deal that keeps the team together and also values him. That's someone who wants to stay, at the right price.

And here's the deadline that matters. Once the regular season tips off tomorrow night, a veteran can only sign an extension if his contract is in its final year, and option years count. Towns has this season and a player option for 2027-28, so his contract isn't in its final year. When the ball goes up against Philadelphia, there's no extension possible this season. The next chance is next summer.

So that's the real situation. Now let's look at the trade ideas, because that's what most of the headlines are about.

All three come from one article by Andrew Peters of Bleacher Report, who identified possible landing spots if the Knicks ever decided to trade Towns. Let me say this clearly before we go through them: these are a writer's ideas. There's no report that the Knicks have talked to any of these teams about Towns. Even the articles relaying them describe a trade as far off.

Idea number one: Golden State.

Peters noted that the Warriors missed out on Giannis Antetokounmpo and Jaylen Brown this summer, and that they could make a splash for Towns to give Steph Curry another real shot at a title in his final years. The package he suggested would likely include Kristaps Porziņģis and draft picks, and he wasn't sure whether Jimmy Butler or Draymond Green would have to be included. He also questioned whether the Warriors even have the draft capital to land a player like Towns.

Let's look at the pieces. Porziņģis signed a two-year, forty-million-dollar deal with Golden State this summer, with a player option in the second year. But he wasn't at media day. He hasn't been at training camp. Steve Kerr said he hadn't seen him, and that his absence is "definitely concerning." Reports say he's dealing with the same mystery illness that's affected recent seasons and is out indefinitely. So the main piece in that package is a player whose availability is unknown.

And then the money. Towns makes about fifty-seven point one million this season. Porziņģis makes around twenty. The Knicks are above the first apron, so they can't take back more salary than they send out, but they can take back less. The problem is on Golden State's side. To absorb Towns's salary, the Warriors would have to send back much more than Porziņģis. And that's where Jimmy Butler comes in. Butler makes about fifty-six point eight million this season, almost exactly Towns's salary. But he's thirty-seven, he tore his ACL on January 19th, he won't be back for the start of the season, and his contract expires next summer.

Butler himself said something revealing this month. Talking about his injury, he said, "I really felt like I let Steph down." That tells you where Golden State's head is. They're thinking about how to give Curry, who's thirty-eight, one more real run. That's a legitimate goal for them. It's just not the Knicks' problem to solve.

So the only version of this trade that actually works on salary is something like Towns for Butler plus picks. Think about what that is for the Knicks. You'd be giving up your second-best player on a title team for an injured thirty-seven-year-old on an expiring deal. That isn't a basketball trade. It's a decision to end the Towns era and reset the books. A defending champion doesn't do that in October. And Towns doesn't hurt the Knicks' cap next summer anyway, because if he opts out, they don't owe him anything. So there's no logic to it for New York.

Idea number two: Washington and Anthony Davis.

We did a whole video on this two weeks ago, when fake videos claimed it had already happened. Here's the short version. Davis plays for Washington. He played against the Knicks at the Garden on October 8th. In June, the Wizards said they weren't listening to offers for him. His extension talks are on hold until about twenty games into the season. And the salary math doesn't work straight up, because Davis makes about fifty-eight point four million and Towns about fifty-seven point one. A first-apron team can't take back more than it sends. So the Knicks would have to add salary, and the cheap contracts they could add mostly belong to players signed this summer, who can't be traded until December 15th.

And then the basketball. Davis is thirty-three. He played twenty games last season. He's around a thirty percent three-point shooter for his career. The Knicks' offense is built on Towns pulling the other team's center out to the three-point line so Brunson can attack the paint. Swapping Towns for Davis would improve the rim protection and shrink the floor for Brunson. I said it then and I'll say it now: no.

Idea number three: Cleveland, and this is the most interesting one.

Peters suggested Cleveland would have to give up either Evan Mobley or Jarrett Allen, and he preferred the version with Allen and role players or picks, because Mobley is twenty-five and entering his prime. Cleveland's core now is Donovan Mitchell, James Harden and Mobley, with Peyton Watson added this summer.

Let me give you the case for this one, because it's not crazy. Mobley is a former Defensive Player of the Year, a twenty-five-year-old big man who protects the rim, switches onto guards and is still improving. For a Knicks team that lost Mitchell Robinson's rim protection this summer, a Mobley-centered defense would be a dream. And Allen is one of the best screen-setters and rebounders in the league on a contract much smaller than Towns's.

But there are real problems. First, Cleveland is the team the Knicks beat in the Eastern Conference finals last spring. Why would the Cavaliers trade their best young player to the team that just eliminated them, to help them repeat? Second, Peters himself says Cleveland would prefer to keep Mobley. Third, a Towns-for-Allen deal is a big step down for the Knicks in talent and in offensive spacing. And fourth, the salary matching would be complicated on both sides, with two teams that each have expensive cores.

So what would a Towns trade the Knicks would actually consider look like? Let me think about it like a front office, without naming any specific deal, because none has been reported. First, it would have to bring back a player who helps the Knicks win this season, not just picks. A defending champion can't take a step back in October. Second, it would have to work under the first-apron rule, meaning the Knicks take back no more salary than they send out. Third, ideally, it would give them a younger core piece on a longer, cheaper contract, so they can stay under the second apron for years. And fourth, it can't make the offense worse for Brunson. Put those four together and you get a very short list. A young, two-way big who can shoot, on a long contract, from a team willing to give him up. There are maybe two or three players like that in the league, and their teams aren't selling. That's the real reason a Towns trade is unlikely. Not loyalty. Math and supply.

So which of the three makes sense? Honestly, none of them, at least not in October. The Golden State version is a salary dump. The Washington version is a worse fit, and Washington isn't selling. The Cleveland version only works if the Cavaliers give up the player they'd least like to give up, to their biggest rival in the East. If the Knicks ever trade Towns, it'll be because something changes. An injury, a relationship that breaks down, or a team that suddenly overpays at the deadline. Not because of a list of ideas in October. And remember, every one of these ideas came from the same article. Three headlines, one source, zero reported conversations.

Let me steelman the other side, though, because there is an argument for at least thinking about it. Towns turns thirty-one next month. His player option for 2027-28 means he can walk next summer. The Knicks are pinned against the second apron, with Brunson, Bridges and Anunoby on big deals. If they're not going to pay him close to the max, waiting until he leaves for nothing would be the worst outcome. So you could argue the front office has to at least know what the market is.

And I'd answer it this way. First, the market for Towns in free agency, according to an anonymous executive who spoke to the Post last week, might be around forty-five to fifty million a year from the teams with cap room. That's below what the Knicks can pay, and the Knicks have his Bird rights. So the risk of losing him for nothing is lower than it sounds. Second, ESPN's Brian Windhorst said he expects the Knicks and Towns to eventually reach an agreement. Third, Towns was outstanding in the title run, about sixteen points, over ten rebounds and almost five assists a game, while shooting over forty-five percent from three. You don't trade that player in October because of a contract disagreement.

Now, Josh Hart, because he's the other player in Shams's "growing cloud."

Hart has a twenty-two million dollar team option for next season, and his extension talks are also paused. Shams named Hart, along with Towns, as a player whose situation could heat up near the February deadline if nothing changes. And Hart's situation is different from Towns's. He's thirty-one. He's playing through a ring-finger injury that, as he's said, could only be fully fixed with a joint replacement that would end his career. And he's the heart of this team. He shot a career-best forty-one percent from three last season. He missed the preseason finale with soreness in his right ankle, but Mike Brown doesn't expect it to affect his availability for tomorrow.

It's also worth remembering what Hart means to the way this team plays. He's the Knicks' best rebounder for his size, the one who pushes the ball in transition, and the one Mike Brown leans on to guard the other team's best wing when Anunoby needs a break. In the Washington preseason game, he was the best Knick on the floor while the starters played. Players like that are hard to find and harder to replace under the apron, because they don't show up as stars on the trade market. They show up as glue.

The team option actually gives the Knicks more control over Hart than they have over Towns. They can decide next summer whether to pick it up. So Hart's situation isn't urgent in the same way. But if the front office wants to reward him with long-term security, the clock on that conversation is running too.

Now the second story: Tyler Kolek. And this is where the headline is simply out of date.

Last week, Ian Begley reported that the Knicks would explore trades involving Kolek, and that they'd need to make a trade by Monday afternoon if they wanted to keep Bruce Brown alongside James Wiseman. One argument made at the time was that Kolek was the most tradable young player on the roster because of his contract. He makes about two point three million this season, with a team option for next season, so a new team would get two years of cheap control. Compare that with McBride, who can be a free agent next summer, and with Pacôme Dadiet, whose fourth-year option for next season is worth about five point three million. There have been suggestions that the Knicks might decline that option, though I haven't seen that confirmed.

The logic was good. The market didn't cooperate. No team would take Kolek or Dadiet on the Knicks' terms, even with second-round picks attached, on a weekend when every team was cutting players. So on Saturday, the Knicks waived Bruce Brown, made Wiseman's spot official, and kept both Kolek and Dadiet. As we explained last time, that actually kept the Knicks' trade flexibility for the next two months, because Kolek, Dadiet and McBride are almost the only non-starters the Knicks can trade before December 15th.

I'll say what I said about Kolek before. He's a real backup point guard. Last season he played sixty-two games, averaging four point four points and two point seven assists, shooting almost thirty-nine percent from three, and he had fourteen points, five rebounds and five assists in the NBA Cup final. His defense can still be targeted. But he's a smart, team-first organizer on a cheap deal. Losing him for nothing would have been a mistake. Keeping him gives the Knicks a fifth guard and a trade chip.

And there's a bigger reason to keep young players like Kolek, Dadiet and Diawara, and it's the age of this roster. Brunson is thirty. Towns is thirty and turns thirty-one next month. Hart is thirty-one. Bridges is thirty. Anunoby turns thirty next summer. This is a veteran championship core, and under the second apron, the Knicks can't simply buy younger players in free agency. They have to develop them. Diawara, at twenty-one, just signed a four-year, eleven-million-dollar deal. Dadiet is twenty-one, six-nine and still raw. And Dink Pate, twenty, is back in Westchester. Those are the players who'll matter if this core has to be refreshed in two or three years.

So, the honest summary. The Knicks are not about to trade two key players. The Towns trade ideas are hypotheticals from one writer, and none of them makes sense for New York right now. The Towns contract question is real, and the extension window closes at tip-off tomorrow, which means this conversation moves to next summer. Hart's future is a quieter version of the same question. And the Kolek story ended with Kolek staying.

What should you watch?

Tomorrow night, watch Towns. The ring, the banner, the crowd. How does he play on the first night of a season with no extension and an open question about his future? I'd guess he plays with something to prove, the way he talked about it at media day.

And watch the crowd. Garden crowds have a way of telling a player how much the city wants him to stay. If the fans give Towns one of the biggest ovations during the ring ceremony, that's a message too, and front offices notice those things.

Watch how Mike Brown uses him. Brown hinted that Towns might play more at power forward this season. Against a big Philadelphia team with Joel Embiid, that might be the night to try it, with Drummond or Wiseman at center.

And over the next few months, watch real reporting, not proposals. If Shams, Bondy, Begley or Katz report that teams are calling about Towns, that's news. If a writer lists three landing spots, it's an idea.
