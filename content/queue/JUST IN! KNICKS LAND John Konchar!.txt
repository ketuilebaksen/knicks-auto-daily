So who is John Konchar? Because if you only know him as one of the names on the camp list, you're missing a great story.

Konchar played college basketball at Purdue Fort Wayne, a school most fans couldn't find on a map. He finished as the program's all-time leading scorer, with more than two thousand points, and he was first-team All-Summit League four years in a row. In college he averaged about fifteen and a half points, eight and a half rebounds and four assists. Those are strange numbers for a guard, and they tell you exactly what kind of player he is. He does a bit of everything, and he rebounds like a forward.

He went undrafted in twenty nineteen. Memphis signed him to a two-way deal. A year later they gave him a multi-year contract. And in July of twenty twenty-two, they signed him to a three-year extension worth about nineteen million dollars. For an undrafted player from a small school, that's a remarkable climb. He earned every dollar of it by being the kind of player coaches trust. Defence, rebounding, the right pass, no mistakes.

Then last February, his career got shuffled. On the third of February, Memphis traded him to Utah in the deal built around Jaren Jackson Jr. Konchar went to the Jazz along with Jackson, Jock Landale and Vince Williams Jr. And in Utah, he had some memorable nights, including two triple-doubles in April.

Now, why did the Knicks want him? I think the answer is in the profile.

Konchar is, in a lot of ways, a smaller version of what Josh Hart does. A guard who rebounds above his size, defends with effort, moves the ball and doesn't need shots to be effective. And there's one more detail about how Konchar got here that I think is worth sitting with, because it's a lesson about this entire league right now.

Minnesota didn't waive him because he played badly. They waived him because they wanted to sign Jonathan Kuminga, and the second apron made it impossible to keep both. A useful veteran lost his job to a spreadsheet. That's the same force that cost the Knicks Mitchell Robinson this summer. It's the same force that leaves New York with two open roster spots and only enough room to fill one.

The apron is reshaping how every contender builds a bench. Teams can't keep their eighth, ninth and tenth men at market prices anymore. So players like Konchar get squeezed out of one team and land in a camp somewhere else on a non-guaranteed deal. For a team like the Knicks, that's an opportunity. For the players, it's a much less secure way to make a living, even for someone who has earned a reputation as a reliable professional.

And it's also why his Exhibit 9 deal cost the Knicks nothing to end. An Exhibit 9 is a non-guaranteed camp contract. Waiving him before the regular season leaves no dead money on the books and doesn't touch the apron. The front office didn't have to choose between supporting a player and protecting the cap. The structure of the contract let them do both.

Now, the part I promised. Can Konchar be traded? I've seen this idea floating around, and I want to put it to rest.

No. A waived player isn't under contract with anyone. There's nothing to trade. When a team waives a player, he goes on waivers for forty-eight hours. Any team can claim him, taking on his contract. If nobody does, he becomes a free agent and can sign anywhere. Either way, the Knicks no longer hold his rights. You can't trade a player you don't have.

And even if he came back to New York, the trade idea still wouldn't work in the short term. A player who signs as a free agent can't be traded until the later of December fifteenth or three months after he signs. That's the same rule we've been talking about with the camp invites and Andre Drummond. So a returning Konchar in October couldn't be moved until January at the earliest.

So if you see a video calling him a trade chip, now you know. It's not how the rules work.
