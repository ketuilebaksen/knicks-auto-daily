Now, what did Wiseman say? Two things stood out, and they were relayed in Knicks coverage this week, including by Posting and Toasting.

On what he brings, Wiseman talked about using his athleticism. Being able to "put my head on the rim every single time," run the floor, and be vocal. That's a clear self-scouting report. He knows his job is vertical: lobs, putbacks, rim runs. He's not trying to be Karl-Anthony Towns.

And on his journey, he was very open. He said it's a blessing just to be here, to go out and enjoy himself and keep working, and to stay diligent after all the injuries and adversity he's had. "It's been a lot," he said. He talked about putting God first, grinding every day, taking it one day at a time, and controlling what he can. Asked what he's learned about himself, he said resilience and persistence through the process.

Now remember how we got here, because his story is the reason those words carry weight. Second overall pick in 2020, onto a Warriors team chasing championships. Missed the entire 2021-22 season with a knee injury. Traded to Detroit in February of 2023, onto losing teams. Signed with Indiana and tore his Achilles in his very first game in October of 2024. Over roughly two and a half years after that, he played only about five NBA games. A short stint with the Pacers last fall, then a ten-day deal in December, then released. And in September, an Exhibit 9 tryout in New York. This is a twenty-five-year-old who's been called a bust for years, and who's played fewer games than a lot of second-year players. When he says "it's been a lot," that's not a cliché.

And Mike Brown has noticed the change. We quoted the first part of this in the last video. Here's the fuller version, as it was relayed this week. Brown said that back in Golden State, Wiseman was talented but really raw in his ability and feel on the court. His feel is a lot higher now. But he's also been humbled by the challenges and adversity in his career, and he understands how hard it is. Brown said everything was new to Wiseman back then. He was a "wide-eyed young puppy," and now he's more seasoned, with the knowledge that this isn't going to be easy and opportunities can come and go like that.

I think that's the most important quote in this whole roster battle. A coach doesn't talk like that about a guy he's about to cut. He's telling you he sees a different person, not just a different player. And notice what Brown hasn't done all camp. He hasn't talked at length about Eubanks. He hasn't talked at length about Bradley. When the coach keeps volunteering thoughts about one player, that's usually the player.

Now let me give you the other side, because if I only told you the good parts, I'd be doing exactly what I criticize other channels for.

On offense, Wiseman was one of the best players on the floor for New York on Tuesday. He ran, he rebounded, he finished lobs, and he worked in the post after offensive rebounds. That's what the Knicks need from a third center.

On defense, the film from Tuesday was much more worrying, and people who broke it down picked up the same thing I'd want you to watch. In the pick-and-roll, when he was in drop coverage, he was sitting too deep. Drop coverage means the big man stays back near the paint to protect the rim while the guard fights over the screen. That's fine. But if you drop too far, you give the ball handler a free pull-up, or enough room to build speed and drive right into your chest. On Tuesday, the Raptors took advantage of both. They shot over the space, and they drove into him, drew contact and got to the line.

The second issue is verticality. When a rim protector contests at the basket, he needs to jump straight up, arms up. If you lean into the shooter, even a little, the referee will call a foul. Wiseman leaned too often. That's why he had no blocks in twenty-four minutes despite his size and athleticism. He wasn't getting beat by a lack of effort. He was getting beat by angles and timing.

These are fixable problems. They're coaching problems, not talent problems. How deep to sit in drop coverage depends on the scouting report, the opponent and the game plan. That's learned with reps, and Wiseman simply hasn't had many. That's the key point. He's twenty-five, but in NBA games, experience and film sessions, he's closer to a second- or third-year player. He's never had a stable two-season run in one system. If he gets one in New York, with Mike Brown, Towns and Drummond around him, he'll get better. It won't happen overnight, but give him a few months and you should see it.

And there's a structural reason the Knicks can live with that. The third center doesn't play every night. When he does play, it's usually because of foul trouble or rest, often for six to ten minutes. Defensive mistakes in a role like that are survivable. And if it's not working by early January, his non-guaranteed contract can be waived before the league-wide guarantee date. Bobby Marks called it a flyer, and that's what it is: a bet with a known cost and a deadline.

Now let's talk about the man on the other side of this decision, because he deserves more than a footnote.

If the reports are right, Bruce Brown will be waived before the season. A thirty-year-old who played all eighty-two games last season in Denver, a 2023 champion who scored twenty-one in Game 4 of that Finals, who told the Post this month that the second apron came up all summer as the reason teams wouldn't sign him. "I thought I played well enough to have a guaranteed spot on a team," he said. "That wasn't the case." And now it looks like the same apron math is about to cost him this spot too.

To be fair to the Knicks, this isn't a judgment on Bruce Brown. The backcourt is crowded: Brunson, McBride, Alvarado, Shamet, Clarkson, Kolek. On the wings there's Hart, Bridges, Anunoby, Diawara and Dadiet. Mike Brown has a lot of guards and wings. He has two centers. When you can only add one player, you add what you don't have. And as I said last video, I changed my lean toward Wiseman after Tuesday because he did what I said would change my mind. Bruce Brown played well in camp. He just plays a position where the Knicks already had answers.

What happens to him? Since he's on an Exhibit 9 deal, once he's waived he becomes an unrestricted free agent. Earlier this offseason, there were reports that the Lakers were interested and that Denver had an open spot. With final cuts happening across the league this week, there will be other teams looking for a physical, versatile guard on a minimum deal. My guess is that he won't be unemployed for long. And honestly, I'd love to see him get a real guaranteed deal somewhere. He earned it.

Let me also explain how a cut like this actually works, because the mechanics matter for Bruce Brown, Eubanks and Bradley.

When a team waives a player, he goes on waivers for forty-eight hours. During that window, any team can claim him and take over his contract as it stands. For camp players on non-guaranteed minimum deals, claims are uncommon, because a claiming team would be taking a roster spot for a player it could probably sign as a free agent two days later anyway. So the usual path is this: he clears waivers and becomes a free agent who can sign anywhere. For a veteran like Bruce Brown, that's actually the better outcome. He picks his next team instead of being claimed by one.

For Tony Bradley, the hamstring makes it more complicated. And this is exactly why the Exhibit 9 contract exists. It protects the team if a camp player gets hurt in camp. Instead of owing him a full season of salary because of an injury in a tryout, the team's liability is limited. Bradley will get his treatment and his rehab. But from the Knicks' side, the injury doesn't trap them into a contract. That's one of the less glamorous reasons all five of these camp players were signed to Exhibit 9 deals in the first place.

And there's one more rule that explains why the Knicks can't just wait. During the regular season, teams can't carry fewer than fourteen standard contracts for more than short stretches, about two weeks at a time. So the Knicks can't open the season with thirteen and "see what shakes loose" on the waiver wire for a month. They have to fill the fourteenth spot now, with the best option they have now. If something better comes along in December or January, that's when the non-guaranteed contract becomes their flexibility.

Think about what that means for the rest of the season. The Knicks will be sitting at fourteen players, right under the apron, with no fifteenth spot. Every injury will test the depth. If two wings go down for a week, Mike Brown will be playing two-way players like McCullar, Nickel and Akins, who are limited to a set number of active games. That's the trade-off ownership has chosen by treating the second apron as a hard ceiling. You keep the core together, and you live with a thin fourteenth and fifteenth spot. It's also why choosing the right fourteenth man matters so much more for this team than for most.

There's one more name I want to mention, because it's the kind of detail that matters if anything goes wrong. On Wednesday, Toronto waived Trey Jemison, a six-ten center who spent last season with the Knicks on a two-way deal. He played thirteen NBA games for New York and was solid in Westchester, about eight points, eight and a half rebounds and more than a block a game. There's no sign the Knicks want him back. Bringing him in on a two-way would mean moving one of McCullar, Nickel or Akins, and a standard deal would run into the apron again. But if Bradley's hamstring is serious, or if Wiseman's body doesn't hold up, it's useful to know a familiar big man is on the market.
