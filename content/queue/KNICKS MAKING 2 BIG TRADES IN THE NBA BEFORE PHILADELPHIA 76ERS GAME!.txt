First, let's be precise about what the plan actually is, because "hunt the center" is something people say without explaining.

Here's how you attack a team with two real bigs and nothing behind them. You don't wait. You go at the starting center in the first six minutes of the game.

You run at him in transition so he has to make a decision at full speed. You put him in ball screens over and over so he's making a choice every trip. You drive straight at his chest early, when officials are calling everything tight. And you get him to two fouls before the first quarter ends.

Now what happens? The coach has a choice, and both options are bad. Leave Towns in with two fouls, and he plays soft for the rest of the half — he doesn't contest, he doesn't body anybody up, and suddenly your best defensive adjustment is gone without anyone even scoring on him. Or sit him, and now Drummond is playing longer than planned in the first half.

Then you do it again to Drummond. And Drummond, for all his rebounding, has never been the most disciplined defender in space. Get him switched onto a guard twice and he's in trouble too.

And that's the whole plan. You're not trying to score on the center. You're trying to remove both of them from the game without ever taking a shot over them.

That's why the fourteenth roster spot matters so much. It isn't about a guy who plays fifteen minutes a night. It's about the six minutes in the third quarter when both of your centers are sitting with foul trouble in a playoff game.

Now let's go team by team, because the East is full of rosters built to run exactly that play.

They have Joel Embiid, which is already the single hardest matchup in this conference for our frontcourt. You cannot double him without giving up open threes, and now they have LeBron James and Jaylen Brown on the wings to punish exactly that.

Philadelphia is the team most likely to make the center spot hurt on night one.

Now let's talk about the answers, because there are three of them and they're not equally good.

Answer number one is the trade. Get another real big before the deadline. We did a whole video on the board — Moussa Diabaté and Johni Broome as realistic options, Yves Missi as the dream. That's the cleanest fix, and it's the one I'd prioritize.

Answer number two is the camp winner. Whoever takes that last roster spot, most likely James Wiseman or Drew Eubanks. And here's how I'd judge whether it's enough. The question isn't whether he can score. It's whether he can play six honest minutes without the defense collapsing. Can he stay in front on a switch? Can he box out? Can he avoid fouling while doing both?

That's a low bar. But it's a real bar, and plenty of backup centers in this league don't clear it.

Answer number three is the one nobody talks about, and it's the most interesting to me. Scheme.

Mike Brown has options that don't require a single roster move. He can pre-switch actions so Towns never gets dragged into space in the first place. He can mix in zone for stretches to hide a weak defensive five. He can change his substitution pattern — and honestly, that's the one I'll be watching from game one.

Because here's a number worth tracking all season. Watch when Towns gets his first rest. If Mike Brown starts pulling him earlier than usual in the first quarter, that's the coaching staff admitting they can't afford foul trouble. It's a small thing that tells you everything about how confident they are in the bench behind him.

And there's a fourth answer, which is Towns himself.

He's a twelfth-year veteran now. He shot forty-five percent from three in a championship postseason. The skill isn't the question. The question is discipline — verticality instead of reaching, not biting on pump fakes, taking the tough foul only when it's actually worth it.

If Towns plays a disciplined season, this entire problem shrinks. Not disappears. Shrinks. And that's genuinely within his control.

And there's one more layer to this that I think gets missed completely.

The foul-hunting plan doesn't just hurt the defense. It changes our offense too.

Think about it. Towns is the reason the spacing works. When he's on the floor, the opposing center has to leave the paint, and that's what opens driving lanes for Jalen Brunson and cutting room for Josh Hart and Mikal Bridges.

Take Towns off the floor and put Drummond on, and the opposing center goes right back to sitting in the paint, because Drummond isn't pulling anybody out to the three-point line. So the drives get harder. The cuts get crowded. The whole offense tightens up.

So when a team hunts Towns' fouls, they're not just removing a defender. They're shrinking the floor at both ends at the same time. That's why those minutes swing games so far beyond what a backup center's box score looks like.

And it's also the argument for a specific kind of replacement. The ideal third big for this roster isn't the biggest body available — it's the one who can roll hard enough and move well enough that defenses still have to respect something. That's exactly why Diabaté's profile keeps coming up, and why a slow, ground-bound center wouldn't actually solve this.

Now let me give you the honest counterweight, because I don't want to scare anybody into thinking this season is doomed.

We won a championship with this problem. It existed last year too. Robinson helped, absolutely — but this team's identity was never rim protection. It was a great starting five, elite chemistry, and the ability to switch and scramble. That hasn't gone anywhere.

And there's a version of this where the depth barely matters. If Towns stays healthy and out of foul trouble, and the starters are as good as they were in May, the fourteenth man plays garbage time and we spend the summer laughing about how worried we all were.

I just wouldn't bet a title defense on everything going right for eighty-two games and four playoff rounds.
