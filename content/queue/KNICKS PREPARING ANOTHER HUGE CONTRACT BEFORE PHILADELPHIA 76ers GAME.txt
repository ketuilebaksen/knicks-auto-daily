Tonight the Knicks raise their banner. And if you've seen headlines saying the Knicks are "preparing another huge contract" before the Philadelphia game, let me give you the honest version. Nobody is signing a big contract today. But there is one Knick who can still realistically get an extension during this season, and his agent just said the door is open. It's not Karl-Anthony Towns. It's Deuce McBride.

So today, let's go through the extension situation properly, and I need to start with a correction of my own. Then McBride, what his agent said and why he's in a strange spot. Then the bigger question a lot of New York columnists are asking on banner day: whether the biggest threat to a repeat is inside the building. And finally, the division, because the Knicks' road back to the Finals got a lot harder this summer, and it starts tonight.

Welcome back to Ketuil KNICKS. It's banner night at the Garden. Let's go.

First, the correction. In my last few videos, I told you that once the season tips off, there's no way Towns can sign an extension until next summer. That was too strong. Here's the more precise rule, and it matters.

The cutoff date for veteran extensions on contracts with more than one year left was October 19th, yesterday, the day before the season. After that date, only players on expiring contracts can sign extensions in the normal way. But a player with an option year can still be extended during the season if the option is declined as part of the process, which turns the current season into the final year of the contract. The cap analyst Keith Smith has pointed out that declining an option to enable an extension is a common move, and ESPN's Bobby Marks has explained that the first year of any new deal would replace the option year.

So here's what that means for each of the four Knicks whose talks were paused.

Karl-Anthony Towns has a player option for 2027-28 worth about sixty-one point one million. To sign an extension during this season, he'd have to decline that option. That's his choice, and it's risky for him. If he declines it and then no deal gets done, he becomes an unrestricted free agent next July. So it's possible, but it requires Towns to give up his safety net first.

Josh Hart has a team option for 2027-28 worth about twenty-two point four million. To extend him during the season, the Knicks would have to decline that option. That's the Knicks' choice, and it's risky for them, because if no deal follows, Hart becomes a free agent.

Tyler Kolek also has a team option for next season, so the same logic applies, though at a much smaller number. His option is worth only about two and a half million, so the Knicks have little reason to rush. They can simply pick it up next summer and keep him cheaply for another year.

Would Towns actually do that? Think about his position. His option is worth about sixty-one million next season, which is more than the first year of the Knicks' reported offer. An anonymous executive told the Post last week that the teams with cap room next summer could probably offer around forty-five to fifty million a year. So the option is his best guaranteed number. Declining it in the middle of the season, without a deal already agreed, would mean giving up that safety net in exchange for a negotiation that hasn't moved. I'd be surprised if he did that unless both sides already had an agreement in principle. In practice, that means the real Towns conversation is still most likely to happen next summer, when he has to decide on the option anyway.

And Deuce McBride is the exception. He's in the final year of his contract, so he can sign an extension anytime until June 30th of next year, with no option to deal with. He's the only one of the four whose extension doesn't require anyone to give anything up first.

Hart's situation has its own timeline. He said at media day there hadn't been much movement and that he didn't think a deal would get done, even though he'd love it. And he's warned that the conversation could change by February if there's no progress. That lines up with what Shams said about the "growing cloud" around Towns and Hart heating up near the deadline. The Knicks hold Hart's option, so they control the timing. But if the relationship is going to be repaired, it has to happen before the deadline turns it into a trade conversation.

So, technically, an extension is still possible for all four. Practically, only McBride's is straightforward. That's the real story behind "another contract," and I wanted to correct what I'd said before.

Now, McBride, because this is where it gets interesting.

His agent, Mark Bartelstein, went on SiriusXM NBA Radio this weekend, as reported by the New York Post. Here's what he said. On whether McBride wants an extension: "No doubt, he would love to get that. But at the end of the day, you work for the Knicks and it takes two sides to get a deal done." On the pause: "If the Knicks aren't ready to do extensions right now, then we just gotta buckle up and go have a great season." On the relationship: "We've got a great relationship with Leon and the front office." And then the line that got everyone's attention: "We'll see. There may be opportunities during the season, maybe in the near future." And finally: "Miles, like any player in his position, would love to get an extension. I think he's earned it."

That's a very calm, professional message. No complaining. No threats. Just: we want this, we respect the team, and we think there's a path.

And Bartelstein added a detail I didn't know. He said McBride came back from abdominal surgery before he was fully healthy, to help the team during the title run. That's the kind of thing a player's camp brings up when it wants credit for sacrifice. And honestly, it's fair.

Here's the context. McBride is twenty-six. He's making about three point nine six million this season, the last year of the three-year, thirteen-million-dollar extension he signed back in December of 2023. That deal turned out to be one of the best-value contracts in the league. Last season, he averaged twelve points in forty-one games, as the Knicks' main sixth man for long stretches. Earlier this fall, he told the Daily News he was "a little disappointed" with how the extension talks had gone.

And in the preseason finale against Toronto, he showed what he does. Three of six from three in thirteen minutes, nine points, including three straight threes to end the first quarter. But here's a detail worth noticing. In that same game, Mike Brown played Landry Shamet twenty-four minutes off the bench, almost twice as many as McBride. That doesn't mean McBride has lost his role. But it tells you there's real competition for those bench minutes, and Shamet earned his with a great preseason.

So why is McBride in a strange spot? Because he's the easiest Knick to extend, and also, in some ways, the most likely to be traded.

Think about it from the front office's view. The priority list for long-term money starts with Jalen Brunson. He's the captain and the Finals MVP, he took less money to help build this roster, and when his next extension comes up, the Knicks have to be ready to pay him. Then come Towns and Hart, the second-best player and the glue. McBride is fourth in that line. He could have the best season of his career and still be behind three players for the same limited pool of money under the second apron.

And at the same time, McBride's expiring contract is one of the few the Knicks can trade before December 15th. Along with Pacôme Dadiet and Kolek, he's one of the only non-starters who can be moved right now. A Knicks On SI piece argued this week that a McBride trade is still more likely than an extension. A Hoops Rumors roundup mentioned a columnist predicting the same. Those are opinions, not reports. But they reflect the math.

So the front office has two realistic paths with McBride. Path one: keep him all season, give the team its best shot at a repeat, and then either extend him or let him test free agency next summer. Path two: use his expiring contract in a trade before the deadline to bring back a player who fits the roster better, maybe a center or a wing on a longer deal.

My view? I'd keep him, at least until we see how the rotation shakes out. McBride is one of the best perimeter defenders on the bench, he can space the floor, and he's been part of every big moment the last three years. If Shamet's preseason holds up and the guard minutes get crowded, revisit it in January. But trading him in October would be selling low on a player the coaching staff trusts.

Now the bigger question, and it's an uncomfortable one. A lot of New York commentary on banner day is asking whether the biggest threat to a repeat isn't another team. It's ownership.

Let me lay out the argument, then give you the other side, because this deserves balance.

Here's the case critics are making. Right after the title, before the parade, James Dolan made it clear that the Knicks would not go into the second apron. That decision effectively meant goodbye to Mitchell Robinson, the team's longest-tenured player, its best rim protector, and one of the best offensive rebounders the league has seen. And he went to Boston, the Knicks' biggest rival. The reporting at the time framed it as an ownership call rather than a front-office basketball decision. Then on October 9th, the Knicks paused every extension negotiation at once. Towns said it was "news to me" and that he was "very disappointed, to say the least." Asked whether a deal would happen before the season, he said, "It don't look good." Josh Hart said at media day that there hadn't been much movement, and that he didn't think it would get done, though he'd love to.

And there's the White House question. After the title, the team indicated it had accepted an invitation to visit. Then at media day, Mike Brown said, "It's still something that our guys, or the organization, is internally discussing," and "whatever we decide to do, we'll all do together." President Trump attended Game 3 of the Finals at the Garden as Dolan's guest, which caused hours of disruption around the arena. Critics say an owner who announces things the locker room hasn't agreed to is creating a gap with his players.

Their conclusion: modern title windows are short, the best way to attract talent is to treat your own players well, and if players feel undervalued, it could cost the Knicks on the court.

Now the other side, and I think it's strong too.

First, the second apron isn't a small thing. A team above it can't combine salaries in trades, can't use its mid-level exception, and can have its future first-round pick pushed to the end of the round if it stays above for too long. Teams like Denver and Cleveland have lived above it, yes, but every team that does pays a long-term price in flexibility. Choosing to stay under it is a legitimate philosophy, not just cheapness, especially for a team that already has Brunson, Bridges and Anunoby on big contracts.

Second, pausing extensions is unusual in its timing, but not in its logic. A front office that wants to see how its players perform as defending champions before committing hundreds of millions of dollars into their thirties is being careful, not hostile. And as we just covered, extensions are still possible during the season if the right steps are taken.

Third, and this matters most, the players haven't stopped playing for each other. Brunson reportedly told teammates to stay professional. Towns said his only focus is winning another title. Hart said he'd love to get a deal done. McBride's agent spoke warmly about Leon Rose. That's not a fractured locker room. That's a group of professionals separating business from basketball, at least for now.

So my honest read: the risk is real but long-term. It won't decide tonight's game. It could matter in February, when Shams has said the situations involving Towns and Hart could heat up if nothing changes. And it'll matter most next summer, when Towns's option and Hart's team option come due. The Knicks became champions in part because everyone on that roster knew his role and his limits. The test for ownership is whether it knows its own.

Now, the division, because the road back starts tonight.

Think about how different this is from last season's other finalist. San Antonio, the team the Knicks beat in five games in the Finals, played in a division where most teams missed the playoffs. The Knicks have the opposite problem. Their own division got loaded this summer.

Toronto traded for Kawhi Leonard. Kawhi's health is always the question. He hasn't played sixty or more games in back-to-back seasons in a long time. But when he plays, he's still elite, a two-time Defensive Player of the Year, and Toronto can throw Kawhi, Scottie Barnes and the young Collin Murray-Boyles at Karl-Anthony Towns or Jalen Brunson. RJ Barrett and Immanuel Quickley are there too. We got a preview in the preseason. The Raptors' starters were outstanding in that hundred-and-forty-eight-to-ninety-eight game, even though the Knicks rested their regulars, and Toronto nearly won the finale at the Garden too.

Boston signed Mitchell Robinson, who now shares the center spot with Neemias Queta. That gives the Celtics two starter-level rim protectors and more depth at the five than either the Knicks or the Sixers. Jayson Tatum is back from the Achilles tear he suffered in 2025. They traded Jaylen Brown but got Paul George and four draft picks in return. And Joe Mazzulla has young players like Baylor Scheierman, Jordan Walsh and Hugo González who earned real minutes. Boston is always motivated against New York. And with Robinson there, it's personal now too.

And Philadelphia, tonight's opponent, added two former Finals MVPs. Jaylen Brown arrived from Boston in July, and LeBron James is starting a record twenty-fourth season, turning forty-two in December. They join Tyrese Maxey, who averaged about twenty-eight points and six and a half assists last season, and Joel Embiid, with Nick Nurse coaching. LeBron doesn't need to carry this team. He can be the Swiss Army knife: steady the offense, make the big plays, and push the pace in transition, where he was one of the league's best last season. The Sixers' big question is Embiid's health and his defense. In the playoffs last spring, the Knicks swept them, and Embiid struggled on that end. Behind him, they're thin, especially with Ariel Hukporti, the former Knick, out for the season with an Achilles tear.

And don't forget the West. San Antonio, the team the Knicks beat in the Finals, opens tonight too, against Oklahoma City in the late game. Victor Wembanyama said at media day that losing the Finals was "haunting." The Spurs are young, motivated and still improving. If the Knicks get back to the Finals, there's a decent chance the team waiting for them will be one that's spent the whole summer thinking about them.

So the Knicks will have to survive this division twice. Once during the regular season, to earn a good playoff seed, and then again in the playoffs, where they could face one or more of these teams. As one columnist put it, last spring the Knicks didn't face a single top-ten player in the playoffs until the Finals. This year, there are three in their own division. Four, if you count Brunson. And as long as the Knicks have their captain, they have a real shot.

So what should you watch tonight?

One, the ceremony and the start. The rings, the first banner since 1973, the crowd. Then the first six minutes. Mike Brown has spent all preseason saying the habits matter more than the score. Watch whether they come out sharp.

Two, McBride's minutes. With Shamet getting more run in the finale, McBride's role on opening night will tell you how the coaches see the bench right now.

Three, Towns. The extension cutoff has passed, and the only path to a deal this season now runs through him declining his option. How he plays tonight, and what he says after, will tell you a lot about his mindset for the season.

Four, the matchups. Who guards Jaylen Brown, and who guards LeBron? Anunoby, Bridges and Hart, if his ankle is fine. And on the other end, how does Embiid hold up against Towns pulling him out to the three-point line?

And five, the bench defense in transition. LeBron and Maxey will push the pace. That was the Knicks' biggest weakness in the preseason.

So that's it. No huge contract tonight, but a real path for McBride, a clearer picture of the extension rules, a fair debate about ownership, and arguably the toughest division in the league, starting tonight at the Garden.
