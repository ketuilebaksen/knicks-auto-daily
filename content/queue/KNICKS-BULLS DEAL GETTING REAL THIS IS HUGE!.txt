The New York Knicks had one of the best training camp stories in recent memory when they kept Landry Shamet on the roster last year.

That veteran guard turned out to be far more than a depth piece. He became one of the most reliable contributors during the championship run against San Antonio, played a meaningful role all postseason, and then took a team-friendly deal to stay this summer.

That's the kind of late-roster magic organizations dream about and almost never find.

So the question, as camp approaches for 2026-27, is whether the Knicks can pull off the same trick twice. And based on the names currently in play, the honest answer is almost certainly no.

Alright. According to SNY's Ian Begley, the Knicks are expected to invite big man Drew Eubanks and perimeter shooter John Konchar to camp this fall. Tyler Nickel, our second-round pick from the most recent draft, is also expected to compete for a spot — assuming he doesn't opt to return to college, which hasn't been fully ruled out yet.

Other names could still enter the picture. Kelly Olynyk has been floating around the market. Maxi Kleber is another option if he stays stateside instead of heading back overseas.

But regardless of who joins that list, the talent ceiling in this group is significantly lower than what we worked with last year, when the competition between Shamet, Malcolm Brogdon, and Garrison Mathews produced a genuine gem.

And I want to explain why, because the Shamet story gets told like a lottery ticket and it really wasn't.

He was not an unknown walking through the door. He'd already spent part of the 2024-25 season here before the championship year, which gave Mike Brown and this staff a real look at him on both ends. He showed a reliable outside shot, hitting nearly forty percent from deep, plus an underrated ability to stay in front of guards. And he'd been a well-regarded prospect since being drafted in 2018, with genuine stretches of quality basketball in Philadelphia, with the Clippers, and in Brooklyn.

He was a known quantity with upside. That's a completely different thing from a lottery ticket. The Knicks weren't lucky so much as they were early.

And none of the players currently on this list carry anything close to that track record.

Eubanks comes the closest to being a legitimate rotation candidate. He's shown a reliable ability to finish around the basket throughout his career and offers some rim protection and offensive rebounding that could be useful in limited doses.

The problem is he couldn't consistently earn meaningful minutes in Sacramento last season — and that was a team hardly overflowing with frontcourt depth. So if Eubanks somehow ends up overtaking Andre Drummond as the primary backup center, something has clearly gone wrong with the plan.

That's not a standard to hope for. That's the realistic ceiling in his case. We need a third center who provides genuine insurance, and Eubanks is a camp body, not a solution.

Konchar is a harder sell. His reputation as a knockdown shooter has never actually been backed up by consistent playing time or production at this level — he's simply never been given enough run to prove whether that reputation is real. And at six-five, he doesn't give us the positional flexibility this rotation needs from a wing, especially given how much we depend on versatile, switchable defenders.

Nickel has genuine upside as a late second-round find. But he profiles firmly as a developmental player right now. He's not the kind of immediate contributor who fills either of our two most pressing needs — a third center, or a reliable backup wing with real size.

And the other names occasionally mentioned, veterans like Gabe Vincent and Kevin Love, are simply too far removed from their best basketball to offer meaningful hope. The roster math is challenging enough without leaning on players well past their peak.

Now, none of this is catastrophic. Camp invites exist precisely for this process and every team goes through it. We were exceptionally lucky to hit a home run with Shamet. Expecting that twice in a row with a thinner pool is unrealistic.

But it does tell you something important. If Mike Brown and Leon Rose are going to find a meaningful rotation addition before October, the path runs through the trade market — not the waiver wire, not another team's late cuts.

Which brings us perfectly to the next story, because there's a decision sitting on Leon Rose's desk right now that unlocks exactly that.

The Knicks have until October thirty-first to decide whether to exercise the fourth-year team option on Pacôme Dadiet's rookie contract for the 2027-28 season.

And based on everything the twenty-one year old has shown — or more accurately, failed to show — picking that option up would be a serious mistake.

Dadiet was selected twenty-fifth overall in the 2024 draft — a first-round investment made expecting the French forward would develop into a real rotation piece. Two seasons later, the returns have been poor and the patience is thin.

He's appeared in only forty-seven regular season games across that span, and those minutes have been almost entirely garbage time in games decided long before he checked in. With the championship core brought back intact, real playing time for a player at his current level is nearly impossible to find.

The financial math makes it clearer. That fourth-year option is valued at approximately five point four million. His third-year option for this season, which New York did exercise, came in around three million — a notably lower number that made sense to absorb while the team kept evaluating.

Locking in another five point four million for a player who can't crack a championship rotation is very hard to justify this close to the second apron. Every dollar matters, and committing that money limits your options everywhere else.

And Summer League was his clearest chance to make a case. He didn't take it.

In four July appearances he shot just thirty-six point four percent from the field and a deeply troubling sixteen percent from three. He also turned the ball over more than twice as often as he recorded assists — alarming for a player who's supposed to have offensive instincts and creation ability.

Those numbers are hard to dismiss precisely because it was Summer League. The baseline expectation for a first-round pick entering year three is that you dominate developmental talent. He couldn't. And at the end of August he put together another string of subpar performances for Ivory Coast in FIBA World Cup qualifying — again, not NBA competition, and he still couldn't separate himself.

That pattern, more than any single bad game, is what makes it so hard to imagine his option leading anywhere productive. When a first-round pick can't earn minutes after two full seasons, it's rarely just about opportunity.

There's also a trade dimension that matters. Exercising his option would add five point four million in fully guaranteed money, which substantially complicates any future deal — teams willing to absorb an uncertain young player want to limit their exposure, and a large guarantee makes that hard.

And we have cleaner alternatives. We hold three second-round picks in 2027, any of which carries a smaller cap hit than his option. A veteran minimum signing costs even less.

Letting it lapse is the only decision that makes sense, and the expectation around the league is that's exactly what happens before October thirty-first.

Now here's the twist nobody saw coming, and it ties this whole video together.

The Knicks lost one of their most valued assistants this offseason when Jordan Brink left Mike Brown's staff for the Chicago Bulls. Brink wasn't just any assistant — he was critical to player development inside this system, and his departure was widely seen as one of the more significant losses of the summer.

But Brink's move to Chicago may actually create a trade opportunity for the franchise he just left.

Because he joined the Bulls not only as an assistant coach, but as their new director of player development. That dual title gives him real influence over which young players Chicago prioritizes — and potentially which players they pursue in trades.

And New York has two obvious candidates for a deal there. Pacôme Dadiet and Tyler Kolek.

Both carry uncertain futures here. Both fit neatly into what Chicago is building in a rebuild. And critically, the Bulls' cap sheet is relatively uncluttered compared to most of the East, which gives them the flexibility to absorb a contract from a team trying to clear space.

For Dadiet, a trade to Chicago where he could develop in a larger role at least gives this situation a productive resolution for both sides — instead of an option decline and nothing to show for a first-round pick.

Kolek presents different value. He's signed to a modest deal worth roughly four point eight million over the next two seasons combined, making him one of the better bargains in our reserve unit in pure salary terms. But signing Jose Alvarado to a three-year deal significantly reduced the urgency to keep him. With Brunson as the unquestioned starter and Alvarado locked in behind him, Kolek has no clear path to minutes.

Meanwhile Chicago is watching its own backup point guard situation evolve, with Tre Jones playing his way toward a larger contract and Rob Dillingham on an expiring deal. Kolek is a cost-effective option for them to stash and develop.

And the Brink connection genuinely matters, because front office relationships accelerate trade talks. He worked directly with both Dadiet and Kolek here. He was the head coach of the Knicks Summer League team that won the title with both of them on the roster. He knows their games and their ceilings firsthand — insight Chicago wouldn't otherwise have without extensive scouting, and that removes the uncertainty that normally slows deals involving young reserves.

From our side, the math works too. Operating near the second apron strictly limits what we can add. Moving Dadiet or Kolek opens a roster spot and creates flexibility for Rose to chase what we actually need — most urgently, a third center behind Karl-Anthony Towns and Andre Drummond.

A Chicago deal doesn't solve the center problem directly. But it clears the path to solve it. Sometimes creating space is the necessary first step.

There's also Tyler Nickel to consider. Rose reportedly wants to sign him to a standard multi-year contract rather than a two-way deal, given what he flashed before the draft. But under current constraints, New York can realistically do one of two things — sign Nickel long-term, or add another center. Not both.

Dealing Dadiet or Kolek to Chicago resolves that dilemma entirely by creating the room to pursue both.
