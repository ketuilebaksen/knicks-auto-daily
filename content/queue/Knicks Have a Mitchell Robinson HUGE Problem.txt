But a real basketball problem that ties together almost everything that happened this week. The Knicks just won a wild game against Portland on a Mikal Bridges buzzer-beater, after blowing a twenty-six point lead. Their defense is one of the best in the league, and their offense is one of the worst. And the center they let walk this summer is struggling in Boston, which has some people asking whether a reunion could make sense. So let's be clear from the start. There is no Mitchell Robinson trade. Nobody has reported talks between the Knicks and the Celtics. What we have is a Heavy column by Sean Deveney, picked up by other sites, arguing that a reunion would make sense. That's an idea, not a rumor with sources behind it. But the problem the idea is trying to solve is very real. So today, let's follow one thread through all of it. What the Knicks lost when Robinson left, what Andre Drummond is and isn't giving them, what happened in that Portland game, and whether bringing Mitch back is realistic or just a nice dream.

Let's start with the game, because it was one of the strangest wins of the season.

The Knicks were rolling. They led sixty-six to forty right before halftime. A twenty-six point lead, at home, on a night when the franchise was celebrating its alumni. Then everything fell apart. They shot five of twenty-two in the fourth quarter. Jalen Brunson missed all five of his fourth quarter shots. Portland came all the way back and took the lead. And with the Knicks down two in the final seconds, Brunson was blanketed, the Blazers took him away completely, and Josh Hart had to find someone else on the inbounds pass. He found Bridges. Bridges set his feet behind the line and buried a three at the buzzer. Knicks win, ninety-seven to ninety-six.

And it's worth remembering who was missing. Karl-Anthony Towns was out with an illness. OG Anunoby was out. Jordan Clarkson was out. Brown didn't have his usual weapons. He needed someone else to step up, and the guy who did it has been the hardest player on this roster to figure out all season.

Here's what Bridges said afterward, and I think it tells you a lot about where his head has been. He said, how Josh threw it kind of indicated that I was going to be open, so you just try to make a play. And then he said, first you win the game, but second, seeing the joy of those guys, and with the alumni there, it's a pretty cool moment. Seeing the joy of those guys. That's a player who has heard every bit of the criticism this season. And for one night, he got to be the reason everybody was smiling.

Mike Brown backed him up, too. He said Bridges is the same when, in Brown's words, quote unquote, we think he's struggling. He just keeps playing and doesn't let anything affect him. He's going to let it fly, he's going to push it, and he's going to defend. That's a coach showing trust, even after a tough start.

But I want to be honest about the bigger picture, because one shot doesn't erase a pattern. Bridges finished with twelve points. And over his previous five games, according to the video, he scored eight, seventeen, five, eighteen and two. Good game, bad game, good game, bad game, and then a really bad one. That's the story of his season so far. It's not a slump that comes and goes. It's inconsistency, game to game. And there's a reason Landry Shamet has been closing games instead of him. Bridges starts. Shamet often finishes. That's the arrangement right now. One buzzer-beater is a great memory, and it might help his confidence. But the real test is what he does in the next ten games.

Now let's connect that game to the bigger issue. Look at that fourth quarter again. Five of twenty-two. Brunson, oh for five. When the offense stalls like that, what does a team usually lean on? Easy baskets. Lobs. Putbacks. Second chances. And that's exactly the kind of offense the Knicks used to get from Mitchell Robinson.

Here are the season numbers the video cites. The Knicks are giving up about one hundred three and a half points a game, the second fewest in the league. That's elite. But they're scoring only about one hundred nine and a half, which the video says is the fifth lowest in the NBA. That's the trade-off in one sentence. The defense held up after Robinson left. The offense didn't.

So what exactly did they lose? Let's go to the numbers, because Daily Knicks did a really interesting breakdown, and it changes the way I think about Drummond.

The first thing you'd assume is that the Knicks lost offensive rebounding. And they didn't, really. According to Daily Knicks, Drummond is grabbing about twenty-one point two percent of the Knicks' own misses when he's on the floor. Robinson's mark last season was twenty-three point nine percent. That's close. Drummond is still one of the best offensive rebounders in basketball. He's been doing it for fifteen years. So the rebounding itself is fine.

The problem is what happens after the rebound. And this is the part I find fascinating.

When Robinson grabbed an offensive board, he usually did one of two things. He dunked it right away, or he threw it back out to a shooter and reset the possession. He almost never forced up a tough shot of his own. Drummond is different. He's much more likely to go right back up with it, through traffic, from awkward angles. And according to Daily Knicks, it isn't working. In the video's version of the numbers, Drummond takes about twenty-four field goal attempts for every thirty-six offensive rebounds he grabs. Robinson took about seven. So Drummond is shooting more than three times as often after an offensive board.

And he isn't finishing. He's shooting about forty-four percent on second-chance opportunities, and about forty-seven point eight percent at the rim, when the league average at the rim is around sixty-seven percent. That's a big gap. For a center, that's a really low number.

Here's what it costs the team. With Drummond on the floor, the Knicks score about one point one nine points per second-chance possession. Last season, with Robinson, it was about one point two eight. That might not sound like much. But over a season, over hundreds of possessions, that adds up to real points. Points this offense can't afford to lose.

And here's the part that really jumps out. According to the same breakdown, when the Knicks kick the ball out after an offensive rebound and shoot a three, they get about one point four one points per shot. When Drummond tries to score at the rim himself, it's about point nine six. So kicking it out is roughly forty-seven percent more efficient than letting Drummond go back up.

So should Brown tell Drummond to stop shooting? I don't think it's that simple. You can't ask a center to never go back up. Sometimes the putback is right there and you have to take it. And Drummond has done a lot of good things for this team. He was the defensive player of the game in the first Cleveland win. The Knicks had twenty-six second-chance points in that game. He's had nights where he grabbed fourteen rebounds in sixteen minutes. But I do think there's a simple adjustment here. When the putback isn't clean, throw it out. Let Shamet, Bridges or Brunson take a wide-open three after the defense has scrambled. Daily Knicks put it well. Fighting the impulse to shoot and kicking it back out isn't that much to ask. I agree. That's not a criticism of Drummond's effort. It's a coaching point. And it's one that could add real points to an offense that needs them.

And there's one more thing Robinson gave the Knicks that numbers don't fully show. The lob. Robinson was a vertical threat. When Brunson drove and the defense collapsed, Robinson was up at the rim waiting for the pass. That forced defenses to make a choice. Stay home on Robinson, and Brunson gets to the basket. Help on Brunson, and Robinson catches a lob. Drummond doesn't play above the rim the same way. So defenses can load up on Brunson without paying for it at the rim. That's a big part of why Brunson has had nights like the Portland fourth quarter, where every shot comes against a crowd.

So that's the problem. The Knicks didn't lose a rebounder. They lost a finisher and a lob threat. And that's exactly the gap the reunion idea is trying to fill.

Now let's talk about Robinson in Boston, because things haven't gone well there.

Robinson left the Knicks this summer and signed a three-year deal with the Celtics. The video says it's worth forty-three point seven million dollars. The number widely reported in July was closer to forty-seven million over three years. Either way, it's a fair price for a starting-caliber defensive center, and Boston paid it.

According to the video and the Heavy column, Robinson started the season as a starter, but he's now come off the bench for nine straight games. He's averaging about sixteen minutes a night, with about five points and five rebounds a game. Both of those would be career lows for him.

Why? Deveney's explanation is fit. Boston has tried to use him further away from the basket, more like a stretch big. And Robinson isn't a stretch big. He's never been a shooter. He's a lob threat, a rim protector and an offensive rebounder. When you take him away from the rim, you take away almost everything that makes him valuable. That's not a knock on Robinson. It's a mismatch between the player and the system.

And the Heavy column is blunt about the Knicks side, too. It says Drummond has been a very good rebounder, but he still isn't the monster on the offensive glass that Robinson is, and he comes nowhere close to being the lob threat his predecessor was. That lines up exactly with what the Daily Knicks numbers say.

So the fit is obvious. Robinson needs a team that plays him near the rim. The Knicks need a center who finishes near the rim. On paper, it's a perfect match.

But on paper isn't how trades work. Let's go through the real questions.

Question one. Would Boston trade Robinson to a rival at all? The Heavy column says maybe. It points out that this is the same franchise that, in the column's words, sent one of the best players in team history, Jaylen Brown, to the Philadelphia seventy-sixers this summer. The point is that Boston will make whatever deal is best for Boston, even with a rival. And Robinson's contract isn't an albatross. Teams around the league know what he can do in the right role. That's fair. But I'd still say it's unlikely. Boston and New York are division rivals, and the Knicks just won a championship with Robinson. Handing him back would be a strange look for the Celtics, unless the return was clearly better than anything else on the table.

Question two. When could it even happen? Robinson signed with Boston as a free agent this summer. Players who sign in the offseason generally can't be traded until December fifteenth. So no deal could happen before then, no matter how much either side wanted it.

Question three. And this is the big one. Can the Knicks afford it? Robinson's salary this season is somewhere around fifteen million dollars. The Knicks are above the first apron, so they can't take back more salary than they send out. And they don't have a lot of mid-sized contracts. Let's look at the options.

Option one is Josh Hart. Hart makes about twenty point nine million. On salary alone, Hart for Robinson works easily. The Knicks would even save money. But Hart is one of the heart and soul players on this team, he's a Villanova brother to Brunson and Bridges, and he's the guy who threw the inbounds pass that won the Portland game. I don't think the Knicks are trading Hart for a backup center, and I don't think most fans would want them to.

Option two is a pile of smaller contracts. Miles McBride makes about three point nine six million. Jose Alvarado makes about four point four four million. Pacôme Dadiet makes about two point nine eight million. Add those three together and you get about eleven point four million. That's still not enough for Robinson. Add Landry Shamet, at about five point four nine million, and now you're over sixteen million, which would cover him. But Shamet isn't trade-eligible until January fifteenth, and he's been the best bench player on the team. And a four-for-one trade would leave the Knicks with only twelve players, which means they'd have to add two more within two weeks. Because of how little room they have under the second apron, they can't really sign anyone until around February fourteenth. So a deal like that would only make sense right at the deadline, and even then it would be very complicated.

Option three is some mix of Drummond, Alvarado, McBride and others. But if you trade Drummond, you've just given up the guy who's been holding down the backup center job, and you're relying entirely on Robinson staying healthy. Which, let's be honest, has never been a sure thing.

So here's the honest answer. A Robinson reunion makes basketball sense. But it doesn't make easy financial sense. The Knicks would almost certainly have to give up a rotation player they really like, or send out three or four small contracts at the deadline and figure out the roster afterward. And Boston would have to agree to help a rival. That's a lot of ifs.

There's also the long-term question. Robinson is under contract for three years. If the Knicks take that on, it affects next summer, when they have to figure out Towns and whether to keep players like McBride. It's not just this season's salary. It's the salary for the next two seasons too.

Now let's talk about one more name, because the video brings him up and he connects to all of this. Mohamed Diawara.

Is that a development problem or a coaching decision? A bit of both. Diawara is young, and he's still learning. But the bigger reason is that Brown trusts veterans in close games, and the Knicks have been in a lot of close games. Still, I like what SI said. Being out of the rotation in November isn't a death sentence under Brown. He's already experimented with Dalton Knecht, Alvarado, James Wiseman and Dadiet at different points this season. Diawara will get another chance. And with Clarkson out for the Portland game, injuries are already opening doors. That's how young players earn minutes on a contender. You stay ready, and when the door opens, you walk through it.

And the video also mentions the G League kids, including Knecht, who's been working with Westchester. That's the other side of the roster story. When the Knicks can't add players because of the apron, development becomes the only way to get better from inside. If one of those young players turns into a real contributor by February, that's like making a trade without making a trade.

So let's put the whole thread together.

Is there a Mitchell Robinson trade? No. There's a column arguing a reunion would make sense. No talks have been reported.

Is there a real problem? Yes. The Knicks' defense is elite, but their offense ranks near the bottom of the league, and losing Robinson's lobs and clean putbacks is part of the reason.

Is Drummond the problem? Not really. His rebounding is close to Robinson's. The issue is what he does after the rebound. He shoots far more often and finishes far less often. Kicking it out more would help.

Does the Bridges buzzer-beater change anything? It's a great moment, and it might help his confidence. But his scoring has been up and down all season, and Shamet is still closing games.

Could the Knicks realistically bring Robinson back? It's possible, but hard. Boston would have to agree to trade him to a rival. Nothing can happen before December fifteenth. And the Knicks would have to match around fifteen million dollars in salary, which means either Hart or a pile of smaller contracts at the deadline.
