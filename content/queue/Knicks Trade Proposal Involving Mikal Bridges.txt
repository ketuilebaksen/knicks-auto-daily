The Knicks’ four-game winning streak is over. They lost to the Cleveland Cavaliers in the rematch, and now they’re six and five. But the bigger conversation coming out of that game isn’t really about Cleveland. It’s about Mikal Bridges. His numbers have been rough, the trade proposals keep coming, and now both Jalen Brunson and Mike Brown have spoken about him. So today, I want to do this a little differently. I want to give you three voices on the Bridges situation. The captain, the coach, and the columnist. Then I’ll give you mine. Because each one of them tells you something different, and together they tell you exactly where this really stands.

First, let’s be honest about the numbers, because nobody should pretend they’re fine. Through this stretch of the season, Bridges is averaging about five point nine points a game. He’s shooting about thirty-three percent from the field and about twenty-six and a half percent from three. For a player who averaged over fourteen points last season and shot about forty-nine percent from the field, that’s a huge drop. And it’s not just the shooting. He’s been passive at times. He’s had turnovers. He’s not attacking the way he usually does. He didn’t take his first free throw of the season until early November. So yes, it’s a real slump. Nobody is making that up, and nobody should.

But here’s the first thing I want you to keep in mind. Bridges was not the reason the Knicks lost to Cleveland. Mike Brown himself called it one of the team’s worst defensive games in the last five or six. That’s a team problem, not a Bridges problem. And the Knicks were coming off four straight wins, including a road win in Cleveland a week earlier. Losing a rematch to a good team happens to every contender. It doesn’t mean the season is falling apart.

Now, voice number one: the captain.

After the game, Jalen Brunson was asked what he’s seeing from Bridges early this season. And his answer was one of the best things a captain could say. He said, no matter what’s going on with what people are saying, the people in this room have his back, night in and night out. He said, we see the work he puts into his game, we see the time he puts in. And he said, when you’re doing the right things day in and day out, things will eventually fall in your favor. Then he finished with this: that’s our brother, we’re going to keep it that way. Short, simple, and it says everything. Brunson doesn’t waste words, and when he chooses to say something like that in public, it’s because he wants every player in that locker room, and every fan watching, to hear it.

That’s not a small thing. Brunson and Bridges go back a long way. They won a national championship together at Villanova in twenty sixteen. They won an NBA championship together last June. When the most important player on the team says the locker room has his back, regardless of the noise outside, that tells you how the people who actually matter see this. They’re not panicking. They’re supporting him.

And remember, just a week ago, after the Knicks beat the Cavaliers in Cleveland, Kenny Atkinson pointed to the Knicks’ continuity as one of the reasons they win. He talked about the three players who played together in college, and how they play on a string on both ends of the floor. That’s Brunson, Hart and Bridges. Opposing coaches see that bond as a weapon. Brunson clearly sees it the same way.

Voice number two: the coach.

Mike Brown was asked about Bridges’ minutes, and he was honest. He said the Knicks have two guys playing really well right now, Landry Shamet and Miles McBride. And he said it’s going to be hard to find minutes at times for Mikal, and maybe even at times for Josh Hart, because those two are playing at a high level. He pointed out that it’s not just their offense. He said Shamet and McBride have been a big part of what the Knicks have done defensively too. And he said, if they’re playing at a high level, I’ve got to keep finding ways to get them on the floor.

That’s an important answer, and here’s why. Brown isn’t saying Bridges is the problem. He’s saying other guys have earned minutes. That’s different. It’s the same message he gave Jose Alvarado and Tyler Kolek a couple of weeks ago: minutes are earned, and change can happen at any time. And look at the evidence. Kolek has taken over a lot of the backup point guard minutes. Alvarado didn’t play against Cleveland. Jordan Clarkson didn’t play either. Brown is riding the hot hands, and right now, the hot hands are Shamet and McBride. Shamet had twenty-two against Brooklyn and has been one of the best shooters on the team. McBride had a plus-minus of plus thirty-nine against Milwaukee and hit shots again against Cleveland.

And notice that Brown included Josh Hart in that answer. Hart just came back after missing three games, two with ankle soreness and one with an illness. So this isn’t about singling out Bridges. It’s about a deep team where good players sometimes play fewer minutes. Brown has a good problem, and he’s handling it the way a good coach should. He’s not protecting anyone’s feelings. He’s not punishing anyone either. He’s just playing the guys who are playing best, and telling everybody else to stay ready.

And let’s talk about the two players who are taking those minutes, because their stories matter too. Landry Shamet re-signed with the Knicks this summer on a four-year deal worth about twenty-four million dollars. That’s a bargain for a player shooting the way he is. He started in Hart’s place during the Chicago, Cleveland and Milwaukee games, and he’s given Brunson and Towns more spacing every time he’s on the floor. He can’t be traded until January fifteenth because of when he re-signed, but honestly, the Knicks wouldn’t want to move him anyway.

Miles McBride is a different story. He’s on an expiring contract worth about four million dollars. His agent, Mark Bartelstein, said in October that there may be opportunities during the season. And Hart said at media day that he didn’t think an extension would get done, and warned that things could change by February. So McBride is playing for his next contract, and he’s playing like it. Plus thirty-nine against Milwaukee. Big shots against Cleveland. One of the best plus-minus numbers on the team in the first Cleveland game. When a player is in a contract year and playing that well, a coach has to find him minutes. That’s not a knock on Bridges. That’s just basketball, and it’s fair.

Now, voice number three: the columnist.

The trade proposal getting passed around is the one from Grant Hughes at Bleacher Report, picked up by The Sporting News and others. The idea is Bridges and two twenty twenty-seven second-round picks to the Los Angeles Clippers, for Max Strus and Derrick Jones Jr. We covered this one in an earlier video, so I’ll keep it short. Hughes’ argument is that Bridges is durable and playoff-tested, but his contract takes up a big piece of the salary cap, and moving him would be the cleanest way to create flexibility to keep the rest of the core, especially Karl-Anthony Towns. And Hughes says that if Bridges doesn’t come out of his slump, you could argue Strus and Jones would actually make the Knicks better in the short term.

Here’s what’s fair about that. The money pressure is real. Towns turned down a four-year, two-hundred-million-dollar extension offer, according to Stefan Bondy of the New York Post, and he has a sixty-one million dollar player option for next season. Miles McBride is on an expiring contract. Josh Hart has a team option. Bridges’ salary goes from about thirty-three and a half million this season to about thirty-six point two million next season, and keeps rising every year after that. ESPN’s Bobby Marks projected that if the Knicks pick up the options for Towns, Hart and Kolek, they’d be only about fourteen point seven million dollars under the second apron next season. So yes, the Knicks will have to make hard choices.

But here’s what’s not fair about it. Hughes’ idea is a columnist’s proposal, not a report. There’s no sign the Knicks are shopping Bridges. And it would mean selling at the absolute lowest point of his career, attaching picks to do it, and breaking up a championship core in the middle of a title defense. An anonymous executive told Heavy recently that the Knicks probably wouldn’t even get one first-round pick back for Bridges right now. When the market says you’d get almost nothing, that’s the strongest argument for waiting.

So now, my voice.

I’m not worried. Not yet. And I want to explain why, because it’s not just loyalty. There’s real evidence, and it comes from the last time we saw Bridges in a slump like this one.

Look at what Bridges did last season. He had a seven-game stretch where he shot about thirty-one percent from the field and twenty-three percent from three. Then he followed it with ten games shooting about fifty-three percent. In the playoffs, after a scoreless Game Three against Atlanta, he shot about fifty-eight percent from the field and thirty-nine percent from three over the next sixteen games. He started all nineteen playoff games, averaged thirteen and a half points on almost fifty-six percent shooting, and scored fourteen with four assists in the Finals clincher against San Antonio. That’s who Bridges is when it matters. His value was never about scoring twenty a night. It’s his defense, his ability to guard the other team’s best perimeter player, his versatility, and his durability.

And even in this slump, he’s found ways to help. In the first Cleveland game, he had season highs of six rebounds and two blocks, plus four assists. Against Milwaukee, he had five rebounds and three steals in seventeen minutes. The jump shot isn’t there. The effort and the defense mostly are.

I also think part of this is about role. The Knicks have more players involved in the offense this season. OG Anunoby is playing more aggressively and taking more shots. Brunson and Towns are carrying the scoring. Shamet and McBride are getting more looks off the bench. Bridges is the kind of player who finds his rhythm with volume, and he’s not getting much volume right now. That can make a slump feel even worse, because you only get a few chances a night to break out of it.

And there’s one more thing worth thinking about. Bridges has been one of the most durable players in the history of the league. At the end of last season, he had the NBA’s longest active streak of consecutive games played, more than six hundred thirty in a row. He’s never missed a game. After a run to the Finals and a short summer, maybe all those minutes are catching up with him, physically and mentally. Josh Hart said it himself after the Brooklyn game: after a title run, sometimes your body has to catch up. That might apply to Bridges more than anyone.

So how does Bridges break out of this? Here’s what I’d want to see. First, get to the rim. When a shooter is cold, the easiest way to warm up is to get a couple of layups and free throws. He went most of the first two weeks without a free throw attempt. That has to change. Second, run. Bridges has always been great in transition, and easy baskets in the open floor can rebuild confidence fast. Third, keep doing the dirty work. Six rebounds and two blocks in one Cleveland game, five rebounds and three steals against Milwaukee. That’s what keeps him on the floor while the shot comes back. And fourth, keep shooting. He’s a career thirty-seven percent three-point shooter. The worst thing a shooter can do in a slump is stop shooting. The law of averages is on his side.

There’s also a mental side. The original video suggested he might be in his own head right now, and that’s probably true. When every missed shot gets turned into a trade rumor, it’s hard not to think about it. That’s why what Brunson said matters so much. When your captain stands up in front of the media and says the locker room has your back, that’s the kind of support that can help a player relax and play free again.

So should the Knicks bench him? I don’t think so, and I’m with the original video on this one. Sending him to the bench wouldn’t fix his shot. It would just add one more thing to his mind and shake up a starting group that won a title. What Brown is doing now actually makes sense. Bridges starts. If he’s not rolling, the closing minutes go to whoever is playing well, whether that’s Shamet, McBride or someone else. That’s how you protect the team without humiliating a player.

Here’s another way to think about it. With Hart back, the Knicks’ wing rotation gets even more crowded. Hart, Anunoby, Bridges, Shamet, McBride, plus Clarkson, plus Mohamed Diawara, who’s been earning minutes too. Brown can’t play all of them thirty minutes. Somebody is going to play less every night. Right now, that’s Bridges, because he’s the one who’s struggling. But that can change quickly. If Bridges has two or three good games, he’ll get those minutes back, and someone else will sit. That’s how a deep team works. It’s not a demotion. It’s a competition, and Bridges has won that competition many times before.

And should they trade him? Not now. Ask me again in January. If he’s still shooting under thirty percent from three after thirty or forty games, the conversation changes. If he bounces back the way he did last season, nobody will remember this stretch. The trade deadline isn’t until February eleventh. There’s no reason to make a decision in November. The best teams make their biggest decisions with the most information, not the least.

Now, a couple of quick notes on the Cleveland loss itself, since the original video touched on them. The video’s host questioned why Jordan Clarkson didn’t play, and why Andre Drummond stayed on the floor so deep into the fourth quarter before Towns came back in. Those are fair questions. Brown has been riding hot hands, and sometimes that means a player like Clarkson sits for a night even though he was great earlier in the season. It also showed that Kolek has taken over a lot of the backup point guard work, with Alvarado sitting again. These are the kinds of decisions you can debate. But they’re also the decisions of a coach with too many good players, not too few.

And let me add one more thing about that Cleveland loss, because it’s worth keeping in perspective. The Knicks had just won four straight, including three blowouts. The Cavaliers were coming off a loss to the Knicks a week earlier, and good teams usually respond in a rematch. Brown said it was one of the team’s worst defensive games in a while. That’s actually a good sign in a way. It means the standard has gone up. Two weeks ago, after the oh and three start, a bad defensive game was normal. Now it stands out. And the Knicks are still six and five, with six wins in their last eight games.

And there’s a bigger-picture point about how the Knicks are built. A lot of teams in the league depend on one or two stars, and when one of them slumps, the whole team falls apart. The Knicks are different. Their fifth starter can go through the worst stretch of his career, and they can still win six of eight. That’s a credit to Brunson and Towns, who have been carrying the load, and to a bench that keeps producing. It also means the Knicks can afford to be patient with Bridges in a way most teams couldn’t. Patience is a luxury, and right now, the Knicks have it.

So let’s put the three voices together.

The captain says the locker room has Bridges’ back, and that he’s their brother.

The coach says Bridges isn’t the problem, but other players have earned minutes, and he’s going to keep playing whoever is playing best.

The columnist says the Knicks could trade him for flexibility, but that’s an idea, not a report, and it would mean selling low.

And my take is simple. The slump is real. The panic isn’t justified yet. Give him time, keep holding him accountable, and let the minutes follow the performance, which is exactly what Brown is doing.
