Let me start with what didn't happen, because a lot of videos this weekend are saying the opposite. There was no trade. Nobody confirmed a massive deal. The Knicks spent a week trying to move Pacôme Dadiet or Tyler Kolek, they offered second-round picks, and they couldn't find a partner. So on Saturday, they made the decision we spent the last week talking about. They waived Bruce Brown.

What they did do is two smaller moves that tell you a lot about how this front office thinks. James Wiseman is expected to take the final roster spot. And a twenty-year-old the Knicks developed last season, Dink Pate, is back in the organization after leaving Providence College. They signed him to an Exhibit 10 deal and waived him so he can go back to Westchester.

Welcome back to Ketuil KNICKS. Today: why the trade didn't happen, what Bruce Brown's exit tells us about the apron, who Dink Pate is and why the Exhibit 10 move is smart, and the opening-night roster heading into Tuesday's banner night against Philadelphia.

Let's start with Bruce Brown, because that's the decision that matters most this season.

Here's how it played out. All week, Ian Begley of SNY reported that there was "strong support" inside the organization for keeping Brown, and that the Knicks would need a trade by Monday afternoon to do it. Brett Siegel of ClutchPoints reported active talks with at least two teams about Dadiet. And the Knicks had the currency: eleven second-round picks over the next seven drafts. But on Saturday, Stefan Bondy of the Post reported that no trade had been found, and the Knicks waived Brown.

Why couldn't a team with eleven second-round picks give away a three-million-dollar contract? We talked about this in the last video, and it played out exactly as feared. To take Dadiet or Kolek without sending salary back, a team needs cap room or a trade exception, and it needs an open roster spot, on the exact weekend when every team in the league is cutting players. Other teams were also trying to attach second-round picks to get through their own roster crunches. So the few teams with room could be picky, and apparently none of them wanted Dadiet or Kolek badly enough to take them, even with picks attached. Bondy's colleague at the Post noted that a team would need draft capital to take on Dadiet's contract, and that Kolek has little value around the league. That's a blunt assessment, and the market seems to agree.

I want to own something here. In the last video, I said that if it came down to Kolek or Bruce Brown, I'd make the trade, reluctantly. The Knicks didn't get the chance, because nobody would take either player on terms they were willing to accept. So the decision wasn't really "Brown or Kolek." It was "Brown or the second apron." And ownership chose the apron.

Let me be fair to Bruce Brown, because he handled this with a lot of class. In three preseason games, he had eleven points, five rebounds and three assists in thirty-eight minutes, with one start. Not much of a stat line, but he wasn't brought in for stats. He was brought in for defense, toughness and experience. And he was honest about what happened to him. Speaking about the lack of interest in free agency, he said, "I was very surprised." And about the second apron's effect on players like him, he said, "It kind of sucks for us." That's a thirty-year-old champion who played all eighty-two games last season, and who'll now be looking for his seventh NBA team.

He's a free agent now. Once he clears waivers, which is the likely outcome for a non-guaranteed camp contract, he can sign anywhere. There was some talk that the Knicks could make a trade after the season starts and then bring Brown back. That's technically possible, but there's no reporting that any deal is close, and Brown has no reason to wait. If a team calls with a guaranteed contract this week, I'd expect him to take it. And honestly, I hope he does.

Where could he land? Over the summer, there were reports of interest from the Lakers, and Denver had an open roster spot before it went quiet. With cuts happening across the league this weekend, some teams will lose a wing to injury in the first few weeks, and a veteran who can defend three positions on a minimum deal is exactly who they'll call. He also has a story every front office knows: he played every game last season and was a key part of a championship rotation. My guess is that Bruce Brown is on a roster within a few weeks.

Now, what does the decision tell us?

A Knicks On SI piece made a good point. The Knicks effectively chose upside over experience at the bottom of the roster. Keeping Dadiet, who's twenty-one, and Mohamed Diawara, who's young and still developing, instead of Brown, who's thirty, tells you the Knicks still care about the development pipeline, even in a title defense. It also suggests they're comfortable keeping Deuce McBride rather than shopping him, and that Kolek may stay as a cheap trade piece if the Knicks need to add a veteran after an injury. All of that is analysis, not reporting. But it fits the pattern.

And the bigger pattern is the one we've been describing for months. The second apron is a hard line for this ownership. They let Mitchell Robinson leave for Boston. They signed Drummond and Clarkson at minimum numbers. They paused every extension negotiation. And now they've let go of a camp player the coaching staff clearly wanted, because keeping him would have cost them the apron. You can disagree with that philosophy. Plenty of fans do. But nobody can say it's inconsistent.

Is it the right call? Let me give you both sides.

The case that the Knicks got it wrong: the bench defense was the biggest problem of the preseason. Thirty-nine fast-break points in Philadelphia. Eighteen threes from Minnesota. Thirty fast-break points in one half in Toronto. Bruce Brown is a direct answer to that. And a title defense is about the next eight months, not Dadiet's development in 2028.

The case that they got it right: Brown would have been the eleventh or twelfth man most nights. The players who'll actually decide those bench minutes are McBride, Alvarado, Shamet, Clarkson and Diawara. If the bench defense is going to improve, it has to come from them, not from a player who'd rarely see the floor when everyone's healthy. And keeping young assets matters for a team that can't add through free agency. Every cheap young player who develops is one fewer thing the Knicks have to pay for later.

My view? I'd have kept Brown if a trade had been possible at a fair price. It wasn't. Given that, I understand the decision, even though I think the Knicks will miss his toughness at some point this season. And if the bench defense struggles in November, this is the decision people will point to.

And what about Tyler Kolek? On this channel, I've called him a real backup point guard. The trade market this week disagreed, at least at the price the Knicks were offering. That doesn't change what he is on the court. But it does change his situation. He stays, he's the fifth guard on most nights, and he'll probably have stretches of DNPs. For a player like that, the best thing that can happen is an injury opening minutes, or a trade later in the season to a team that needs a point guard. The Knicks will keep his cheap contract and his team option for 2027-28 as a trade chip, and they'll revisit it around the deadline. For Kolek, the message is the same as it is for everyone outside the starting five: Mike Brown said every day is a competition, and that hasn't changed.

Now James Wiseman, quickly, because we've covered him in detail. With Brown gone, Wiseman is expected to sign for the fourteenth spot. Over four preseason games, he had thirty-two points, seventeen rebounds and three blocks in forty-six minutes, headlined by the eighteen-and-ten night in Toronto. Mike Brown summed him up this week in five words: "He's young, he's talented, he's raw, still." That's exactly right. He'll be the third center behind Towns and Drummond.

And here's a detail from the Post that matters. Wiseman is filling the third-center role that Ariel Hukporti held last season, and Hukporti, who signed with Philadelphia this summer, is out for the season. I mentioned Hukporti in my last video as being on the Sixers' bench for opening night. If he's out for the year, he won't be in uniform. That's a rough outcome for a player who was part of a title team.

The other thing to watch is Wiseman's contract. Reports haven't said yet whether it's guaranteed. If it's non-guaranteed until the league-wide guarantee date in early January, the Knicks have a built-in exit if his body doesn't hold up. If it's guaranteed, that's a big statement of trust.

Now, Dink Pate, because I think this is the most interesting move of the weekend, even though it's the smallest.

Dink Pate is twenty. He was born in March of 2006, he's from Dallas, and he's six-eight with a long wingspan. He has one of the most unusual paths in basketball. In April 2023, at seventeen, he signed with the G League Ignite, which reportedly made him the youngest known professional basketball player in U.S. history. Ignite shut down the following spring. He played the 2024-25 season with the Mexico City Capitanes, averaging about ten points a game, and went undrafted in 2025. Then the Knicks signed him and sent him to Westchester.

And last season in Westchester, he took a real step. In thirty-six regular-season games, he averaged sixteen points, six point seven rebounds and three point six assists, shooting about forty-two percent from the field and almost thirty-seven percent from three. Counting the Tip-Off Tournament, it was fifty games at about sixteen points, with his three-point percentage a bit lower, in the mid-thirties. Either way, the progress is the story. Earlier in his career, shooting was his biggest weakness, with his three-point percentage reportedly in the high twenties. In Westchester, he became a genuine scoring threat.

So why did he leave? This is where it gets complicated. Last January, Pate turned down two-way and ten-day contract offers, reportedly including interest from the Knicks, because he wanted to keep his college eligibility. He'd only played on an Exhibit 10 deal and in the G League, so he believed he could still be eligible. He committed to Providence. But the NCAA never cleared him. According to the Providence Journal, his case went on through the summer and into the fall, and he had to sit out a closed scrimmage. There was an NCAA statement that would bar players who'd signed NBA contracts, including two-way and ten-day deals, and Pate's camp was arguing that it shouldn't apply to him. Two of his Providence teammates have even filed antitrust lawsuits against the NCAA. Pate decided he couldn't keep waiting with no guarantee, and as Kevin McNamara wrote, he didn't want to risk being denied with no professional option to fall back on. So he withdrew and came back. Providence's coach, Bryan Hodgson, said, "Dink Pate will always be a Friar, and we fully support his decision."

Now, why sign him and immediately waive him? This is the part that confuses a lot of fans, so let me explain how Exhibit 10 deals work.

An Exhibit 10 is a non-guaranteed, one-year minimum contract for camp, built specifically for players headed to the G League. Here's the key. If a team signs a player to an Exhibit 10 and then waives him before the season, and that player then joins the team's G League affiliate and stays there for a set period, he earns a bonus worth tens of thousands of dollars. And the NBA team keeps his G League rights as an affiliate player. So signing and waiving Pate isn't a contradiction. It's the standard path. It pays him a bonus, puts him back in Westchester, and keeps him in the Knicks' system rather than letting another G League team grab him.

It's also worth comparing this to the camp deals the Knicks gave the veterans. Wiseman, Bruce Brown, Eubanks, Bradley, Agbaji and Konchar all got Exhibit 9 deals, which are camp contracts with limited injury protection for the team. Exhibit 9s don't come with a G League bonus or affiliate rights. That's because those players were competing for an NBA roster spot, not a G League one. Pate's Exhibit 10 tells you clearly what the plan is: Westchester first, and then we'll see. Teams can hold the G League rights to a small number of players this way each season, so using one of those spots on Pate is a sign the Knicks still see something in him.

An Exhibit 10 can also be converted into a two-way contract before the season, but the Knicks' three two-way slots are already full: Kevin McCullar, Tyler Nickel and Jaden Akins. So for now, Pate is a Westchester player.

But here's why it matters. Two-way contracts aren't permanent. If one of the Knicks' two-way players struggles or gets hurt this season, the Knicks can waive him and give that slot to someone else. And Pate, after one more productive stretch in Westchester, would be an obvious candidate. A twenty-year-old, six-eight wing who can handle the ball, rebound and score is exactly the kind of player a second-apron team needs to develop cheaply, because two-way contracts don't count against the cap or the apron.

That's the connection to Bruce Brown. The Knicks couldn't afford a thirty-year-old champion on a minimum deal. But they can afford a twenty-year-old prospect on a G League deal, and maybe a two-way contract later. When you're a second-apron team, that's how you build. You can't buy depth in free agency. You have to grow it. Jaden Akins, undrafted and now on a two-way, is another example. Pate could be next.

Let me add the honest caution. Pate is a prospect, not a guaranteed NBA player. His defense and decision-making are still developing, and the jump from sixteen points in the G League to contributing on a title team is enormous. A lot of G League scorers never make it. His fifty-game sample also showed a lower three-point percentage than his regular-season number, which is a reminder that the shooting gains are real but still fragile. So don't expect him in the Knicks' rotation this season. Expect him to be a name worth tracking in Westchester, and a candidate for a two-way spot if one opens.

So here's the opening-night roster, as it looks heading into Tuesday.

The starters: Jalen Brunson, Mikal Bridges, OG Anunoby, Karl-Anthony Towns and Josh Hart. The guards behind them: Deuce McBride, Jose Alvarado, Landry Shamet, Jordan Clarkson and Tyler Kolek. On the wing: Mohamed Diawara and Pacôme Dadiet. At center: Andre Drummond and James Wiseman. That's fourteen standard contracts, right under the second apron, with no fifteenth man. Plus the three two-way players, McCullar, Nickel and Akins, each eligible for up to fifty regular-season games.

That's a deep guard group and a thin frontcourt. Notice the shape of it. Five guards behind Brunson, two young wings, and two centers behind Towns. If a big man gets hurt, the Knicks will be leaning on small-ball lineups and Wiseman's health. If a wing gets hurt, Diawara and Dadiet will get real minutes. And if the bench defense doesn't improve, the Knicks don't have a Bruce Brown to call on. The margin for error is thin, and the front office has accepted that.

And remember the calendar. On December 15th, players signed this summer, Clarkson and Drummond among them, become tradable, which gives the Knicks more ways to match salary. Shams has already flagged the February deadline as a point where things could heat up if nothing changes on Towns and Hart. And the January guarantee date is when the Knicks will have to decide on Wiseman if his deal isn't guaranteed. So this roster isn't final for the season. It's final for opening night.

Sportsbooks have the Knicks as roughly a two-to-one favorite. But emotional nights can go either way, and Mike Brown has spent the whole preseason telling his team that the scoreboard isn't the point. Watch three things. How the Knicks start after the ceremony. Whether Wiseman checks in during the first half. And whether Towns plays any minutes at the four, which Brown hinted at this month.

Let me close with the title of the video going around. "Massive trade confirmed." No. The Knicks tried to make a small trade, couldn't, and let a good veteran go because of the apron. They brought back a promising twenty-year-old on a G League path. And they're about to start a title defense with fourteen players and almost no room for error. That's the real story, and I think it's a more interesting one.
