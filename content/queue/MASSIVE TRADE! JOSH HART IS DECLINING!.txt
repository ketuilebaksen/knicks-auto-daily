The title says massive trade, and that Josh Hart is declining. Let’s sort that out right away. There’s no trade. Nobody has traded Josh Hart, and nobody has reported talks. What we do have is a tough stretch for Hart, a worried column from Daily Knicks, a fan’s trade idea on a Lakers podcast, a column about Dejounte Murray, some brutal clutch numbers, and a debate about Mike Brown’s lineups. That’s five real conversations. So let’s go through all five, and let’s be honest about each one. Some of these worries are fair. Some of them go too far. And the record right now, six and six, is a good reason to take all of it seriously without panicking.

Story one: Josh Hart. Is he hurt, or is he declining?

Let’s start with the numbers, because they’re not good. On Friday, Hart scored zero points in twenty-three minutes. He went oh for four from the field and oh for three from three, and he was minus eleven. And this time, it was Hart who sat in the fourth quarter, not Bridges. Through nine appearances this season, he’s averaging about seven point three points, six point seven rebounds and three point eight assists in about thirty minutes a game. That’s close to five points a game less than last season in about the same minutes. He’s shooting about thirty-six percent from the field, about thirty percent from three, and sixty percent from the free-throw line. Daily Knicks pointed out that since the first three games, he hasn’t had a single double-double, and he hasn’t scored more than eight points in any of his last six appearances.

And the detail that worried the writer the most was the fast breaks. Daily Knicks said that when Hart gets a one-man fast break, it almost always used to end in a basket or a foul. This season, some of those have ended in strips or blocks. That’s the kind of thing that makes people wonder whether he’s lost a step.

But here’s the other side, and it’s a big one. Hart has been hurt. He said a calf injury kept him out for about three weeks in September. Then he hurt his right ankle in training camp, and he aggravated it during the season. He’s already missed three games, two with ankle soreness and one with an illness. Before the season, we also knew he was playing through a finger injury on his right hand that he’s said can only really be fixed with a career-ending surgery. That’s a lot for one body to handle in the first month of a season.

And then there’s the workload. Daily Knicks noted that since Hart’s first full season with the Knicks in twenty twenty-three twenty-four, only two players have logged more total minutes than him: Mikal Bridges and Jalen Brunson. He played into the middle of June last season. He turns thirty-two in March. Hart said it himself after the Brooklyn game: after a title run, sometimes your body has to catch up.

It’s also worth remembering how good Hart was last season, because that’s the bar people are comparing him to. He shot a career-best forty-one point three percent from three. He was a constant triple-double threat. He guarded bigger players, he rebounded like a big man at six-four, and he closed games for a championship team. When a player sets a standard like that, even a normal slump looks like a collapse. And when that slump comes with a sore ankle, a calf injury and a bad finger, it’s hard to separate the injuries from the age.

So what’s the honest answer? I think it’s mostly the injuries and the workload, at least for now. A player doesn’t suddenly fall off a cliff over one summer, especially a player whose game is built on effort and toughness. But I do think the question is fair, and the answer will be clearer in a month. If the ankle heals and his legs come back, so will the fast breaks. If they don’t, the Knicks have a real problem. Daily Knicks said the Knicks won’t win a title with this version of Hart, and I agree with that part. They need the real Josh Hart back.

So what should the Knicks do with Hart right now? I’d start with his minutes. He’s playing about thirty minutes a night while dealing with an ankle that keeps flaring up. With Shamet and McBride playing so well, Brown has a real chance to bring that number down closer to the mid-twenties for a few weeks. Let the ankle heal. Let the legs come back. A healthy Hart in April is worth a lot more than a tired Hart in November. And the Knicks have the depth to do it. That’s the whole point of having a bench this good.

There’s also everything Hart brings that doesn’t show up in the box score. He’s one of the voices in that locker room. He’s the one who talked openly about the fatigue after the Philadelphia loss, and about getting the team’s championship habits back after the Brooklyn win. Players like that matter during a slow start. Even when he’s not scoring, he’s setting the tone for how this team is supposed to compete.

Story two: the Lakers trade idea.

This one needs a big asterisk. A listener of a Lakers podcast hosted by Jovan Buha proposed a trade: Josh Hart to the Lakers, and the Knicks get Jaden Hardy, Kevon Looney, Jake LaRavia and three second-round picks. Buha said he’d do it from the Lakers’ side. He called LaRavia a poor man’s Josh Hart. But he also asked the obvious question: why would New York do that? He said maybe they save some money, and it’s a cheaper alternative to dumping Towns or Bridges. And then he said this: if the Knicks put Hart on the market, they could probably get more than LaRavia and three seconds.

So let’s be clear about what this is. It’s a fan’s idea on a podcast. Heavy wrote it up. That’s it. It’s not a report, and it’s not a proposal from either team.

And there’s a big problem with it that nobody mentioned: the roster. The Knicks have fourteen standard contracts. If they trade one player and get three back, they’d be at sixteen. The league maximum is fifteen. So the Knicks would have to waive somebody right away just to make the deal legal. And because they’re above the first apron, the three incoming players would have to make less, combined, than Hart’s twenty point nine million. That’s possible, but it’s a lot of pieces moving for three role players.

The bigger point the video makes is fair, though. Hart’s contract, with a team option of about twenty-two point four million for next season, makes him movable. And the Knicks do have real money pressure. Towns has a sixty-one million dollar player option. Bridges’ salary keeps rising. Brunson’s goes up too. If Hart really is declining, his contract becomes harder to justify next summer. But I’d say this: you don’t sell a player like Hart at his lowest point for a package even the other team’s reporter says isn’t enough. If the Knicks ever move Hart, it should be for real value.

Story three: Dejounte Murray as an escape route for Bridges.

According to Sportsnaut, Dejounte Murray has asked for a trade from New Orleans, and the Pelicans are four and nine. The column argued that Murray could be an escape route from Bridges’ contract. Let me give you the facts the video used.

Bridges is averaging about six point nine points a game, and that’s after an eighteen-point game on Friday. That was his best scoring game of the season, and it’s worth celebrating. He’s shooting about thirty-seven percent from the field and about twenty-nine percent from three. And he’s owed more than one hundred fifteen million dollars over the next three seasons, including a forty-one and a half million dollar player option in twenty twenty-nine thirty.

The column’s case for Murray: he’s a better rebounder and ball handler, with about five point eight rebounds and five point four assists a game for his career. He’s a better scorer. And the video says his contract lines up closely with Bridges’ salary, and that he has about a thirty million dollar player option next season that he’d probably decline. I’d treat those contract details as the video’s version. If they’re right, a Murray deal would give the Knicks more flexibility next summer than keeping Bridges.

But there are big risks. Murray ruptured his Achilles last season. He hasn’t been the player who made the All-Star team in twenty twenty-two. And the column itself mentioned concerns about the locker room. So the Knicks would be betting on a guard coming back from one of the hardest injuries in sports, while giving up their best perimeter defender, who’s also part of a championship core. That’s a big bet, and it’s a bet the Knicks don’t need to make in November.

And let’s remember who has spoken up for Bridges recently. Jalen Brunson said the locker room has his back, and said, that’s our brother. Kenny Atkinson pointed to the Knicks’ continuity, the three players who played together at Villanova, as a reason they’re so tough to beat. Bridges started all nineteen playoff games last season, shot about fifty-six percent, and scored fourteen with four assists in the Finals clincher. The eighteen-point game on Friday could be the first sign he’s coming out of it. I wouldn’t trade him for Murray now. But it’s the most realistic Bridges idea we’ve seen, because the salaries are close and both teams would be betting on a bounce-back.

Story four: the clutch problem.

This is the stat that hurts the most. According to Posting and Toasting, the Knicks are one and six in clutch games this season. That’s games within five points in the last five minutes. They have a net rating of about minus seventeen in those minutes, around twenty-second in the league. Their turnover rate and assist rate are about league average. The rebounding goes from dominant to just okay. But the real problem is shooting. They have a true shooting percentage of about fifty point seven percent in the clutch, around twenty-third in the league. And if you take out the comeback win in Chicago, the numbers get even uglier: about minus forty in net rating, an offensive rating around eighty-three, and a true shooting percentage around forty-four.

And the article went through every loss. In the opener against Philadelphia, a costly Anunoby turnover and an easy Maxey layup. In Boston, Towns’ moving screen while down three late. In Orlando, a long stretch of empty possessions with bad shooting and turnovers. In the second Philadelphia game, LeBron’s tip-in off an offensive rebound with about twelve seconds left. In Cleveland, the Cavs scoring almost every trip when the Knicks blitzed Donovan Mitchell. And against Indiana, a post touch for Towns down two with two point eight seconds left that didn’t go.

The critique that stood out to me was this. The writer said the Knicks have relied on too much isolation in late-game offense for years. And he wished they’d use Towns’ passing to free up a shooter. That’s a fair point. Last season, the Knicks were one of the best clutch teams in the league, twenty-one and thirteen. You could almost relax in a close game. This season, they’ve lost six of seven.

And here’s a detail that matters: a lot of those late-game problems came from players who are usually reliable. Towns, Anunoby, Brunson. These aren’t players who suddenly forgot how to play in big moments. Brunson was the Finals MVP. Anunoby made huge plays all through the playoffs last season. That’s one reason I think this is more about execution and play design than about the players themselves. When your best players are getting stuck in isolation against loaded defenses, the answer usually isn’t a trade. It’s a better plan.

Is it fixable? I think so. Seven games is a small sample, and a lot of these losses came down to one or two plays. But the pattern is real. The fix probably looks like what helped the Knicks win in Cleveland the first time: more ball movement, more of the Nova motion offense, fewer turnovers, and more trust in the passing of Towns and the shooting of Shamet and McBride. Late-game isolation works when Brunson is at his best, and it won them a lot of games last season. When the defense loads up on him, the Knicks need a second option.

And about Bridges’ eighteen-point game on Friday. That’s a big deal, even in a loss. It was his best scoring night of the season by a lot. If you’re looking for signs that he’s starting to come out of the slump, that’s the first real one. One game doesn’t fix everything. But remember what happened last season: a terrible stretch, then ten straight games shooting about fifty-three percent. If Friday was the start of something like that, the whole Bridges trade conversation could disappear in a couple of weeks.

Story five: Mike Brown’s experiments.

Daily Knicks argued that the Knicks can’t afford Brown’s experimental lineups much longer. Against Indiana, Brown played Pacôme Dadiet in the first quarter of an NBA Cup game. He’s also played Tyler Kolek and Brunson together in very small lineups, with McBride or Shamet as the nominal power forward.

The column cited research suggesting that a team’s record starts to say a lot about it early. One study found records start to become telling after about eight games. Another found records are about fifty percent indicative by twelve games and about seventy percent by twenty-four. The Knicks are six and six at the twelve-game mark. The column compared that to last season, when the Knicks were five and three after eight games with a strong net rating and finished with fifty-three wins. And it warned that even if the Knicks outperform their current pace by a few wins, they might finish around forty-six, which might not be enough for home-court advantage in the first round.

Here’s my take. I understand the concern, and I think Brown should lean more toward winning now. But I don’t think every experiment is a mistake. Trying Brunson and Kolek together in November is exactly what the regular season is for. Giving young players like Dadiet a look helps you know what you have before the deadline. And Brown has made some good calls this season too. Shamet starting while Hart was out worked. Riding McBride’s hot hand worked. Naming Drummond the defensive player of the game in Cleveland was a great message. The balance is the key. Experiment in the first quarter of a game in November, sure. But in the fourth quarter of a close game, go with what you trust.

And let’s keep the record in perspective, because six and six sounds worse than it feels when you look at how the Knicks got there. They started oh and three. Then they won six of their next eight. They beat Cleveland on the road, beat Chicago by thirty, and beat Milwaukee by thirty-two. Then they lost the rematch to Cleveland and a close Cup game to Indiana. Most of their losses have been close games that came down to the last few possessions. That’s why the clutch numbers matter so much. Flip two or three of those finishes, and the Knicks are eight and four or nine and three, and nobody is talking about decline. That doesn’t excuse the losses. But it tells you where the problem really is.

The East is also crowded right now. Boston, Philadelphia, Detroit, Toronto, Cleveland and Miami are all in the mix. A forty-six-win pace would be a disappointment for this team, but there’s a lot of season left. The Knicks don’t need to panic. They need to win the close ones again.

So let’s put it all together with my verdicts.

On Hart: it’s probably mostly the injuries and the workload, but it’s a fair concern. The next month will tell us a lot.

On the Lakers idea: it’s a fan’s podcast pitch, it would put the Knicks over the roster limit, and even the Lakers reporter said the Knicks could get more. I’d pass.

On Murray: it’s the most realistic Bridges idea yet, but the Achilles risk is huge, and Bridges just had his best game of the season. I’d wait.

On the clutch: one and six is a real problem, but it’s fixable with better late-game offense and less isolation.

On Brown: experiment early, coach to win late. That’s the balance.

And the video’s big prediction is that the Knicks will make a trade before the deadline, and it’ll involve Bridges or Hart. I don’t think that’s a crazy prediction. The money is tight, and the play has been inconsistent. But I’d lean the other way. With only one tradable first-round pick, about eight hundred nine thousand dollars of room under the second apron, and a championship core that’s already been through slumps before, I think the more likely move is a smaller one, for a backup center, using pieces like McBride, Kolek or Dadiet. Breaking up the Villanova core in the middle of a title defense would be the last resort, not the first move.
