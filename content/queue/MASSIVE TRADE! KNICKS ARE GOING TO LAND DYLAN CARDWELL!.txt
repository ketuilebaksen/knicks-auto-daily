Here's the problem. Scott Perry isn't a Knicks executive. He used to be. He was the Knicks' general manager from 2017 to 2023. Today, he's the general manager of the Sacramento Kings. So the video took a true story about the Kings and turned it upside down. The "reservation" in its running joke wasn't for the Knicks. It was Sacramento saving a seat for its own player.

And you can see how the mistake, or the trick, happened. Perry's name is familiar to Knicks fans. He was the front-office executive who helped run the team in the years before Leon Rose took over and then worked alongside Rose for a few seasons. So when a video says "Knicks executive Scott Perry," a lot of fans nod along. But he left the organization years ago, and Sacramento hired him as general manager in 2025. Any quote from Scott Perry today is a quote from the Kings' front office.

First, the real story.

Dylan Cardwell went undrafted in 2025 after five seasons at Auburn, and signed a two-way contract with the Kings. Two-way players can only be active for a limited number of games, and by February his were almost used up. So when Sacramento made a three-team trade on the first weekend of February, sending Dennis Schröder, Keon Ellis and Dario Šarić out and bringing De'Andre Hunter in, the deal cleared roster spots. Shams himself reported at the time that part of the Kings' motivation was roster flexibility, including converting Cardwell to a standard contract. Days later, the Kings did exactly that.

And around the same time, Kings general manager Scott Perry talked about Cardwell in a way that tells you how much they like him. He compared him to a Hall of Fame center. "I'm not saying that Dylan is Ben Wallace today," Perry said, before explaining what he saw in him. When the GM of a team mentions Ben Wallace about a second-year undrafted big, that's a team that wants to keep the player, not move him.

And look at what that February trade says about Sacramento's direction. They brought in De'Andre Hunter, a three-and-D wing under contract through 2027, as Shams described him. That's not a team tearing everything down. It's a team trying to stay competitive while developing young, cheap players like Cardwell. That matters for this rumor, because a team in that mode doesn't usually sell its best-value young big for a prospect who isn't playing.

Let me also explain why the conversion mattered so much for Sacramento. A two-way contract lets a team keep a young player without counting him against the cap, but he can only be active for a limited number of regular-season games, fifty under the current rules, and he can't play in the playoffs. By February, Cardwell was almost out of games. If the Kings wanted to keep using him, they had to convert him to a standard contract, which meant they needed an open roster spot. So they made one. That's the trade Perry was talking about.

So that's what Scott Perry said and did. A Kings executive, about a Kings trade, to keep a Kings player. The fake video kept the words and swapped the team.

And here's one more reason the original sentence should have set off alarms, even if you didn't know where Perry works. NBA executives don't publicly announce that they've cleared a roster spot for a player on another team. That would be tampering, which the league punishes with fines and sometimes lost draft picks. When a front-office executive talks publicly about a specific player, it's almost always his own player. So a "Knicks executive" saying the Knicks made room for a Kings player would have been a scandal, not a scoop.

Now, the column that started all this, because it's real, and it's a reasonable piece.

On October 21st, Dan Favale of Bleacher Report published a list of every NBA team's top three trade targets. For the Knicks, he named three big men. Number one, Moussa Diabaté in Charlotte. Number two, Dylan Cardwell. Number three, Luka Garza in Boston. That's an opinion column, not a report. Favale is a smart cap writer, and his reasoning is thoughtful, but nothing in it says anyone is talking to anyone.

His case for Cardwell is mostly about contract value and fit. Cardwell's deal covers this season through 2028-29 for a combined total of about seven point four million dollars. And Favale's key argument is that both Diabaté and Cardwell can play alongside Karl-Anthony Towns, which he says the Knicks can't really do with Andre Drummond or James Wiseman. That's a fair point. A mobile big who rebounds and defends in space could let Towns slide to the four for longer stretches, which Mike Brown has hinted he'd like to do.

Now, who is Dylan Cardwell, really? Because he's an interesting player.

He's a six-eleven center from Augusta, Georgia. At Auburn, he played a limited role for five years, mostly as an energy big off the bench. In his rookie season in Sacramento, he played forty-four games, averaging about twenty minutes, with five point four points, seven point five rebounds, one point four assists and one point five blocks. He shot almost fifty-nine percent from the field, mostly around the rim. He's not a shooter. He made about fifty-one percent of his free throws. But he became a fan favorite in Sacramento for his energy, rebounding and interior defense.

And for an undrafted player who barely played in college, that's a remarkable rise in one year. And the contract really is unusual. After the conversion in February, he signed a four-year standard deal: a small amount for the rest of last season, then about two point one five million this season, about two point five three million next season, and about two point seven four million in 2028-29, which is a team option. There's some disagreement in the reporting about how much of next season is guaranteed. The Kings' beat reporter James Ham reported that the last two years were non-guaranteed, while Cardwell's agent told Shams that the first two years were fully guaranteed. Either way, Sacramento has a lot of flexibility with him. But either way, for a rotation-level big, it's one of the cheapest deals in the league.

How would he compare to what the Knicks already have? Drummond is the best rebounder in the group by far, and he can pass, but at thirty-three he's best in short stints and plays mostly in drop coverage. Wiseman is the most athletic, a vertical lob threat, but he's still raw defensively and coming back from years of injuries. Cardwell's game is different from both. He's mobile, he protects the rim, he rebounds, and he doesn't need the ball. That's the profile Favale is pointing at when he says Cardwell could play next to Towns, and it's a profile the Knicks simply don't have on the roster right now. A big who can defend in space and rotate quickly lets Towns guard the other team's power forward, which is the version of Towns-at-the-four that Mike Brown has hinted at.

The weaknesses are real, though. He isn't a shooter, so he wouldn't help the spacing Brunson needs. And a fifty-one percent free-throw shooter is the kind of player opponents might foul late in close games, the same "Hack-a-Mitch" problem the Knicks dealt with in the playoffs. For a backup center playing fifteen to twenty minutes, that's manageable. But it's why he's a role player, not a fix-everything answer.

So, does a Cardwell trade make sense for the Knicks? Let's do the math, because the rules decide it.

The Knicks are above the first apron. They can't take back more salary than they send out. Cardwell makes about two point one five million this season.

Dadiet makes about two point nine eight million. So Dadiet for Cardwell works. The Knicks would take back less than they send. The trade would save them about eight hundred thousand dollars, and their roster would stay at fourteen. It wouldn't open a fifteenth spot, because one player goes out and one comes in. But it would swap a young wing who isn't in the rotation for a big man who rebounds and protects the rim.

Kolek makes about two point three million. That works too, which is interesting. Kolek for Cardwell is a legal one-for-one swap. Remember, Marc Stein reported that the Knicks would move Kolek only for a "coveted" center. Cardwell would at least fit the description in terms of salary.

Compare that with the other names on Favale's list. Diabaté makes about two point four six million, which Dadiet can match, but Kolek can't. And Garza would require looking at his own number with Boston. So of the three, Cardwell is actually the easiest to fit under the Knicks' rules. That part of the idea is solid.

And let me give you a little more on the other two names, since they'll show up in their own fake videos soon enough. Diabaté is in a contract year with Charlotte. His salary this season is about two point four six million, mostly non-guaranteed until January 10th, and he becomes an unrestricted free agent next summer. Favale's point is that a rental is easier to get. Garza is the opposite type. He's a stretch big who shot over forty-three percent from three last season and is one of the league's better offensive rebounders, but he's a weaker defender, and in Boston he's fallen behind Robinson and Queta. He's also heading to free agency. So the three names give you three different versions of the same idea: a mobile defender in Cardwell, a rental rebounder in Diabaté, and a shooter in Garza.

But now look at it from Sacramento's side, and this is where it gets hard.

The Kings made a trade in February partly to keep Cardwell. Their general manager compared him to Ben Wallace. He's twenty-four, cheap, under team control for three more seasons, and productive. Why would they give that up for Dadiet, a twenty-one-year-old who hasn't found an NBA role, or for Kolek, a backup point guard? And if the Knicks don't get a deal like this done before the deadline, there are other routes. January 10th is the league-wide guarantee date, and in the days before it, teams decide which non-guaranteed players to keep. Some useful big men always get released around then. The Knicks can't chase expensive bought-out veterans in February, because as a first-apron team they can't sign a waived player whose salary was above the mid-level exception. But players on small contracts are fair game. So the center hunt has more than one path, and a trade isn't the only one.

So my honest read: Cardwell is exactly the type of player the Knicks should be looking for. Young, cheap, a rebounder and rim protector, on a contract that fits under the second apron.
