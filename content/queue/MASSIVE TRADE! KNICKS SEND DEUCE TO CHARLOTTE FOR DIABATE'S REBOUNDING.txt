Okay. The player.

Moussa Diabaté is twenty-four years old, six-foot-ten, and last season he averaged seven point nine points, eight point seven rebounds, and a block per game.

And I want you to sit with that rebounding number, because it's the entire reason this proposal exists. Eight point seven boards a night from a player who isn't a full-time starter is not normal. That's a player who competes on the glass possession after possession, especially on the offensive end.

We covered Diabaté on this channel a couple of weeks ago, and I said then that he was exactly the swing this roster has been begging for. Nothing has changed my mind. He runs the floor like a wing, he catches lobs, he blocks shots, and he brings back the chaos we lost when Mitchell Robinson left for Boston — the second-chance energy that turns a bad possession into a putback and breaks an opponent's spirit.

He is not a finished product. He isn't going to space the floor, and he won't run offense for you. But that's not the job. Mike Brown's system is one real big surrounded by four players who are a little undersized for their positions. That big has to screen, roll, rebound, protect the rim, and survive a switch. That's Diabaté's whole profile.

Now, why would Charlotte even consider this? This is the part that's changed, and it's worth explaining.

A few weeks ago on this channel, we talked about Charlotte's frontcourt being crowded — they added Naz Reid, they have Ryan Kalkbrenner, and they drafted another big. At the time, the thinking around the league was that Kalkbrenner might be the expendable one, because Charlotte genuinely likes Diabaté.

But crowded is crowded. When you have three bigs and one of them is a rebounding specialist on a rebuilding team that's going nowhere in April, somebody becomes available. And what a young rebuilding roster usually lacks is exactly what New York would be sending back — a proven, tough, two-way guard on a reasonable contract who sets a professional standard in the locker room.

That's a real trade. Both sides are moving from surplus into need. That's not fantasy — that's how actual deals get made.

Which brings us to the hard part. Because this costs Deuce McBride, and I'm not going to wave that away.

Deuce is a genuine fan favorite. He's earned this fan base's love through defense, through clutch shooting, and through the kind of energy that makes you want to run through a wall with him. He's a New York guy. Trading him will sting, and anybody who tells you it won't isn't being honest with you.

And here's what makes it even harder right now — the timing.

Two weeks ago, everybody assumed McBride was gone because the Knicks couldn't afford him. Then the league raised its cap projection, the math changed, and suddenly keeping him became realistic. We covered that here. We even worked out that his next contract likely lands around twelve and a half million, because the market for small, defense-first, low-usage guards is surprisingly cheap.

So this trade isn't the old story of "we can't pay him." It's a different question entirely, and it's a harder one.

Now that the Knicks can keep Deuce McBride — do they still want to?

And here's how I'd frame that decision, because I think it's the cleanest way to think about it.

New York has two real currencies left. Deuce McBride, and one tradable first-round pick in 2033. That's basically it.

There's a center problem to solve. So the question isn't whether you like McBride. Everybody likes McBride. The question is which currency you spend.

Spend the 2033 pick, and you can chase a bigger name — Yves Missi, for example, who New Orleans has already refused multiple times. Spend McBride, and you get a solid young big without touching the last pick this franchise owns.

Put it that way, and this proposal looks a lot smarter. You're trading from a position where you still have depth — the guard and wing rotation survives without him — to fill the one position where you have almost nothing behind Karl-Anthony Towns and Andre Drummond.

That's not breaking up a champion. That's roster construction.

Now let me steelman the other side, because the loudest argument against this trade is a serious one.

It goes like this. You just won a championship. The formula worked. You don't dismantle a winning locker room for a depth upgrade — and a backup center is, at the end of the day, a depth upgrade. McBride plays real minutes in playoff games. Diabaté would play spot minutes in most of them. You're trading a rotation piece for a specialist.

And that argument has teeth. I'd answer it with one word: April.

Because Mike Brown's rotations get very demanding on frontcourt depth in the postseason. Every opponent's plan against this team is to attack Towns, get him in foul trouble, then do the same to Drummond. When that happens in a playoff game, you need somebody who can hold the paint for six minutes without the whole system collapsing.

Right now, that somebody is whoever wins the fourteenth roster spot in camp. That's a lot of faith to put in a camp contract.

And there's a league-wide reason this kind of move keeps coming up, which I think explains the urgency.

Look at what every contender is chasing right now. Frontcourt depth and defensive versatility. Not scoring. Not another shooter. Bigs who can survive a switch and hold the paint.

That's because the modern playoff bracket is full of elite centers, and the team that gets caught thin at that position in a seven-game series gets exposed within two games. Everybody learned that lesson the hard way at some point. This front office clearly learned it too, which is why they've spent the entire offseason chasing center after center, from long shots like Missi to practical ideas like this one.

And here's the part that makes it urgent for us specifically. Our biggest strength is also our biggest vulnerability. Towns is an offensive weapon unlike almost any big in this league. He is also the player opponents will hunt on defense every single night. That combination means the minutes behind him aren't garbage time. They're the minutes that decide whether the system holds up when the plan goes wrong.

Now let me also be honest about what I don't know, because I'd rather tell you that than pretend.

I don't know Diabaté's exact contract situation, and that matters enormously. Part of the appeal of trading an expiring deal for a young big is that you gain control beyond this season. If that control isn't actually there, the math on this gets much less attractive. That's the first thing I'd want confirmed before celebrating.

I also don't know that Charlotte would do a straight swap. If word spreads that they're willing to move him, other teams get involved, and the price climbs. A straight McBride-for-Diabaté deal is one thing. McBride plus a second, or McBride plus real draft capital, is a different conversation.

So here's my bottom line.

If this is a straight swap, I make the call today. It solves the most urgent structural problem on this roster, it costs nothing in draft capital, and it fits the coach's system almost perfectly.

If Charlotte asks for more on top of McBride, I'd slow down — and I'd go back to the same thing I've been saying all month. September is the worst time to buy. In February, every one of these prices looks different.

And if it never happens at all? That's fine too. Because the real value of this proposal isn't the trade itself. It's that it shows you the actual choice in front of this front office. One guard, one draft pick, one hole at center. Something has to be spent.

I'd rather spend the guard than the pick. That's my honest answer, and it's the same answer I'd give if nobody liked Deuce — which, of course, everybody does.
