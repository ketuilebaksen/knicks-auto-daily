Monday night was supposed to be the last stand for Drew Eubanks and Tony Bradley. With James Wiseman sitting, the two veterans got their shot at the Knicks' third center job against one of the biggest front lines in the league. And one of them went five for five.

The Knicks lost to Minnesota a hundred and twenty-two to a hundred and four. That's oh and three in the preseason, and we'll be honest about that, because some of it was ugly. But the final open roster spot is the story with real consequences, and Monday changed the conversation a little. Not as much as the box score suggests, and I'll explain why. The final cuts are about a week away, so let's go through what happened and what it means.

Welcome back to Ketuil KNICKS. Let's start with the setup, because it matters for reading the result.

Going into Monday, James Wiseman had been treated as the favorite for the job. Stefan Bondy of the New York Post said early in camp that Wiseman had impressed in practice and should be seen as the front-runner. Then he backed it up in the opener in Philadelphia, with eight points on three of four shooting, five rebounds, three of them offensive, and a block in about nine minutes. He was the first big off the bench after Towns and Drummond, and he played again against Washington. Across the first two games, he was the only camp center who got real minutes.

Eubanks and Bradley, meanwhile, had almost nothing. Each played about six minutes in Philadelphia. Eubanks had one point, one rebound, a turnover and two fouls. Bradley made his only shot and didn't do much else. Neither got on the floor against Washington. And Ian Begley of SNY had narrowed the race to Eubanks versus Wiseman, with Eubanks as the safer choice because of Wiseman's injury history.

So the plan for Monday, as it was described going in, was simple. Wiseman sits. Eubanks, Bradley and Ochai Agbaji get the minutes. If either of the two veteran centers was going to change the direction of the race, this was the night. And the opponent made it a real test, because Minnesota brought Rudy Gobert and the second-year big man Joan Beringer, who has drawn a lot of attention around the league.

So, what happened?

Tony Bradley played about seven minutes and scored ten points on five of five shooting, with two rebounds. He was the only player in the second unit to reach double figures. If you just look at the line, that's exactly what a guy fighting for a job wants. Five shots, five makes, nothing forced.

Now the context, because that's what the coaching staff will be looking at. Those seven minutes came at the end of the game, when the result was already decided. Most of the recaps describe them as garbage time. He finished minus one in those minutes, so the team didn't really win his stretch, even with him scoring every time he shot. And he only had two rebounds in a game where the Knicks were outrebounded thirty-nine to twenty-eight. The job Bradley is competing for isn't about scoring ten in mop-up minutes. It's about holding the line for six minutes in a real game in February, when Towns is in foul trouble and Drummond is resting. Rebounding, screening, protecting the rim and staying out of foul trouble. We didn't learn much about any of that on Monday.

But I don't want to undersell it either. Tony Bradley has been the forgotten man in this race. He arrived later than the others, on an Exhibit 9 deal, and he's been talked about as a long shot. What he did on Monday is the one thing a long shot can do, which is make it impossible to ignore him. Five for five shows soft hands, good positioning around the rim, and finishing. Those are real skills for a third center whose job will be to catch the ball on the roll and put it in. One of the recaps said he "strengthened his case," and that's fair. He did. He didn't win the job, but he's now part of the discussion.

Drew Eubanks had a quieter night. He played sparing minutes, and his best moments were defensive. He had a block and a steal, and he finished an alley-oop from Jose Alvarado. It was solid, and one of the player grades gave him a B. But solid isn't what he needed on a night when the front-runner was sitting. Eubanks's whole case is that he's the steady, reliable, low-risk option. Monday didn't hurt that case, but it didn't add anything new to it.

And here's some context that's been left out of a lot of coverage. Eubanks is twenty-nine, and he's played more than four hundred NBA games, for San Antonio, Portland, Phoenix, Utah, the Clippers and, last season, Sacramento. He played forty-two games for the Kings before needing season-ending surgery on a torn ligament in his left thumb in March. So when Begley calls him the safer choice, that's what he means. Eubanks has done this job before for a lot of teams, and the coaches know exactly what they'd get.

And then there's Ochai Agbaji, who was also given his turn on Monday. It went badly. Eighteen minutes, zero points, oh for six from three. Agbaji isn't a center, but he matters to this race, and I'll get to why in a minute. For a player whose whole pitch is three-and-D, an oh-for-six night in your one real audition is a tough result.

Now, the roster math, because this is where the third center decision gets more interesting than most people realize.

Right now, the Knicks have thirteen guaranteed contracts. The league requires at least fourteen standard contracts on the roster for the regular season. And the Knicks are about three point two seven million dollars under the second apron, which ownership treats as a hard ceiling. That leaves room for exactly one more player on a veteran minimum contract. One spot. Not two.

So here's the part a lot of coverage misses. That one spot isn't automatically a center. Wiseman, Eubanks and Bradley aren't only competing with each other. They're competing with Bruce Brown, and to a lesser extent Agbaji, for the same roster spot. One SI writer predicting the cuts after two games called Bruce Brown "the winner" and listed Eubanks, Bradley and Agbaji as long shots. In that projection, Wiseman makes it only if a trade opens up a second spot.

And that changes how you read Monday. The coaching staff isn't asking "who is the best of the three camp centers." It's asking "what does this roster need most in its fourteenth spot: a third center, or a versatile, physical guard who can defend?" After a night when Minnesota shot fifty-three percent from three, the case for a perimeter defender gets a little louder. You could argue the Knicks' bigger problem on Monday was on the perimeter, not in the paint.

Is there a way to keep both a center and Bruce Brown? Only through a trade. One idea floating around, from a Knicks Substack, is moving Dadiet, who makes just under three million, to open room for both. I'd treat that as an idea, not a report. Dadiet played well on Monday, he's young, and the Knicks have been patient with him. But it shows how tight this is. Every roster spot on a team at the apron costs something somewhere else.

There's one more detail worth knowing. The camp players are on Exhibit 9 contracts, which mainly protect the team if a player gets hurt in camp. Whoever wins this spot would most likely sign a non-guaranteed deal. Non-guaranteed contracts across the league become fully guaranteed in early January. So whoever gets the fourteenth spot gets about two and a half months to prove it. If it isn't working by January, the Knicks can move on without paying the full season. That's important, because it makes taking a risk a lot cheaper.

And that brings us to the real debate, which is bigger than one preseason game: risk versus reward.

Let me give Eubanks's case first, in its strongest form, because it's a good one. He's twenty-nine. He's played more than four hundred games. He's been a backup center for six different franchises, and he's played in the playoffs. He knows how to set a screen, protect the rim within a system, and avoid hurting you. Last season's thumb injury was a hand problem, not a knee or an Achilles. If your priority is that the third center plays fifteen nights this season without any surprises, Eubanks is the choice. That's Begley's argument, and it's a reasonable one.

Now Wiseman's case. He's twenty-five, four years younger than Eubanks. He's six-eleven, about two hundred forty pounds, and he showed up to camp in noticeably better shape. He was the number two pick in the 2020 draft. And when you look at his career, the problem has rarely been talent. It's been circumstances and health. He went to Golden State as a teenager onto a team chasing titles, a team that couldn't afford to let him make mistakes. He missed the entire 2021-22 season with a knee injury. He was traded to Detroit in February of 2023, where he played on teams that were losing far more than they were winning. Then he signed with Indiana and tore his Achilles on opening night of the 2024-25 season. Since then, he's played only a handful of NBA games. He had a short stint with the Pacers last fall, then a ten-day contract in December.

Put that together and you get a talented big man who's never had a stable place to develop. And the Knicks are probably the most stable environment he's ever been in. They're the defending champions, with a clear structure and two experienced centers in front of him. The third center isn't asked to do everything. Set hard screens, run handoffs, roll to the rim, finish, rebound, and be big on defense. Wiseman can do those things right now. We saw it in Philadelphia.

There's also the Mike Brown connection. Brown was an assistant with the Warriors during Wiseman's first two seasons there. He knows Wiseman's strengths and weaknesses better than anyone else on this staff. That cuts both ways, by the way. If Brown has seen him up close and still wants him, that tells you something. But it also means Brown knows exactly what the problems were.

And the weaknesses are real. Wiseman has never been much of a passer. His defensive instincts haven't matched his physical tools. He gets lost in rotations, and he can be late on help. And the health question isn't trivial. A player coming off an Achilles tear who's played only a handful of games in two seasons is an unknown, no matter how good he looks in October.

Then there's Bradley's case, which looks different after Monday. He's twenty-eight, six-eleven with a seven-five wingspan, a former first-round pick. He sits right in the middle of the other two on both age and risk. He doesn't have Wiseman's ceiling or Eubanks's track record, but he showed on Monday that he can finish around the rim. If the coaches see the third center as a pure catch-and-finish role, Bradley's case got better this week.

And let me remind you why this role matters more than the "third center" label suggests. The Knicks are coming off a long championship run, which means a short summer for Towns and the rest of the starters. Keeping Towns fresh across eighty-two games is a priority, and that means some nights off, some lighter minutes, and some games where foul trouble pulls him early. Drummond is thirty-three and has had health concerns in the past. If either of them misses a stretch, the third center isn't a garbage-time player anymore. He's in the rotation, playing real minutes against real centers, maybe for two or three weeks. That's the scenario the coaches are actually picking for. Not Monday night. A Tuesday in January in Milwaukee or Cleveland, with Drummond out and Towns picking up his third foul in the second quarter.

So here's where I land, and I'll be clear that this is opinion.

If the Knicks decide the fourteenth spot should be a center, I'd still lean toward Wiseman. Monday didn't change that for me. Bradley's five for five came in minutes that told us less than Wiseman's minutes in Philadelphia. And because the contract wouldn't be guaranteed until January, the risk on Wiseman is lower than it looks. If he gets hurt or doesn't work out, the Knicks can still go back to the market, or to one of the veterans if they're still unsigned.

But I'm not sure the fourteenth spot should be a center at all. That's the honest takeaway from Monday. The Knicks have Towns and Drummond. Mike Brown has shown he'll play smaller lineups. And the thing that hurt them most on Monday was perimeter defense, not size. I'd want to see one more game before deciding. If the Knicks get torched from three again by Toronto, Bruce Brown's case gets stronger.
