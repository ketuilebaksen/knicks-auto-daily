"Mike Brown says Karl-Anthony Towns should be grateful for the Knicks." I've seen that headline all week, and I'm going to save you the trouble of looking for the clip. There isn't one. Mike Brown never said it. I went through every Brown quote about Towns I could find from media day through the end of the preseason. He talked about Towns's role, his feel, and where he might play. He never said anything about gratitude, the contract or the extension. Somebody put words in the coach's mouth to sell a contract story.

But there is a real Towns story this week, and it's more interesting than the fake one. An executive from another team told the New York Post which teams could realistically sign Towns if he leaves next summer, and the honest answer to "how much leverage does Towns have" is probably not what you'd expect. So today: what Brown actually said, what's real about the Towns situation and what isn't, and then the math that I think explains why the Knicks paused these talks in the first place.

Welcome back to Ketuil KNICKS. Let's start with the coach.

On October 12th, Mike Brown was asked about Towns's role this season. Here's what he said: "There's a chance he may play a little bit more at the four, but what does that equate to?" And: "He has a better feel, and I have a better feel of how to use him when he's there. But I couldn't give you an exact number." He also said, "I'm not sure yet."

That's it. A basketball answer about Towns playing more power forward next to another big. It actually connects to the roster moves this week. With James Wiseman likely making the team as a third center and Andre Drummond as the backup, Brown has more bodies who can play the five, which gives him more freedom to slide Towns to the four in certain lineups. That's a real development, and it's the opposite of a coach trashing his star. It's a coach saying he understands how to use his star better than he did a year ago.

And at media day, Brown's message was about the team, not any individual. "We can't afford to have any slippage in our process," he said. That's the line that matters from him. Not gratitude. Standards.

Now, to be fair, there is one real piece of tension between Towns and his coach, and I don't want to hide it. At media day, Towns said that during last season he'd asked Brown to run more of the offense through him, and he was disappointed that didn't happen as much as he wanted. That's real. It's on the record. But listen to how it's framed. It's a player wanting a bigger role in a system that just won a championship. It's not a feud. And Brown's response this month, talking about having a better feel for how to use him, sounds like a coach who heard it.

So the headline is fake. Now let's go through the video it came from, because almost every tell we've talked about this month is in there.

Tell number one. It says Tom Thibodeau has "stayed completely quiet" about the situation. The Knicks' head coach is Mike Brown. If a video about the Knicks names Thibodeau as the current coach, it was built from an old template. That alone should end it.

Tell number two. The running gag. This one is a "gravitational field" around Madison Square Garden. It comes back several times. When a video needs a metaphor to carry it, it usually doesn't have reporting to carry it.

Tell number three. The history is wrong. The video says Towns arrived in New York "in September of 2025" for Julius Randle "plus two additional draft picks." The trade was agreed in late September of 2024, before the 2024-25 season, and it was Julius Randle, Donte DiVincenzo and a protected first-round pick going to Minnesota, with Charlotte joining as a third team. Leaving out DiVincenzo, who was a key piece of the deal, tells you the writer didn't look it up.

Tell number four. "A real reported proposal from Heavy." The video says Heavy published a trade proposal sending Towns to Boston for Paul George, and then calls it "a real reported proposal." Those two words don't go together. A proposal is a writer's idea. A report is when someone with sources says teams are talking. I couldn't find any report of Boston and New York discussing Towns. And think about the idea for a second. Paul George is thirty-six. Towns is turning thirty-one next month. Trading your second-best player on a title team for an older, more injury-prone player wouldn't solve the Knicks' contract question. It would just give them a worse version of it.

Tell number five. The "institutional silence" claim. The video says James Dolan and Leon Rose have stayed silent, and that silence is "the loudest possible signal." The Knicks made a public statement on October 9th, pausing all extension talks. That's not silence. And front offices almost never comment on individual negotiations. Calling normal practice a "held breath" is how you turn nothing into drama.

And tell number six. The way it describes the Bleacher Report piece. The video insists it wasn't speculation, but "an actual rival executive" speaking "on record to a national outlet." Let's look at what actually happened.

Here's the real story. On October 15th, Stefan Bondy of the New York Post quoted an anonymous NBA executive, and Bleacher Report then wrote it up. So it wasn't on the record, and the original reporting was the Post's, not Bleacher Report's. Here's what the executive said: "Only a few possible suitors stand out to me right now in terms of cap space teams and fit." "Chicago and the Clippers have the cleanest path." "The Nets can get there with the money, but that's not a good fit." And: "Golden State and Dallas could get around fifty million in space with more tinkering."

Notice what that's about. Not a trade. Free agency. The executive was answering a specific question. If Towns turns down his player option and becomes a free agent next summer, which teams could create the cap space to sign him? That's an honest, useful question, and it's a different story from "rival teams are lining up to trade for Towns."

And there's one more line in that reporting that the fake videos skipped, and I think it's the most important sentence of the week. Executives believe that even the teams in the best position could offer Towns something like forty-five to fifty million dollars a year. That's below what the Knicks can pay him.

Before the math, let me deal with the idea in the title itself, because a lot of fans genuinely believe it, even though Mike Brown never said it. The argument goes like this. Minnesota moved on from Towns. The Knicks traded for him, built around him and Brunson, and within two seasons he had a ring. So he should be grateful and take the offer.

I understand the feeling. But I don't think gratitude is how professional contracts work, and I don't think it's fair to ask that of any player. The Knicks didn't trade for Towns as a favor. They traded Julius Randle, Donte DiVincenzo and a first-round pick because they thought he'd help them win a title, and he did. That's a deal that worked for both sides. Towns delivered. Asking for a contract that reflects that isn't ingratitude. And on the other side, the Knicks pausing talks isn't disrespect. It's a front office doing its job under the tightest payroll rules in the sport. Both can be true at the same time.

So let's do the math, because this is what explains the Knicks' approach.

Towns is making about fifty-seven million this season. He has a player option for 2027-28 worth about sixty-one million. He's eligible for a four-year extension worth up to about two hundred and seventy-six million. Shams reported the Knicks' offer was about two hundred million over four years.

Now, here's the part that changes how you see this. Two hundred million over four years averages fifty million a year. And under the extension rules, as ESPN's Bobby Marks explained, the first year of any new extension would replace his sixty-one million dollar option. So if the Knicks' offer starts somewhere in the mid-forties, which is roughly how a two-hundred-million deal with standard raises would be structured, Towns would be taking a pay cut of around fifteen million dollars in 2027-28 just to sign it. That's why he hasn't signed. It's not just the total. It's that the first year is worth less than what he's already owed.

Now look at his alternatives. Option one: sign the Knicks' offer. Long-term security, but a pay cut next season. Option two: pick up the sixty-one million option for 2027-28 and become a free agent in 2028, at almost thirty-three. Option three: turn down the option and become a free agent next summer, at thirty-one.

And here's where the executive's quote matters. If he becomes a free agent next summer, the teams with cap room, Chicago and the Clippers, maybe Golden State or Dallas with some work, could reportedly offer around forty-five to fifty million a year. That's about the same per year as what the Knicks are offering now, maybe less. And the Knicks hold his Bird rights, which means they can go over the cap to re-sign him and offer more years and bigger raises than any other team.

And the Bird rights advantage is bigger than most people realize. If Towns becomes a free agent, the Knicks can offer him a five-year deal with annual raises of up to eight percent. Any other team can only offer four years, with raises of up to five percent. So even if a cap-space team matched the Knicks' starting salary, New York could still offer an extra year and steeper raises, which over the life of the deal could add up to tens of millions of dollars. That's a real structural edge, and it's one of the reasons teams with a homegrown or acquired star rarely lose him in free agency unless the relationship has broken down.

So when people say the Knicks are taking a huge risk by pausing talks, I'd push back. The market, as one executive describes it, doesn't seem to have a team that can blow away New York's number. Towns's strongest card is the sixty-one million option itself, the guaranteed money next season, not the open market. And that's probably why the Knicks were comfortable pausing. They're betting that waiting doesn't cost them much.

But let me steelman Towns's side, because he has a real case.

First, he was outstanding in the title run: about sixteen points, over ten rebounds and almost five assists a game, while shooting about fifty-five percent from the field and over forty-five percent from three. You don't win a title without that. Second, the cap is rising, and more teams may find room by next summer than an executive can see in October. And third, a great season changes everything. If Towns makes an All-NBA team this year, that forty-five-to-fifty-million estimate looks low, and the Knicks' two hundred million looks cheap. That's his bet.

And there's the human side. After the pause, Towns said, "It was news to me," and "Very disappointed, to say the least." He also said, "It's business, but there is business on the court for us to handle." And then: "My only philosophy since we won, the only thing I've had in my mind, is about how do we win another one. How do we keep the trophy in New York past just this one summer?" That's not a player asking out. That's a player who's hurt and still focused on winning here.

He's also not the only voice. Carmelo Anthony weighed in on his podcast with a simple message about this team: "You don't break up the family." You can argue with that from a cap perspective. But it tells you how the people around this team feel about a group that just won.

There's also a timing rule that makes all of this more pressing, and we talked about it last week. Once the regular season starts on Tuesday, a veteran can only sign an extension if his contract is in its final year, and option years count. Towns has this season plus the 2027-28 option, so he isn't in his final year. That means if there's no deal before opening night, there can't be an extension during this season. The next chance is next summer. So the Knicks' "later in the year" really means "next offseason" for Towns.

Now let's look at those suitors honestly, because each one has a catch.

Chicago. The Bulls are one of the teams Bobby Marks has listed with potential cap room for 2027. Towns would be the best player they've had in a long time. But going from a title team to a team that's been stuck in the middle of the East for years is a big step down in winning, and Towns just told us winning is his only philosophy.

The Clippers. On paper, they have a path to space. But this is a front office in turmoil. Lawrence Frank is suspended. Trent Redden is the interim head of basketball operations. Steve Ballmer is serving his own one-year suspension after the investigation tied to the Kawhi Leonard endorsement deal, and Kawhi is now in Toronto. A free agent choosing between a stable champion and an organization in that situation would have a lot to think about.

Brooklyn. The executive said it himself: they can get to the money, but it's not a good fit. And Golden State and Dallas would need "more tinkering," and in Dallas, the timeline is built around Cooper Flagg.

So none of those landing spots is clean, except in the narrow cap-space sense the executive meant. That's another reason the Knicks' position is stronger than the headlines suggest.

So where does that leave us?

And to understand why the Knicks are being careful, look at the payroll. This season, the five starters alone make about a hundred and ninety-two million dollars. Towns at about fifty-seven, Anunoby at forty-two and a half, Brunson at about thirty-seven point seven, Bridges at about thirty-three and a half, and Hart at about twenty-one. That's before a single bench player. Add a max extension for Towns starting at around sixty-eight million in 2027-28, and the Knicks would be deep into the second apron for years, with all the restrictions that come with it: no aggregating salaries in trades, frozen draft picks, and almost no way to improve the bench. That's not an excuse. It's the reality the front office is managing.

Here's my honest read. The Knicks aren't trying to push Towns out. They're managing a contract at the second apron, with Brunson, Bridges and Anunoby already on big deals, and they don't want to pay full max money into Towns's mid-thirties before seeing this season. Towns isn't trying to leave. He's disappointed that the Knicks' number starts below his option, and he believes he's worth more. Both positions make sense. And the market reported this week suggests the gap isn't as dangerous for the Knicks as it sounds.

The real risk isn't next summer. It's the relationship this season. If Towns plays like an All-NBA player and the Knicks still don't move, the frustration could grow. And if things sour, February's trade deadline is where Shams's "growing cloud" could turn into something real. I don't think we're there. But that's the thing to watch.

So, what should you watch?

One. Tuesday night, opening night against Philadelphia. Watch how Mike Brown uses Towns, and whether he plays any minutes at the four next to Drummond or Wiseman. That's the actual Brown-Towns story this season. And it isn't new territory for Towns. In Minnesota, he spent two seasons playing power forward next to Rudy Gobert, and in 2024 that pairing took the Timberwolves to the Western Conference finals. He knows how to space the floor from the four, attack slower defenders off the dribble and rebound from the weak side. The question for the Knicks is whether a Towns-Drummond or Towns-Wiseman frontcourt can defend in space against smaller lineups. If Brown finds ten good minutes a night of that look, he'll have answered the bigger-lineup question we've been asking all preseason, and he'll have given Towns more of the offensive role he's been asking for.

Two. Towns's body language and his words after games. He's been measured so far. If that changes, you'll hear it.

Three. Real reporting, not proposals. If a named reporter, Shams, Bondy, Begley, Katz, says teams are calling about Towns, that's news. If a writer says "here's a trade idea," it's not.

And four. The 2027 cap sheets. Cap space in October is a projection, not a fact. Teams add salary, make trades and change plans all season long. As the season goes on, watch which teams actually clear space. If Chicago or the Clippers start shedding salary in February, that's a signal. If they don't, Towns's free-agent leverage stays exactly where that executive put it.
