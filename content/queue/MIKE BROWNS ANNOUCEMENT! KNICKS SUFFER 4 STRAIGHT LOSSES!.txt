The Knicks have now lost four straight. Before anyone panics, all four are preseason games, and the fourth one, a hundred and forty-eight to ninety-eight in Toronto on Tuesday, was played without Jalen Brunson, Josh Hart, OG Anunoby, Mikal Bridges, Karl-Anthony Towns, Landry Shamet, Andre Drummond and Jordan Clarkson. Deuce McBride put it simply afterward: they were "missing eight of our guys."

But there's a line from Mike Brown, said before that game, that I can't stop thinking about. He said he wants to see how this team reacts "when we lose three in a row or four in a row." He said that knowing what was coming. The media would be critical, the fans would be critical, and he wanted to see whether the group keeps doing the right things anyway.

So today, the honest version of "four straight losses." What Brown actually announced and said, what happened in Toronto and what it means, the big winner from a fifty-point loss, which sounds strange but is real, Jordan Clarkson's new role, and the Karl-Anthony Towns extension clock, which runs out on opening night and which most people still don't understand.

First, Mike Brown and what he actually said, because headlines are calling it an "announcement" and I want you to know what it was.

Going into the back-to-back in Toronto, Brown made it clear the regulars wouldn't carry the load in the second game. In the end, the five starters didn't play at all, and Shamet, Drummond and Clarkson stayed home too. That's the rest decision. On its own, it's a normal preseason call on the second night of a back-to-back, six days before the opener.

The more interesting part was the philosophy. Brown said he's looking forward to seeing how this team handles adversity. He went back to last season, when the Knicks went through a bad losing stretch and came out the other side as champions. And then the line I quoted: "I want to see how we react when we lose three in a row or four in a row." He wasn't predicting failure. He was telling his players that the scoreboard isn't the test. The test is whether they keep doing the right things when the scoreboard is ugly.

And after the game, he said exactly that. "It's not necessarily what's on the scoreboard at that particular time," Brown said. "It's are you doing the right things to be better and/or get better?" Then he went right to specifics. "Fifteen second-chance points in one half is not good. Thirty fast-break points in one half is not good." And finally, "I really didn't care about the score, especially with the group we had on the floor."

That's the whole announcement. Not a firing. Not a trade. Not a lineup change. A coach telling everyone, before and after, that the October record doesn't count and the habits do. And notice the two things he named: second-chance points and fast-break points. Those are effort numbers, not talent numbers. That's been his message since day one of camp. Physicality in Philadelphia. Competition in practice. Now effort in transition and on the glass.

Now let's be honest about the game itself, because a fifty-point loss deserves at least a few minutes, even with the reserves.

Toronto played its starters. Kawhi Leonard, in his first game back in a Raptors uniform since the 2019 title run, had twenty-one points in about sixteen minutes, four of eight from three. Scottie Barnes had nineteen on eight of ten. Collin Murray-Boyles had nineteen and nine rebounds without missing a shot, seven for seven. And yes, two former Knicks hurt their old team. RJ Barrett had seventeen on six of twelve, and Immanuel Quickley had eight points and six assists. Trayce Jackson-Davis was four for four off the bench, and Ja'Kobe Walter hit four threes.

The Raptors led forty-two to twenty-two after one quarter and eighty-six to forty-seven at halftime. They shot about fifty-seven percent from the field. The Knicks shot seven of forty-one from three, seventeen percent, their worst of the preseason. They had seventeen turnovers to Toronto's eight. They were outrebounded fifty-one to thirty-eight. The only quarter they won was the third, thirty-one to thirty.

So the Knicks' starting five on Tuesday was Jose Alvarado, Deuce McBride, Mohamed Diawara, Bruce Brown and James Wiseman. Against a Toronto team playing its regulars, Kawhi, Barnes, Barrett, Quickley and Jakob Poeltl among them. Let me say it as plainly as I can. That's not a test of the Knicks. That's a test of the bottom half of the Knicks' roster against the top half of Toronto's. And on that test, the Knicks' bench lost by a lot.

Is that worth worrying about? A little, and here's the specific part. Brown has been demanding more from the bench since the first week of camp. The bench defense has been a talking point in every game: thirty-nine fast-break points in Philadelphia, eighteen threes from Minnesota, and now thirty fast-break points in one half in Toronto. The Knicks don't need their reserves to beat the Raptors' starters in October. But they do need the guys in their real rotation, McBride, Alvarado, Shamet, Clarkson, Drummond, to defend in transition and finish possessions with a rebound. Of that group, only McBride and Alvarado played Tuesday. So I'd treat this game as a stress test for the deep bench, not a verdict on the team.

Now, the winner. Because there was one, and it matters for the decision the Knicks have to make in the next few days.

James Wiseman started at center and played about twenty-four minutes. He had eighteen points on seven of eight shooting and ten rebounds, a double-double, and he was four for four from the line. He grabbed four offensive rebounds, which was forty percent of the team's total. He had three turnovers and no blocks, so it wasn't a perfect night. But on a night when almost everyone around him struggled, he was the best player on the floor in a Knicks uniform.

You know the race by now. One open roster spot. Thirteen guaranteed contracts, a fourteenth required, and the second apron allowing exactly one veteran minimum. Wiseman, Drew Eubanks and Tony Bradley at center, Bruce Brown and Ochai Agbaji on the wing. And this week, The Athletic's James L. Edwards the third said he expects the fourteenth spot to go to a center, with Wiseman the likely choice and Eubanks and Bradley falling behind.

Let's look at the other two centers on Tuesday. Eubanks had eight points on three of five shooting, which was solid. There's no diagnosis or timetable, so I don't want to make too much of it. But if Bradley has even a minor hamstring strain this week, it's hard to see him winning a roster spot that's decided this week. After going five for five against Minnesota, that's tough luck.

Now, I owe you something. In the last video, I said that if I had to choose that day, I'd keep Bruce Brown, and if the Knicks wanted a center, I'd lean Wiseman. I also told you what would change my mind: Wiseman getting real minutes and looking like a rotation player, not just a lob catcher. Well, he got twenty-four minutes as a starter, scored in the post and on putbacks, rebounded in traffic, and kept his composure while his team was getting run off the floor. That's real evidence. So I'll be honest. The case for Wiseman is stronger now than it was two days ago, and I think he's now the favorite, not just among the centers, but for the spot overall.

Bruce Brown also started, and I haven't been able to find his individual numbers, so I won't guess. His case is still about the bench defense problem, and Tuesday showed that problem is real. But one roster spot can't fix everything, and if the coaching staff believes a third center is required, Wiseman made it very hard to pick anyone else.

Let me add the right amount of caution. Twenty-four minutes in a fifty-point loss isn't the same as six minutes in a close game in February. Wiseman still has the issues scouts have talked about for years. His feet can get choppy in pick-and-roll coverage. He can be late on rotations. He can get handsy setting screens, which leads to offensive fouls. He's never been a big shot-blocker for his size. And the health questions are real. He missed the 2021-22 season with a knee injury and tore his Achilles in his first game with Indiana in October of 2024. Before this preseason, he'd played only a handful of games in two seasons.

But here's what Mike Brown said about him this week, looking back at their time together in Golden State. "Back then, obviously, he was talented but he was really raw in terms of his ability and feel on the court." That's the point. Brown saw the raw version. He knows what's different now. And if he's playing him as a starter, he likes what's changed. There's a nice footnote too. Wiseman is technically an NBA champion already. He was on Golden State's roster for the 2022 title, though he didn't play that season because of the knee. One Sporting News headline this week called him the "high-upside seven-foot NBA champion." It's a little generous, but it's true.

And remember the contract structure. If Wiseman gets the spot, he'll almost certainly be on a non-guaranteed deal until the league-wide guarantee date in early January. That gives the Knicks two and a half months to find out if Tuesday was real. One writer this week summed it up well: "Give him a test, not the job." I agree with that. The roster spot is a test with a deadline. And the cost of failing it is low.

Now let's talk about a veteran whose role is settled, but whose story is worth telling, because it's the opposite of the Wiseman story. Jordan Clarkson.

When this offseason started, it wasn't certain Clarkson would come back. He'd just won the first title of his career, and the Knicks' early priorities on the bench were reportedly Landry Shamet, Jose Alvarado and the young forward Mohamed Diawara. So the question was fair. Would a former Sixth Man of the Year, a career scorer, come back for a smaller role and minimum-level money? And the answer was yes. In July, Shams reported a one-year deal for about three point nine million dollars.

And here's a detail that connects to the roster math we've been doing. On the Knicks' payroll, Clarkson shows up at about two point four five million, not three point nine. That's the one-year veteran minimum rule we explained last time. The player gets his full minimum based on experience, the team is charged the two-year amount, and the league covers the difference. Clarkson's contract is a perfect example of why the Knicks can fit exactly one more veteran under the apron.

In comments relayed in Knicks coverage this week, Clarkson talked about his role in a way you don't usually hear from a scorer. He said playing his role for this group is something he's fallen in love with, that he loves playing for this team and these teammates, and that that was the biggest lesson from last season. His goal now is to be more comfortable in the system, bring a spark when he gets in, and keep doing the small things that win. That lines up with what Shamet has said about this bench for months: nobody in that second unit is pushing a personal agenda.

And that's not just talk, because of how last season went for him. Clarkson played in each of the Knicks' first forty-two games, at about twenty minutes a night, averaging just under ten points. Then, after a rough stretch for the team, Mike Brown shook up the rotation, and Clarkson went from every night to barely playing. He had a long run of DNPs, and for a while it looked like he was stuck at the end of the bench. A lot of veteran scorers would have sulked or asked out.

Instead, he changed his game. He's been a shoot-first volume scorer for his whole career, not known for defense or rebounding. When he came back into the rotation, he was different. Better shot selection, more drives and floaters, fewer tough jumpers. And more importantly, he competed on defense. He didn't turn into a lockdown defender, but he became a pest, staying in front of his man and crashing the offensive glass. That earned the staff's trust back, and he was part of a title team. When the Knicks re-signed him, his regular-season average was the lowest of his career, about eight and a half points, and under five in the playoffs. And he still came back.

So why does this matter now? Because Clarkson is exactly the kind of veteran Brown keeps talking about when he says everybody outside the starting five is fighting for minutes. Clarkson isn't one of the five. He knows it. He may start the season with irregular minutes, especially if the staff wants to keep developing Diawara, who played twenty-eight minutes on Tuesday and scored thirteen with three threes. And Clarkson sounds ready for that. When there's an injury or a cold stretch, he'll be ready. On a team that every opponent will treat as their measuring stick for eighty-two games, that kind of veteran matters.

Now the biggest story underneath all of this. Karl-Anthony Towns.

Late last week, the Knicks announced that they're pausing all extension negotiations so the team can focus on the season. That covers every extension-eligible player, including Towns, Josh Hart, Deuce McBride and Tyler Kolek. We covered the announcement when it happened. What's new is Towns's reaction. After practice on Sunday, he said, "It was news to me," and "Very disappointed, to say the least." He added, "It's business, but there is business on the court for us to handle." And then, "My only philosophy since we won, the only thing I've had in my mind, is about how do we win another one. How do we keep the trophy in New York past just this one summer?"

That's a measured response. Disappointed, honest, but focused. I don't read it as a player who wants out. I read it as a player who's hurt and saying so, and then putting it aside.

The money hasn't changed. Towns is eligible for a four-year extension worth up to about two hundred and seventy-six million dollars. Shams reported the Knicks' last offer was around two hundred million over four years. Towns makes about fifty-seven million this season and has a player option worth about sixty-one million for 2027-28. That's a gap of about seventy-six million dollars, and most of it would be paid in years when Towns is in his thirties.

And here's the rule that turns this pause into something bigger. Once the regular season starts, a veteran can only sign an extension if his contract is in its final year. Option years count toward the length of the contract. Towns has this season plus a player option for next season, so his contract isn't in its final year. That means if the Knicks and Towns don't agree before opening night on Tuesday, October 20th, against Philadelphia, there's no extension possible during this season at all.

So the Knicks saying they'll revisit things "later in the year" means something different for Towns than for others. For McBride, whose contract expires next summer, an in-season extension is still possible. For Towns, the next chance is next summer. He could pick up his option, which would put him in the final year and make him extension-eligible again. Or he could decline it and become a free agent, with the Knicks holding his Bird rights. Either way, the pause effectively takes a Towns extension off the table until the 2027 offseason. That's why some people read this as more than a scheduling decision. And it's why Towns said he was disappointed.

There's a similar question for Josh Hart, who has a twenty-two million dollar team option for next season. Since that's an option year, his deal also isn't in its final season, so the same opening-night logic applies to him.

Let me steelman the Knicks here, because it's a reasonable choice. A front office at the second apron, already giving Brunson, Bridges and Anunoby big money, might want to see a full season of Towns at thirty-one before committing two hundred-plus million into his mid-thirties. They want to see how this roster performs as champions under a target every night. And they keep all their options, including the trade market, which, as Shams said when he talked about the "growing cloud," could heat up around the February deadline if nothing changes. That's not a crazy position. It's a cautious one.

And the risk, from Towns's side, is just as clear. If he has a great season, his price goes up, and the Knicks might regret not paying him now. If he opts out next summer, they'd be negotiating against the whole league. And if the relationship sours, the "business on the court" he talked about gets harder to keep separate. I don't think we're there. But the clock is real, and it hits zero on Tuesday night.
