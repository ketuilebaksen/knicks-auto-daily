First, the business, because it's the part making people nervous.

Towns holds a player option worth sixty-one million dollars for 2027-28, and he's eligible for an extension worth up to roughly two hundred and seventy-six million over four years. And according to Shams Charania, talks between the two sides have stalled.

Now, before anybody panics — that word sounds much worse than the situation is.

Towns has leverage. He has no reason to accept a big discount in September, and there is a lot of time left. We covered the numbers on this channel recently: one projection has the deal landing around a hundred and eighty million, another closer to two-forty. Those are very different outcomes, and neither side gets there in a week.

So this isn't a crisis. It's a slow negotiation, which is what these almost always look like from the outside.

Now the part everyone is actually talking about. His body.

The six-time All-Star, who turns thirty-one in November, looks noticeably leaner heading into this title defense. Photos from his summer went around social media fast, and the reaction was the same everywhere. That is a slimmer version of the big man who just helped win a championship.

It was a big summer away from basketball too. He married Jordan Woods after six years together — a wedding ring to go with the championship ring from June. They honeymooned in Africa, went on safari, snorkeled, took a hot air balloon ride.

And then the photos surfaced, and the conversation stopped being about the trip.

So what should we make of it?

Let's start with the honest concern. A lighter center could lose strength on the boards, or get moved around by bigger bodies in the post. That's a fair question about any big man.

But here's the thing — Towns has never built his game on mass. His weapons have always been his shooting touch, his passing, and his ability to move like a much smaller player.

And let me put some numbers on what those weapons produced, because I don't think people fully appreciate what happened last spring.

Towns became only the eighth center in league history to record more than ninety assists in a single playoff run.

He became the first center ever to shoot fifty-forty-ninety during a championship run — at least fifty percent from the field, forty percent from three, and ninety percent from the line.

And his plus two-fifty-eight plus-minus is the best mark ever recorded in a single postseason. By anyone.

Read that again. The best single-postseason plus-minus in the history of the sport.

So the honest framing of this story isn't "is he going to be too small." It's much more interesting than that. How do you improve on the best postseason a center has ever had?

And I think the answer to that is exactly why he lost the weight.

Because for years, the single biggest criticism of Towns has been his defense in space — specifically when he gets switched onto quicker players out on the perimeter. Better lateral quickness directly attacks that. Switch, recover, stay in front. That's the one hole in his game and this is the most direct way to address it.

On offense, extra mobility should make him more explosive attacking closeouts off the three-point line, which is already one of the most difficult things in basketball to guard.

And then there's the part nobody puts on a highlight. Health.

Over the past two seasons, Towns has dealt with a nagging knee issue that he's mostly just played through. Carrying less weight means less load on the joints, and that could keep his knees fresher across eighty-two games and another long playoff run.

He's listed at two hundred and forty-eight pounds. And it's widely accepted around this league that big men who drop weight give themselves a better chance of extending their careers.

The classic example is Tim Duncan. After a first-round exit in 2011, he came back leaner — and he was a key piece of a championship team in 2014, at thirty-eight years old.

Now let me give you the wrinkle, because this isn't the first time we've had this conversation.

Last October, Towns told Steve Popper of Newsday that he'd slimmed down over the summer to prepare for more minutes at power forward. That plan never really happened. Mike Brown rarely used lineups with two traditional bigs, and Towns ended up anchoring the middle for most of the season.

And that role isn't changing. The Knicks lost two centers in free agency, Andre Drummond is in his fifteenth season, and whoever wins the camp battle is a third option. Towns is playing center. Again.

So some people will say that's exactly why losing weight is risky.

I don't buy it. He's still a seven-footer, and a few pounds doesn't make a seven-footer disappear in the paint. This isn't the nineties, where centers wrestled in the post every night. The game is faster than it has ever been, and a center who can shoot, pass, and run the floor is an enormous advantage.

Lighter, quicker, healthier Towns might be the most important development of this entire offseason — and it didn't cost the front office a single asset.

Alright, now the roster news, because two contracts got signed on Sunday.

The Knicks officially signed their second-round pick Tyler Nickel to a two-way contract. And per SNY's Ian Begley, they also signed Kevin McCullar Jr. to a two-way deal.

That leaves exactly one two-way spot open with camp underway. Media day was Monday, camp opens right after, and the first preseason game is October fifth. The clock ran out on waiting.

Now, not every fan is going to love the Nickel decision. He played well at Summer League — twelve point two points a game while shooting forty-one point three percent from three. That kind of shooting is valuable, and plenty of people wanted a standard contract.

But I think the two-way is genuinely the right call, and here's the number that explains it.

Pacôme Dadiet averaged four point seven minutes a game last season.

That's what being young on a contender looks like. There are no development minutes available on a team trying to repeat. So the choice isn't "two-way or rotation player." It's "two-way, or sitting at the end of the bench in a suit."

On a two-way, Nickel can play up to fifty NBA games, and the rest of the time he's in Westchester taking real shots, playing real minutes, and working on defense, ball handling, and decision-making. That's how a shooter becomes a complete player.

And it cuts both ways, which is the beauty of it. If he lights up the G League, the Knicks can convert him to a standard deal and hand him that fifteenth roster spot. If he isn't ready, they keep him on the two-way all season and decide next summer. Nobody is locked into anything.

Same path now for McCullar. Both of them get camp and preseason to make an impression, and both are fighting to prove they belong in the NBA and not just the G League.

And there's a practical benefit for the team too. Over a long season, with injuries and back-to-backs, two-way players give a coaching staff extra bodies it can call up without touching one of the fifteen standard spots. For a champion, having young players who already know the system and can step in on short notice is real value.

Which brings us to that last two-way spot. Because on Friday, something unusual became available.

The Clippers waived center Jamarion Sharp, per Jake Fischer. He'd been on a two-way deal there, and the release surprised a lot of people.

And here's what makes him interesting. He is seven feet five inches tall.

But he's not just tall — he knows how to use it. Last season in the G League with the Texas Legends, he averaged seven and a half rebounds in about twenty-five minutes a night, including two point eight on the offensive glass.

His real impact was rim protection. Four point one blocks per game. He led the entire G League in blocks, was named G League Defensive Player of the Year, and made the all-defensive team.

And those numbers were a jump from his first G League season, when he averaged seven point one rebounds and three point one blocks. That's a player getting better, not a player coasting on his height.

His path wasn't easy either — undrafted in 2024 after playing at John A. Logan College, Western Kentucky, and Ole Miss, then working up through the G League to earn a two-way. He just turned twenty-five in August.

The honest caveat: he has never played an NBA regular season game. So of everyone in this frontcourt conversation, he's the least proven.

And there's a real cost to choosing him, because Liam Robbins has been viewed as a strong candidate for that last two-way spot. With one slot left, picking Sharp likely means closing the door on Robbins unless the front office finds another way.

But here's why I'd at least make the call. Two-way contracts carry almost no risk. They're easy to move on from, and this front office has already shown it will change those spots mid-season when something isn't working.

The third center spot doesn't sound like a headline. But depth up front is exactly what decides seasons once injuries and fatigue pile up — and a seven-foot-five shot blocker is a specific weapon for specific matchups, even in small minutes.
