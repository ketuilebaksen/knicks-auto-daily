The Knicks just did something you almost never see an NBA team do. They announced, publicly, that they've stopped negotiating.

On Friday, a team spokesman put out a statement saying the Knicks' entire focus is on preparing for the upcoming season, and that they will not be negotiating contract extensions until later in the season. That covers Karl-Anthony Towns. It covers Josh Hart. It covers Deuce McBride and Tyler Kolek. Four extension-eligible players, and the talks for every one of them are now officially on hold.

A few hours later, Towns posted "Always Love" with orange and blue hearts. Hart posted "NY" with the same hearts.

First, why announce it?

Teams pause negotiations all the time. They just don't tell anyone. A front office stops returning calls for a few weeks, the agent understands, and the story goes quiet. The Knicks chose to put it in writing. I think there are three reasons, and they all point in the same direction.

Reason one is noise. Over the past two weeks, this story has been everywhere. Towns at shootaround saying "it don't look good." Shams going on NBA Today and talking about a growing cloud around the franchise. Ian Begley saying that by January, the Knicks would have to at least think about other options. Hart saying that if nothing is resolved by February, "it might be a different conversation." Every day a new quote, every day a new headline. The season opens on October twentieth, against LeBron James and the 76ers. A team defending a title doesn't want its first two weeks dominated by contract questions. Announcing a pause gives everyone, including the players, an answer they can give. "We're not talking about it until later." End of conversation, at least for now.

Reason two is leverage. When talks are ongoing, every quote from a player and every leak from an agent moves the market a little. When talks are officially paused, there's nothing to leak. The Knicks have essentially said, our offer is our offer, and we'll revisit it when we have more information. That's a strong position to take, and it's a deliberate one.

Reason three is information. Later in the season, the front office will know a lot more. How healthy Hart's finger is over the long grind. How Towns is playing in his twelfth season. How Drummond, Wiseman or whoever wins the backup job is holding up. Whether a trade is available. And what the league's cap projections look like for twenty twenty-seven, twenty-eight. Waiting is a way of buying information before committing hundreds of millions of dollars.

Now, is this good or bad? One Knicks outlet's headline called it bad news for everybody, including New York. And I understand that. Uncertainty wears on players. Hart said as much in training camp, that uncertainty can affect people. A pause doesn't remove the uncertainty. It just moves it to later.

But I think the reaction from the players tells you something. Towns and Hart didn't post anything angry. They posted love for New York, within hours. That's two players telling the fan base, publicly, that they're not going anywhere emotionally, even if the money isn't settled. That's the best possible response to a moment like this.

Now let's get into the numbers, because this is where I think most of the coverage has missed the real story.

Towns makes about fifty-seven million dollars this season. Next season, he has a player option worth about sixty-one point one million. He's eligible for an extension of up to four years and about two hundred and seventy-six million dollars. The Knicks have reportedly offered about two hundred million over four years.

Most people look at that and see a seventy-six million dollar gap. And that's true over the full deal. But here's the part I want you to think about.

An extension would start in twenty twenty-seven, twenty-eight. It would replace the player option year. Under the league's rules, raises in an extension like this can go up by as much as eight percent a year. If you take a four-year deal worth about two hundred and seventy-six million and build in standard eight percent raises, the first year comes out somewhere around sixty-one or sixty-two million. That's almost exactly the same as his player option.

In other words, the max extension doesn't really give Towns more money next season. He can get roughly that number just by picking up his option. The real difference between the Knicks' offer and the max is in the years after that. Twenty twenty-eight, twenty-nine. Twenty twenty-nine, thirty. Twenty thirty, thirty-one.

And now look at the Knicks' offer the same way. Two hundred million over four years with standard raises would start somewhere in the mid-forties next season and climb into the mid-fifties by the end. So the Knicks' offer would actually lower Towns's salary next season, compared with the option, by something like fifteen million dollars. And it would keep him well below max in the later years.

I want to be careful here. I don't know exactly how either side has structured its proposals. These are standard-raise estimates, not reported figures. But the shape of it is important. The fight isn't really about next year. It's about twenty twenty-eight and beyond.

And what happens in twenty twenty-eight? As we've covered, Jalen Brunson's next contract, projected at about four years and two hundred and fifty-seven million dollars, is expected to start in the twenty twenty-eight, twenty-nine season. That's the exact year where Towns's max and the Knicks' offer start to separate.

So when you hear that the Knicks won't move, this is why. It's not that they think Towns is worth fifty million and not sixty-nine. It's that they can't have Towns near the max and Brunson at the max in the same seasons and still stay under the second apron, which owner James Dolan has said on WFAN he won't approve crossing.

And here's the cap picture as reported. The Knicks have roughly two hundred and twenty-six point seven million dollars already committed for twenty twenty-seven, twenty-eight. Yahoo's reporting put that at about nine point three million below the projected second apron, though it also noted a different figure elsewhere, so the exact line isn't clear. Either way, the margin is thin. And that's before any decisions on Hart, McBride or anyone else.

And this summer already showed us what that line costs. Mitchell Robinson, eight years in this building, a key piece of the title run, left for Boston on a three-year deal worth about forty-seven point four million dollars. The Knicks let him go rather than cross the apron. That's the precedent sitting over every one of these negotiations. The front office has already proven it will lose a valued player before it breaks Dolan's rule.

And the queue doesn't end with these four. Jalen Brunson and OG Anunoby both become extension-eligible next summer. So whatever the Knicks do with Towns, Hart, McBride and Kolek isn't just about this year. It's about leaving enough room for the captain and for one of the best two-way forwards in the league a few months later.

Ian Begley put it in two words. The Knicks are "voluntarily limited." The limit is a choice. The owner made it. And every negotiation flows from it.

Now let's look at the other three players, because each of their situations is different.

Josh Hart. He makes about twenty-one million this season. The Knicks hold a team option for next season at about twenty-two point four million. And he's eligible for an extension worth up to about a hundred and thirty-two million over four years, which is around thirty-three million a year.

The key word there is team option. Unlike Towns, Hart doesn't control next season. The Knicks do. If they pick up the option, he's under contract at twenty-two point four million next year, and they can decide about the long term after that. That gives the front office a lot of leverage. It also explains why Hart is the one talking about February. If nothing happens by the trade deadline, his future is in the team's hands, not his.

And there's the finger. Hart is playing through a damaged ring finger that can only be fixed by a joint replacement, which would end his career. He looked completely normal against Washington this week. But any team thinking about a four-year commitment has to think about that.

Deuce McBride is in the final year of a three-year, thirteen million dollar extension. He becomes a free agent next summer. That makes him the most valuable player on the bench, the cheapest contract with real production, and the one player the Knicks could lose for nothing. We've covered all fall how his name keeps coming up in trade conversations for a centre. A paused extension doesn't change that. If anything, it makes it more likely that McBride is either extended late or moved by the deadline. Letting him walk for nothing next summer would be the worst outcome.

And Tyler Kolek. He has a team option of about two point five million for next season. He's the cheapest of the four, and he's also the one whose role is least settled. He had a good preseason opener, and he's exactly the kind of young player Brown has said will get a chance if he earns it. For Kolek, the pause doesn't change much. The Knicks control his option either way.

There's one more piece of context for McBride that I think gets overlooked. Earlier this week, Bruce Brown told the Post that the second apron was the reason he kept hearing all summer from teams that couldn't sign him. "I heard that all summer," he said. The apron is squeezing the market for solid role players across the whole league. That cuts both ways for McBride. On one hand, fewer teams may be able to pay him what he wants next summer, which could make a Knicks extension later in the season more realistic. On the other hand, the Knicks themselves are one of those squeezed teams. If the market is thin, McBride's best offer might come from a team with cap space, not a contender. Either way, the pause gives both sides time to see what that market looks like before anyone commits.

Now, the rules. Because there's a detail here that could matter a lot later in the season.

Extensions can be signed all the way through June thirtieth, twenty twenty-seven. But under the league's rules, an extension during the season generally requires the player's current contract to be expiring. As Yahoo laid it out, that means Towns would have to decline his player option, and the Knicks would have to decline the team options on Hart and Kolek, before an in-season extension could happen. That's a complicated set of steps, and it's one more reason the Knicks are saying "later in the season" rather than "next week."

So what does "later in the season" actually mean? Here's how I'd read the calendar.

October and November: the season, and nothing else. The Knicks want the focus on basketball.

December fifteenth: most of this summer's free-agent signings become tradable. The trade market opens up.

January: Begley's point. If the sides are still far apart, the Knicks have to start thinking about alternatives.

February: the trade deadline, and the month Hart and Shams both pointed to.

And then June thirtieth, the last day extensions can be signed before free agency.

So the pause doesn't end the story. It puts the next chapter right in the middle of the most important stretch of the trade calendar.

If you want to picture what a compromise might look like, the numbers point to one obvious shape. Because the real gap is in the later years, a deal could start near the value of Towns's option next season, which gives him the year-one money he can already get, and then grow more slowly than a standard max, so the twenty twenty-eight and later numbers stay manageable when Brunson's raise lands. Another version is fewer years, or a final year that's partly guaranteed. I want to be clear that this is me thinking out loud, not anything that's been reported. But when the disagreement is about the back end of a contract, the solution usually is too.

Now let me steelman both sides, because I think both have a case.

The Knicks' case is simple. They're not refusing to pay anybody. They're refusing to cross a line their owner has set, and they're being honest about it. They've offered Towns two hundred million dollars, which is an enormous amount of money. They hold the options on Hart and Kolek. They have time. And they'd rather make these decisions with real information about health, performance and the cap than make them now under public pressure.

The players' case is also simple. Towns has already said he's willing to take a considerable discount. He feels he earned a bigger deal after winning a title. Hart has been the emotional engine of this team, playing through an injury that would have sidelined most players. McBride wants to be paid like the rotation player he is. And all of them are being asked to play a full season under uncertainty, while the team holds most of the leverage.

Both of those are true. That's what makes this hard.

So where do I land? I think the announcement was smart in the short term. It removes the noise before the opener, it gives the players a clean answer for every interview, and the players' reaction suggests the relationships are fine.

But I think it carries real risk in the long term. Every month of waiting moves leverage around. For Hart and Kolek, it moves towards the team, because the team controls their options. For McBride, it moves towards free agency, which is the outcome the Knicks least want. And for Towns, it moves towards his player option, which is his biggest piece of leverage. If he has a great season, the price goes up. If he struggles, the Knicks' offer looks better. Either way, the Knicks are betting that the information they get will be worth the leverage they might lose.

I'd also say this. The Knicks now have two months to make the bench decisions that might make some of this easier. If they can move a smaller salary like Pacôme Dadiet's, or get real value for McBride, or find a cheap centre who sticks, that's room they can use when talks resume. The pause isn't the end of the plan. It's the start of a different part of it.
