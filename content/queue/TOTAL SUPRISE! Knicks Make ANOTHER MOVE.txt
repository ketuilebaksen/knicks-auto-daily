There wasn't another move. No trade, no signing. The surprise from opening night is a player the Knicks signed back in July, on a minimum deal, that a lot of people thought was finished: Andre Drummond. On a night when the Knicks' starters couldn't make shots, their backup center was the best player on the floor for long stretches of the first half.

So today, let's talk about Drummond properly. What he did against his old team, the historic number behind it, why he might fit this roster in ways Mitchell Robinson didn't, and the one thing Mike Brown admitted after the game that could limit all of it.

Welcome back to Ketuil KNICKS. Let's get into it.

Here's the setup. Early in the opener against Philadelphia, Karl-Anthony Towns picked up two quick fouls. Mike Brown had to go to his bench much earlier than he wanted to. In came Drummond, who spent the previous two seasons with the Sixers.

And for the rest of the first half, he controlled the game. Ten points, twelve rebounds, six of them offensive, three assists and two blocks, all before halftime. According to the reporting after the game, that made him the first Knick in the play-by-play era, going back to the 1997-98 season, to record a first-half double-double in his Knicks debut. And the Knicks were plus seven with him on the floor in the first half, the best mark on the team. They led fifty-eight to fifty-four at the break.

He finished with twelve points on five of thirteen shooting, fourteen rebounds, seven of them offensive, four assists, two blocks and a steal in twenty-four minutes. The fourteen rebounds led the game. And this was on a night when the starting five shot just twenty-two of fifty-eight, about thirty-eight percent. The bench, led by Drummond, kept the Knicks in it.

Here's what Mike Brown said about him afterward. "I thought he played with a lot of bounce and energy in the first half on both ends of the floor." And then: "I thought he was really, really, really good in the first half." Three reallys. And notice the words "first half." We'll come back to that.

Now the number that explains why this wasn't a fluke.

There's a stat called total rebound percentage. It's simple. When a shot misses and you're on the floor, what share of the available rebounds do you grab? It's better than rebounds per game because it doesn't depend on how many minutes you play or how fast your team plays. And on the all-time career leaderboard at Basketball Reference, number one is Andre Drummond, at about twenty-five point one percent. Number two is Dennis Rodman, at about twenty-three point four. Then Clint Capela, Reggie Evans and DeAndre Jordan, all around twenty-two.

Think about that. Rodman is the name everyone uses as the gold standard for rebounding. Drummond is ahead of him by more than a point and a half, and nobody else is within three points of Drummond. By the most widely used rate measure, Drummond is the best rebounder in NBA history. He's said as much himself in interviews over the years, and the numbers back him up.

And it helps to remember who Drummond was, because a lot of younger fans only know the late-career version. Detroit took him ninth overall in 2012. He became a two-time All-Star with the Pistons, and he led the league in rebounding four times. For several years, he was the most dominant rebounder in basketball, a twenty-and-fifteen threat on some nights. Then came the journeyman years: Cleveland, the Lakers, Philadelphia, Brooklyn, Chicago, and back to Philadelphia. His scoring dropped and his minutes shrank, but the rebounding never really left. Last season, in about twenty minutes a night, he was still one of the most productive rebounders per minute in the league.

He's also one of the rare big men with a lot of career steals to go with his blocks, which tells you how active his hands are. When people call him washed, what they really mean is that he doesn't score twenty a night like he did in Detroit. That's true. But nobody's paying him to score twenty. He's on a one-year minimum deal, which counts about two point four five million against the Knicks' cap. They're paying him to do one thing better than almost anyone who's ever played. Rebound.

Why does a rebounding rate matter so much? Think about what an offensive rebound actually is. It's a second possession. If an average NBA possession is worth a little more than one point, then seven offensive rebounds are roughly seven extra chances to score, in a game the Knicks lost by six. On a night when the starters shot thirty-eight percent, those extra possessions were the only reason the Knicks were close. And on defense, a center who grabs a high share of the other team's misses ends possessions, which is how you stop the second-chance points that Mike Brown has been complaining about since the preseason. Rebounding isn't glamorous. It's math.

Now let's talk about fit, because this is where it gets interesting.

Over the last few seasons, the Knicks built an identity on the offensive glass. They were one of the best offensive rebounding teams in the league, and the engine of that was Mitchell Robinson, who was among the very best in the league at rebounding the ball while he was on the floor. Then in July, Robinson signed with Boston, three years and about forty-seven million dollars, because the Knicks wouldn't go into the second apron to keep him. And the Knicks replaced him with Drummond, on a minimum.

When the Knicks signed him, the reaction was mixed. Some graded it a C. And even on this channel, back in September, we said Drummond was a rebounding replacement, not a Mitchell Robinson replacement. I still think that's right on defense. Drummond isn't the rim deterrent Robinson was. Robinson changed shots just by standing there, and he knew every Knicks coverage after eight years in the building.

But on opening night, the specific job got done. By one count of the box score, the Knicks won the offensive rebounding battle fourteen to six, and Drummond alone grabbed seven, more offensive rebounds than the entire Sixers team. That's the identity, back on night one.

And the money matters too. Robinson's new deal in Boston pays him around sixteen million dollars a year. Drummond counts about two point four five million against the Knicks' cap. The gap between those two numbers is part of how the Knicks stayed under the second apron while keeping the rest of their title core together. If Drummond can give them even seventy or eighty percent of Robinson's rebounding impact at a fraction of the cost, that's one of the best value signings of the offseason.

And there are two areas where Drummond might actually help in ways Robinson didn't.

The first is passing. Drummond had four assists on opening night, tied for the team lead in the first half, and he's always been a capable passer out of the high post and off the offensive glass. Robinson was never much of a passer. That matters for how the Knicks want to play. Mike Brown wants about twenty "sprays" a game, kick-outs from the paint to shooters on the perimeter. When Drummond grabs an offensive rebound in traffic, he can kick it out to Bridges or Anunoby in the corner instead of forcing a contested putback.

The second is free throws, and I want to be careful here. Robinson's struggles at the line were a real problem in the playoffs, where teams intentionally fouled him, "Hack-a-Mitch," to force Mike Brown's hand late in games. Drummond has never been a good free-throw shooter either, so I won't oversell this. But he's been more serviceable at the line in recent seasons, and a big man who can make even six out of ten is one a coach can leave on the floor in a close game. That's a small but real advantage.

This also wasn't out of nowhere. In the preseason, Mike Brown went out of his way to praise him. After the Minnesota game, Brown said, "His communication right now is unbelievable," and talked about Drummond defending the pick-and-roll the right way and being big. In the preseason finale against Toronto, Drummond had fourteen points on six of seven shooting, eight rebounds and four assists. Against Minnesota, he had three blocks in twelve minutes. So opening night was the continuation of a strong month, not a random explosion.

And think about how Brown might use him alongside Towns. Brown has hinted that Towns could play a little more power forward this season. The most natural partner for that is a center who rebounds, screens hard and doesn't need the ball, which describes Drummond exactly. A Towns-Drummond frontcourt in short stretches would give the Knicks size and rebounding, with Towns spacing the floor from the four. It's not a lineup you'd play for thirty minutes, because of the defense in space. But for six or eight minutes against a big team, it's a real option.

And there's one more factor that nobody talks about. Towns is the only other true seven-footer in the regular rotation. On opening night, he picked up two early fouls. Every night that happens, the backup center isn't a backup anymore. He's the center. That's why Drummond's role matters more on this team than a typical backup's would.

Now, the catch. Remember "first half"? Here's the second half.

After halftime, Drummond grabbed just two more rebounds, and he missed three of his four shots from within five feet of the rim. He looked tired. And Mike Brown said so. "We probably ran him too long, because it looked like in the second half..." He said he needs to keep getting to know Drummond to figure out where his minutes should be for him to be effective. Brown also said the starters seemed to run low on energy, so this wasn't only Drummond. But the point is fair.

Here's the context. Over the last four seasons, in Chicago and Philadelphia, Drummond averaged about seventeen minutes a game. On opening night, because of Towns's foul trouble, he played twenty-four. He's thirty-three. His best version is a short, intense stretch of rebounding, screening and protecting the paint, and then a rest before his legs go.

There's also a smaller fix on offense. One analysis of last season found that when Drummond tried to score directly off his own offensive rebounds, those putbacks were less efficient than Robinson's. I can't verify the exact figures, so treat them carefully. But the idea makes sense if you watched opening night: three of four misses at the rim in the second half, mostly on second efforts in traffic. The best offensive rebounders don't just go back up. They read the floor. Drummond's passing gives him that option.

So the best version of Drummond for the Knicks probably looks like this: around eighteen to twenty minutes a night, in shorter stints, especially when the starters need rest or when Towns is in foul trouble. And on offense, when he grabs a rebound in traffic, the smart play isn't always to go back up. It's to kick it out and reset. The first half against Philadelphia showed what he can still be in those short bursts. The second half showed what happens when he's asked to do too much.

And this is where James Wiseman comes in. Wiseman made the team as the third center, a younger, more athletic option who can run the floor. If Mike Brown keeps Drummond to the right number of minutes, Wiseman is the release valve on nights when Towns gets into foul trouble. That's exactly the scenario the Knicks were planning for when they chose Wiseman over Bruce Brown. And on Friday in Boston, with Mitchell Robinson and Neemias Queta on the other side, we might see all three bigs.

And Friday has a storyline that writes itself. Drummond, the man the Knicks signed to replace Mitchell Robinson, against Mitchell Robinson, in Robinson's first game against the team he spent eight seasons with. Robinson knows every Knicks set, every tendency, every coverage. Drummond will be trying to win the glass against the best offensive rebounder the Knicks have had in a generation. And Boston pairs Robinson with Neemias Queta, so the Celtics can keep two size-and-energy bigs on the floor for most of the game. If the Knicks win the offensive glass in that matchup, it'll say a lot about whether they've really kept their identity.

And it raises a fair question about the center hunt. Marc Stein reported this week that the Knicks are still looking for one more center, and that they'd move Tyler Kolek only for a "coveted" one. If Drummond keeps playing like he did in the first half against Philadelphia, does that urgency fade? I don't think it disappears. Drummond is thirty-three, on a one-year deal, and the Knicks need a long-term answer behind Towns. But a strong Drummond changes the timeline. Instead of chasing a center before Christmas, the Knicks can wait for the deadline, when prices often come down and more of their own players become tradable. In that sense, Drummond's play gives the front office something valuable: patience.

Let me give you the counterargument, because it's real. One great half against a Sixers team with a thin frontcourt isn't proof. Philadelphia was missing Ariel Hukporti for the season, and Dean Wade was out. Joel Embiid played, but the Sixers' bench bigs are limited. Boston, with Robinson and Queta, will be a much tougher test on the glass. And Drummond's defense in space, guarding pick-and-rolls against quick guards, has been his weakness for years. Teams will try to pull him away from the rim and attack him. In practice, that means the Knicks will probably use Drummond mostly in drop coverage, sitting back near the paint in the pick-and-roll and protecting the rim, rather than switching onto guards on the perimeter. That works against teams that rely on big men and drives. It's riskier against teams with elite pull-up shooters, who can punish a big who sits back. Against Philadelphia, with Maxey, Simons and Caldwell-Pope, that's a real concern, and it's part of why the matchups will dictate his minutes from night to night. So I wouldn't call him an upgrade on Robinson overall. Not on defense, and not over a full season.

Over a full season, the minutes question also includes the schedule. The Knicks will have back-to-backs, long road trips and stretches of four games in six nights. A thirty-three-year-old center who plays best in short bursts is exactly the kind of player a coach protects on the second night of a back-to-back. That's where Wiseman's minutes will probably come from, and why the third-center spot was worth fighting over in the preseason. Keeping Drummond fresh for March and April matters more than squeezing an extra five minutes out of him in October.

But here's my take. Drummond might be the Knicks' biggest X-factor this season. Not because he's their best player. Because the Knicks' identity is the offensive glass, the player who did that best just left, and they replaced him with the best rebounder by rate in NBA history, on a minimum contract. If Mike Brown finds the right minutes, opening night wasn't a ceiling. It was a preview.

And it ties back to the roster decisions we've covered all month. The Knicks couldn't keep Robinson under the second apron. They couldn't keep Bruce Brown. They've been hunting for a young center, Yves Missi and others, and getting told no. Drummond is the cheap answer that's actually working. On a team that has to find value at the bottom of the roster, that's huge.

So what should you watch?

One, Drummond's minutes on Friday in Boston. If Brown keeps him around eighteen to twenty, in shorter stints, that's the plan Brown described taking shape.

Two, the offensive glass against Robinson. Boston's bigs will try to take away exactly what Drummond did on opening night. If he wins that battle against the man he replaced, that's a statement.

Three, the kick-outs. When Drummond grabs an offensive rebound in traffic, does he force it or kick it out? The more he resets, the more sprays the Knicks get.

Four, Towns's fouls. If Towns stays out of foul trouble, Drummond's role shrinks to a normal backup. If he doesn't, Drummond's minutes and his stamina become a nightly storyline.

And five, Wiseman. Watch whether Brown uses him to give Drummond a breather in the second half, so the fade from opening night doesn't happen again.

Tell me in the comments. Is Drummond already a better fit for this team than Mitchell Robinson, or is it too early to say? And how many minutes a night would you give him?

One more thing before I go. If Drummond keeps this up, his one-year deal becomes its own story next summer. A thirty-four-year-old who rebounds like this would be a cheap re-signing target again, and after one season the Knicks would hold what are called Non-Bird rights, which let a team re-sign its own free agent for a modest raise over his previous salary, even when it's over the cap. That's a long way off. But it's another reason this minimum deal could end up mattering more than anyone expected in July.
