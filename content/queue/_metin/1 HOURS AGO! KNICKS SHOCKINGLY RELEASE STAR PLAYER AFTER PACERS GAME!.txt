Question one, before: Is Josh Hart hurt, or is he declining?

Here’s what the video laid out, and most of it is fair. On Friday against Indiana, Hart scored zero points in twenty-three minutes. According to Kenny Kluska’s November fourteenth report, Hart averaged about seven point three points, six point seven rebounds and three point eight assists through his first nine appearances. He was playing about thirty minutes a game, almost the same as last season, but scoring nearly five fewer points a night. His shooting splits were about thirty-six percent from the field, about thirty percent from three, and sixty percent from the line. And he hadn’t scored more than eight points in any of his last six games.

The video also pointed to the injuries. Hart told Stefan Bondy of the New York Post that a calf injury kept him out for about three weeks in September. Then he hurt his right ankle in training camp, and he aggravated it during the season. He’s already missed three games, two with ankle soreness and one with an illness.

And the video made a point I really respect. It drew a line between a theory and a fact. It said, we know the injuries were reported, but we don’t know that they’re the cause of his slump, and nobody has proven that connection. That’s exactly the right way to talk about it, and it’s a lot more honest than the title. A lot of videos would just say he’s hurt, or just say he’s done. This one said: we don’t know yet.

One correction, though. The video says Hart turned thirty-two on March sixth. He hasn’t yet. He was born on March sixth, nineteen ninety-five, so he’s thirty-one now, and he’ll turn thirty-two this coming March. It’s a small thing, but when we’re talking about age and decline, the age matters.

And the video is right about the workload. According to Kluska, since Hart’s first full season with the Knicks in twenty twenty-three twenty-four, only Mikal Bridges and Jalen Brunson have logged more total minutes on the team. Add a run to the Finals that ended in the middle of June, and that’s a lot of miles.

Question one, after: what do we know now?

Honestly, not much more yet, and that’s okay. One or two games after a scoreless night won’t settle a question like this. But here’s what I’d say. Look at how Hart’s game works. Sprinting in transition. Changing direction for rebounds. Accelerating past a defender. Taking contact at the rim. Every one of those things depends on a healthy calf and a healthy ankle. So the theory that the injuries are limiting him isn’t wild. It’s the most natural explanation. But the video is right that we shouldn’t treat it as a fact until the team or Hart says more.

The video’s suggestion was to find out how healthy Hart really is, and if the medical staff thinks the calf or ankle is limiting him, consider giving him more recovery time. It was careful to say that’s an opinion, not a report that the Knicks are planning to shut him down. I agree with that opinion. The goal isn’t to squeeze every minute out of Hart in November. It’s to have the best version of him in April, May and June.

And the Knicks have the depth to do it. Landry Shamet has been one of the best players on the team, with the best on-off numbers on the roster. Miles McBride has been excellent. Mohamed Diawara has earned minutes. Mike Brown even said recently that it’s hard to find minutes for Bridges, and sometimes for Hart, because Shamet and McBride are playing so well. So if Hart needs to play twenty-four minutes instead of thirty for a few weeks, the Knicks can handle it.

But rest isn’t magic either. The video said this too. If Hart is medically ready, he still has to find his finishing again, get his confidence back on his shot, and bring back the rebounding and transition pressure that make him so valuable. Some of that is physical. Some of it is rhythm. And some of it is just confidence, which comes back one made shot at a time.

Let me add something the video didn’t mention. Last season, Hart shot a career-best forty-one point three percent from three. He was a constant threat to get a triple-double. He guarded bigger players and rebounded like a big man at six-four. That’s the bar we’re comparing him to. When a player sets a standard that high, a normal slump can look like a collapse. And Hart has also been playing through a finger injury on his right hand that he’s said only a career-ending surgery would really fix. That’s a lot of things going on with one body.

So what should we watch for over the next few weeks to know which way this is going? Here’s my checklist. First, the fast breaks. If Hart starts finishing those one-on-none and one-on-one breaks again, that’s the clearest sign his legs are back. Second, the free throws. Sixty percent from the line is not normal for him. Free-throw shooting is mostly rhythm and comfort, and when it comes back, it usually means a player feels better overall. Third, the rebounds. Hart’s value has always been grabbing boards in traffic and starting the break himself. If his rebounding climbs back toward eight or nine a night, the rest of his game usually follows. And fourth, his minutes in the fourth quarter. If Brown starts trusting him to close games again, that tells you the coaches think he’s back.

And there’s a money side to this that’s worth mentioning. Hart makes about twenty point nine million dollars this season, and the Knicks hold a team option on him for next season worth about twenty-two point four million. With Karl-Anthony Towns’ contract question and Mikal Bridges’ rising salary, the Knicks have real decisions to make next summer. That’s why Hart’s slump gets so much attention. A healthy, productive Hart is worth every dollar of that option. A declining Hart makes that decision a lot harder. So the next couple of months aren’t just about wins and losses. They’re about the shape of this roster going forward.

There’s already been some noise about that. A fan on a Lakers podcast pitched a trade sending Hart to Los Angeles for three role players and three second-round picks, and even the Lakers reporter hosting the show said the Knicks could probably get more if they ever put Hart on the market. That’s not a report, and it’s not a real proposal from either team. But it shows how fast people start talking when a player like Hart slumps. The best way for Hart to end that talk is the way he always has: by outworking everybody on the floor.

So my answer to question one is the same as the video’s, just with a little more context. We don’t know yet. The injury explanation makes sense. The age question is fair. And the next month will tell us a lot more than one bad night against Indiana.

Before we move on, let me explain how to read these injury designations, because the video did a nice job with it and it’s worth repeating. Out means the player isn’t playing. Doubtful means he probably won’t. Questionable means it’s genuinely uncertain, and the team will usually decide closer to tip-off. Probable means he’ll most likely play. A player moving from out to questionable, like Barnes did, is progress. But it isn’t the same as being cleared. And with soft-tissue injuries like a groin or a calf, teams are often very careful, because coming back too early can make it worse. That’s true for Barnes, and it’s true for Hart too.

Question two, before: would Scottie Barnes play for Toronto?

The video said Barnes had missed four games with a left groin strain, took part in some of Saturday’s practice, and was upgraded to questionable for the game against the Knicks. It explained that questionable means uncertain, not cleared. And it laid out what Barnes would change if he played. He can bring the ball up, find teammates, attack closeouts, and use his size to see passing lanes. On defense, he has the length to bother ball handlers and guard different kinds of players. The video also warned that a groin strain can affect acceleration and lateral movement, so even if he played, he might not look like himself.

And it’s worth remembering who Barnes is, because he’s not just any player. He was the Rookie of the Year in twenty twenty-two. He’s become an All-Star. He’s six-seven with a huge wingspan, and he can play almost every position on the floor. On offense, he can bring the ball up, run a pick-and-roll, post up smaller defenders and find cutters. On defense, he can guard guards and forwards. Toronto had been struggling to create offense without him. So when he came back, the Raptors got their best playmaker back, even if he wasn’t at full speed.

Question two, after: he played. And he made one of the biggest shots of the night.

Barnes did return. And with the game on the line at the end of regulation, after the Knicks’ final possession of the fourth quarter didn’t work, Barnes hit a step-back three that helped force overtime. So the video was right to worry about him, even if he wasn’t at a hundred percent. When he’s on the floor, Toronto has a creator who can make big plays.

And that win mattered more than usual, because coming into it, the Knicks were one and six in clutch games this season. Their only clutch win had come against Chicago. Last season, closing games was their identity. Brunson led the league in clutch points for the third straight year and led the playoffs in fourth-quarter scoring. So beating Toronto in overtime, after a heartbreaking end to regulation, was a sign that the old version of this team is still in there.

It also extended a streak that’s worth knowing. The Knicks have now beaten the Raptors fourteen straight times, going back to December of twenty twenty-three. That’s the same month the Knicks traded RJ Barrett and Immanuel Quickley to Toronto for Anunoby. Quickley led the Raptors with seventeen points on Sunday. Anunoby closed the game.

Let me also mention the minutes, because it ties back to the Hart question. Brunson played forty-one minutes in Toronto, his most of the season. That’s a lot for a player who had wrist surgery this summer, in a game in November. It made sense in a must-win game that went to overtime. But it’s exactly why Hart’s health matters so much. When Hart is at full strength, he takes pressure off Brunson. He rebounds and pushes the ball, so Brunson doesn’t have to bring it up against a set defense every time. He makes the extra pass. He guards the other team’s best wing so Brunson can save energy on defense. Without that version of Hart, Brunson has to do even more. And forty-one minutes a night isn’t sustainable over eighty-two games.

So what connects the two stories? The video said it well. Hart’s slump shows how much a team loses when a high-energy player isn’t at his best. Barnes’ return shows how much a versatile two-way player can change an opponent. Different players, different situations, but both matter far beyond the points column. That’s a good lesson for how we judge players in general. The box score is only part of the story.

And I’d add one more connection. On Sunday, the Knicks won a game that came down to energy and extra possessions. Towns had seventeen rebounds. The Knicks crashed the glass.
