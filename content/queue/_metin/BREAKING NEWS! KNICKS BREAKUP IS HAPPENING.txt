Now, the real story. Because Windhorst did write something, and it's interesting.

On October 16th, Brian Windhorst wrote about the Towns situation for ESPN. Here's what he said. If Towns isn't extended, he could opt out and become a free agent next summer. And then: "There definitely are teams, starting with the Golden State Warriors, LA Clippers and Atlanta Hawks," that would probably "line up to make big offers." And: "This is what other teams are studying with the Knicks."

So Windhorst named three teams. But look at the framing. It's conditional. If Towns isn't extended. If he opts out. Then those teams would likely be interested. That's a very different statement from "Towns is being traded to Golden State." It's about free agency next summer, not a trade this season. And there's nothing in it about any team being "the most serious," and nothing about Shams confirming anything.

In fact, the same week, Windhorst said he expects the Knicks and Towns to eventually reach an agreement, despite the paused talks. So the reporter whose work the fake video is built on is actually leaning the other way.

Windhorst also added a detail that most people missed. If Towns walked, he estimated the Knicks could have roughly forty million dollars of room to build around Brunson. That's the other side of the coin. Losing Towns would hurt. But it wouldn't leave the Knicks with nothing.

Now let's look at the three teams Windhorst named, because each one has a catch, and a Daily Knicks piece made a sharp point about them: none of them is a clear title contender.

Golden State. Steph Curry will be thirty-nine entering next season. The Warriors would probably have to move Jimmy Butler and Draymond Green to create real space. And as we discussed, Butler is coming off a torn ACL and Kristaps Porziņģis is out indefinitely with an illness. A Towns-Curry pairing sounds exciting. The Clippers. They're probably the team most likely to clear max-level space. But they're also an organization in turmoil, with Lawrence Frank and Steve Ballmer both suspended after the investigation tied to Kawhi Leonard, and with very few of their own first-round picks for years. They just traded Kawhi to Toronto. A rebuilding franchise isn't usually where a thirty-one-year-old who says his only goal is winning another title wants to go.

Atlanta. This is the most interesting one. The Hawks were the only team to beat the Knicks twice in last year's playoffs, so they know exactly what Towns can do. They're expected to have cap flexibility from expiring contracts. But there are questions about how a Towns and Jalen Johnson frontcourt would defend, and whether Atlanta has a lead creator like Brunson to make it all work.

And remember why the Bird rights matter so much here. If Towns becomes a free agent, the Knicks can offer him five years with raises of up to eight percent a year. Any other team can offer only four years, with raises of up to five percent. So even if Golden State, the Clippers or Atlanta matched the Knicks' starting salary, New York could still offer an extra year and bigger raises. Over a full contract, that difference can be worth tens of millions of dollars. That's a structural advantage the fake video never mentions.

So Towns would have options next summer. That's real leverage. But the options are teams that can offer money, not teams that can offer him a better chance to win than New York. And an anonymous executive told the Post last week that even the teams best positioned for cap space could probably offer around forty-five to fifty million a year, which is less than the Knicks can pay, with the Knicks holding his Bird rights. That's why I think the Knicks' patience isn't as reckless as it looks.

Now, the money. Because the fake video made a claim that matters: that Brunson "is going to need a three-hundred-million-dollar extension next summer," and that the Knicks can't pay both him and Towns. Let's check that.

Brunson signed a four-year, hundred-and-fifty-six-and-a-half-million-dollar extension in July of 2024. It runs through 2027-28, with a player option for 2028-29. By taking a shorter deal at less than the max back then, he reportedly left tens of millions on the table to help the Knicks build this roster. Everybody in New York knows his next deal will be big.

But how big, and when? According to Stefan Bondy of the Post, Brunson could sign a four-year extension worth about two hundred and fifty-seven million in 2027, or wait until 2028, when he could be eligible for a five-year deal worth around four hundred and seventeen million. On Windhorst's podcast this summer, ESPN's Vincent Goodwill predicted Brunson would get the two-hundred-eighty-to-three-hundred-million range he's expected to command, "and then some." So "three hundred million next summer" is in the right neighborhood as a prediction, but it isn't a fixed number, and the timing is a choice Brunson will make.

And let's be clear about why Brunson's next deal will be so large. In 2024, he chose a four-year extension at less than his maximum, reportedly leaving a significant amount of money on the table compared with waiting for a bigger deal. That choice helped the Knicks keep building a contender around him. Then he won Finals MVP. Nobody in the league expects him to take a discount again, and he shouldn't have to. When the time comes, the Knicks will pay him. That's the one contract decision in this whole story that isn't in doubt.

And here's the part the fake video got half right. The Knicks' payroll is tight. This season, the five starters alone make about a hundred and ninety-two million dollars. Brunson's next deal will be much bigger than his current one. Towns wants a deal close to the max. Hart has a twenty-two million team option next season. McBride is in a contract year. And ownership has drawn a hard line at the second apron. You can't fit all of that at full price under that line. Something will have to give.

The Knicks' offer, about two hundred million over four years, would start at around forty-four or forty-five million in its first season with standard raises. And the timeline matters. The extension cutoff for multi-year contracts passed on October 19th. So what does next summer actually look like? Here's the calendar. In late June, Towns has to decide on his player option for 2027-28. The Knicks have to decide on Hart's team option and on Kolek's. McBride becomes a free agent if he hasn't been extended or traded. And Brunson's eligibility for his next extension comes into play. That's an enormous amount of decision-making packed into a few weeks, and it's why this front office wants to see a full season before committing. Every one of those decisions affects the others under the second apron.

And what about the option nobody in ownership wants to talk about: crossing the second apron? Let me be fair to Dolan's position. A team above the second apron can't combine salaries in trades, can't use the taxpayer mid-level exception, can't take back more salary than it sends out, and if it stays above the line for too many seasons, its future first-round pick gets pushed to the end of the round. On top of that, the luxury tax bill becomes enormous. For a team with an aging core, those restrictions can make it almost impossible to refresh the roster. So staying under the line isn't just about money. It's about keeping the tools to fix problems. But plenty of championship teams have decided that a year or two above the line is worth it while their window is open. That's the real debate. Not whether Towns is leaving tomorrow, but whether ownership will bend if keeping the title core requires it.
