Let's run his story, because the career arc is the whole point.

Ochai Agbaji is twenty-six years old. He was the fourteenth overall pick in the 2022 draft out of Kansas, where he was the Most Outstanding Player of a national championship run. He started his career in Utah. Then, in the middle of his second season, the Jazz traded him to Toronto — in the same deal that sent Kelly Olynyk north, a name you've heard plenty of times on this channel.

He stayed in Toronto until last season, when his contract was effectively moved to Brooklyn and their young, developing roster.

So think about what that means. Three franchises in four years. A lottery pick who got moved before his rookie contract was even half over. And a player who has spent most of his professional life on rebuilding teams where nobody was playing for anything in March.

That's not the profile of a bust. That's the profile of a player who has never once been put in a structure designed to win.

And that's exactly what New York is betting on.

Because look at the blueprint. Landry Shamet was a first-round pick who got traded within months, bounced across six franchises, and carried the "useful but replaceable" label everywhere he went. Garrison Mathews was a similar kind of gamble. The Knicks' logic with both was simple.

Don't assume a player has reached his ceiling just because his career hasn't gone the way it was supposed to. Put him into a competitive structure. Surround him with high-level players. Put him under a coach who has proven he can get results. And see what happens.

That's the Agbaji bet in one sentence.

Now, what does New York actually need from him? Because this is where people get it wrong.

Nobody expects him to become a major scorer. Nobody expects the ball in his hands. The Knicks already have players for that — Jalen Brunson, Mikal Bridges, OG Anunoby, Josh Hart, Karl-Anthony Towns. That's the center of this whole project and it isn't changing.

The question is whether Agbaji can be the dependable reserve who defends, runs in transition, plays without the ball, and punishes defenses when he gets open looks from three.

A traditional three-and-D wing, basically. And he has the athleticism for it.

But here's where I want to be more honest than most of the coverage, because the Shamet comparison is flattering and it's also a little misleading.

When Shamet arrived, nobody questioned the shot. The shot was the one thing everybody agreed on. What he needed was stability, a defined role, and a team that trusted him.

Agbaji's question is different. For him, the shot is the question.

Three-and-D only works if the three is real. Without it, you're just a good athlete who defends, and defenses will happily leave you alone in the corner and send an extra body at Brunson. With it, you're one of the most valuable role-player profiles in the entire league.

So if you want to know whether this experiment works, don't watch his highlight dunks in preseason. Watch one thing. Catch-and-shoot threes from the corner, off Brunson drives. If he's knocking those down at a respectable clip in October, he's a real candidate. If he isn't, no amount of athleticism will save the roster spot.

And he's twenty-six, which matters too. That's still young enough that "he hasn't found his level yet" is believable. But it's the last age where that sentence is believable. This camp isn't just an opportunity for him. It's close to the final one.

Now let's talk about the competition, because it's real and it's the reason these signings are more interesting than they look.

The Knicks didn't bring Agbaji in alone. They put him in a room with John Konchar and Bruce Brown, and all three are fighting for extremely limited wing space.

And the three profiles could not be more different.

Konchar is the connective wing. He rebounds far above his size, he defends, he keeps the ball moving, and he never needs to dominate a possession. He makes the players around him slightly better without you ever really noticing why.

Bruce Brown brings championship experience, real secondary ball-handling, and the versatility to guard multiple positions. He's the only one of the three who has already done this exact job for a title team.

And Agbaji is the youngest and the most explosive of the group — better in transition than either of them, with the purest three-and-D profile of the three.

So the front office isn't just asking who's best. They're asking which skill set this specific rotation is missing. Do you want the connector, the ball-handler, or the athlete?

My honest read? Brown starts camp as the favorite, because he's the safest and he's done it before. Konchar is the smartest fit on paper. And Agbaji is the one with the highest ceiling, and therefore the one who needs to show the most to win the job.

That's a genuine competition. Not a formality.

And the same thing is happening in the frontcourt. James Wiseman and Drew Eubanks are competing for the third center spot behind Towns and Andre Drummond. Wiseman — the second overall pick in 2020 — brings size, athleticism, and upside. Eubanks brings an established resume as a reserve center and a much clearer history of handling physical minutes in the paint.

And then there's Liam Robbins, who looks like he's on more of a developmental path — more likely competing for a two-way contract than a standard roster spot.

So picture the whole board. Agbaji, Konchar, and Brown fighting for wing depth. Wiseman and Eubanks fighting for the third center job. Robbins staying in the conversation through the developmental route.

And sitting over all of it is the part that doesn't show up in any box score.

New York technically has two open roster spots. But as SNY's Ian Begley has highlighted, the team cannot simply fill both with veteran minimum contracts without crossing into second apron territory.

So this isn't only about who plays the best basketball in camp. The front office also has to think about roster math, contracts, and financial restrictions — which means a player could outplay the field and still not make it, simply because the money doesn't work.

And here's a detail from last year that explains why this camp is harder than the last one.

Last season, New York opened roster space more easily than people expected. And part of that came from a situation nobody saw coming — Malcolm Brogdon announced his retirement after nine years in the NBA. That freed up a spot out of nowhere.

You cannot plan around that kind of luck twice.

And that's why these Exhibit 9 contracts are so convenient. If Agbaji doesn't impress, New York has a simple way out. These deals are low-risk by design. Players can be waived easily, and if a player gets hurt in camp, the team's obligation is capped at a small fixed amount — reported at around fifteen thousand dollars.

From Agbaji's side, it's the first time in his career he's walking into a building where the goal is a championship, not a draft pick.
