First, what do we mean by an enforcer?

It's an old-fashioned word, and it's easy to picture someone who just shoves people. That's not what the modern version is. A modern enforcer is the player who makes the other team's life physically uncomfortable for forty-eight minutes. He sets screens that actually hit someone. He boxes out every time a shot goes up. He meets drivers at the rim so they think twice about coming back. He protects his stars, not by fighting, but by making sure the other team pays a price for every hard foul and every cheap shot. And he sets the tone in practice, so that the bench plays harder too.

And Knicks fans know this role better than almost anyone. The great New York teams of the nineties were built on it. Charles Oakley. Anthony Mason. Players whose job was to make every trip into the paint expensive for the other team, and to make sure Patrick Ewing never felt alone in a fight. That identity, tough, physical, unafraid, is part of what the Garden expects from its big men. It's part of why Robinson was so beloved. And it's exactly the identity Mike Brown is trying to rebuild.

Every championship team has someone who does at least part of that. For this Knicks team, for years, a lot of that job belonged to Mitchell Robinson. He was the offensive rebounder, the rim deterrent, the guy whose presence changed shots without blocking them. And this summer, he left for Boston on a three-year deal worth about forty-seven point four million dollars. The Knicks let him go rather than cross the second apron.

And now look at what the head coach has been saying. After the first preseason game, a hundred and twenty to ninety-seven loss in Philadelphia, Mike Brown said the thing that bothered him most was the lack of physicality, up and down the roster. Not on the ball. Not in defensive positioning. And then he said he can't coach it. Players have to find it individually. That's how you find minutes, he said. That's how you make the team.

That's a coach asking, publicly, for an enforcer. He just didn't use the word.

And it's worth remembering why physicality wins in May, not just in October. Playoff series are long. Teams see the same sets again and again, and the margins come down to second-chance points, free throws and who gets pushed off their spot. A team that wins the physical battle gets extra possessions, and extra possessions were a huge part of how this team won last year. Losing that edge is the quiet risk in losing Robinson.

So why the timing? Why does it matter so much before the Sixers game specifically?

Because Philadelphia is the first test. They have LeBron. They have Joel Embiid when he's healthy. They beat the Knicks by twenty-three in the preseason opener, even without their biggest stars, and they did it with energy and transition. And this is the team the Knicks swept in the second round last spring, a series where Andre Drummond, then a Sixer, said it felt like an away game the whole way. Philadelphia will remember that. Opening night will be physical.

And one more note from that preseason game, because it matters and because it's sad. Ariel Hukporti, who spent time with the Knicks before signing in Philadelphia, tore his Achilles in that game. He'll miss the entire season. Whatever your feelings about any team, that's a terrible way for a young big man's season to end, and we wish him a full recovery.

Now, the name you keep hearing.

A lot of the videos making the rounds use the word enforcer because it's the nickname people gave Isaiah Stewart in Detroit. "Beef Stew." The rugged big man who ranked among the league's best defensive frontcourt players. Before injuries limited his last season, his defended field-goal percentage, the rate at which opponents made shots against him, was around forty-three percent, which ranked fifth in the league.

Here's the problem with those videos. Isaiah Stewart doesn't play for Detroit anymore. In late June, the Pistons traded him to the Memphis Grizzlies for three future second-round picks, as they reshaped their roster. And this week, Memphis announced that Stewart has a moderate left ankle sprain, suffered in their preseason opener. He'll be re-evaluated in two weeks, and he'll miss the Grizzlies' regular-season opener.

So when someone tells you the Knicks just landed "the enforcer" before the Sixers game, ask them one question. Which team was he traded from? Because if they say Detroit, they haven't read the news since June.

Could the Knicks ever look at Stewart in Memphis? Maybe, later in the season. But right now he's hurt, the Grizzlies just acquired him, and the Knicks' trade rules make an October deal very hard, as we'll see in a minute.

So let's look at where the enforcer is actually going to come from. And I think the answer starts inside the building.

Before we go through the bigs, one name we shouldn't forget. Karl-Anthony Towns is the starting centre. He isn't a classic enforcer, and nobody's asking him to be. But against Washington, he defended Anthony Davis without fouling, and when he was on Davis, Davis had a much harder time. That's a form of physicality too. If Towns can hold up against Embiid and the other big bodies in the East without early foul trouble, the backup doesn't have to do as much.

Option one: Andre Drummond.

He's already the backup centre, and through two preseason games, he's been the most physical big the Knicks have outside of Towns. In the opener, he had three blocks. Against Washington, he played twenty minutes and had nine rebounds, five of them offensive, which was a game high, plus four assists. He came into camp lighter. He's moving better than a lot of people expected, including me. And he signed for about three point nine million dollars on a one-year deal.

The honest limit: he isn't the rim deterrent Robinson was. He contests, but he doesn't change shots just by standing there. But if the job is boxing out, crashing the glass and putting a big body on people, Drummond is already doing it.

Option two: Drew Eubanks.

Eubanks is the most naturally physical of the camp centres. He rolls hard, he finishes through contact, he blocks shots, and he plays like a veteran who knows his job is to make the other team uncomfortable. Ian Begley called him the safest choice for the last roster spot. His problem so far is opportunity. He barely registered in the opener, one point and one rebound, and he didn't play against Washington. Monday against Minnesota is his real audition. If he plays with the physicality Brown is asking for, his case gets a lot stronger.

Option three: Tony Bradley.

Bradley's whole case is the old-school stuff. The box-out, the screen set at the right angle, the willingness to wall people off. Mike Brown said when he signed that Bradley has hurt the Knicks in the past. In the twenty twenty-five Eastern Conference Finals with Indiana, he was a plus fifteen in limited minutes. He hasn't played much this preseason, and Begley has narrowed the race to Eubanks and Wiseman. But if Brown is truly grading physicality, Bradley is exactly the type of player that grade favours. Monday matters for him too.

Option four: James Wiseman.

Wiseman is the most talented of the three camp bigs, and he's been the most impressive. Eight points, five rebounds and a block in the opener. Four points and two blocks in nine minutes against Washington, catching lobs from Jose Alvarado, the only backup centre who played that night. He gives the Knicks a vertical threat they lost with Robinson.

But here's the honest question in the context of this video. Is Wiseman an enforcer? Physicality has been the knock on him for years. He's quick, he's long, he blocks shots. Whether he'll set the hard screen and take the hard foul is still an open question. If Brown wants a lob threat, Wiseman is the answer. If Brown wants a bruiser, Eubanks or Bradley might be.

That's the real decision in front of the coaching staff. Not who's best on paper. What kind of big man this team needs most.

Now, the trade options. Because some of you are going to say, forget the camp guys, go get a real one.

I understand. And there are real names out there. But let's go through them honestly, with the rules the Knicks live under.

The rules first. The Knicks are about three point two million dollars below the second apron, which owner James Dolan has said he won't cross. Above the first apron, they can't take back more salary than they send out. Most of this summer's free-agent signings can't be traded until December fifteenth. And as of this week, the Knicks have officially paused contract extension talks with Towns, Hart, McBride and Kolek until later in the season. All of that makes an October trade hard.

Myles Turner in Milwaukee. The best fit on paper. A rim protector who shoots threes. But he makes about twenty-eight million a year with a fifteen percent trade kicker. To match that in October, the Knicks would essentially have to send out Hart, McBride, Dadiet and Kolek together. That's not a pre-opener move.

Daniel Gafford in Dallas. A true rim-runner and shot-blocker, around eighteen million a year, and Dallas reportedly wants a first-round pick. The Knicks only have one realistically tradable first, in twenty thirty-three.

Kel'el Ware in Milwaukee. The one whose salary works. About four point six five million this season, which Kolek and Dadiet could match. But he's twenty-two, the Bucks are rebuilding around young players, and they'd want picks and young talent. And the knock on Ware in Miami was inconsistent effort, which is the opposite of what an enforcer is supposed to bring.

And Isaiah Stewart in Memphis. Injured, newly acquired, and not realistically available in the next nine days.

So the honest answer is that a trade before the Sixers game is very unlikely. If a trade for a physical big happens, the realistic windows are after December fifteenth, when more of the roster becomes tradable, or at the February deadline, when prices change.

Which means the enforcer for opening night is almost certainly already on the roster.

And there's a deadline on all of this. Before the regular season starts, NBA teams have to trim to a maximum of fifteen players on standard contracts, plus their three two-way players. The Knicks have thirteen guaranteed deals and, because of the apron, room for realistically one more. So in the next week, the coaching staff and the front office have to decide between Wiseman, Eubanks and Bradley, and the wings, Bruce Brown and Ochai Agbaji. Four of those five won't be on the opening-night roster. That's why every preseason minute right now is an audition.

And Bruce Brown is worth a mention in the toughness conversation too. He isn't a centre, but against Washington he defended well enough that the Wizards passed out of his matchup, drew a foul instead of giving up an easy layup, and forced a late turnover. He told the Post the second apron is why he's fighting for a job. A wing who plays like that is exactly the type of player who raises the physical temperature of a second unit. The problem is that he'd almost certainly need a trade to open a second roster spot.

So who do I think it is?

I think it's a combination, and here's why. Drummond is going to take most of the backup minutes. He's earned that with two strong games. That covers a lot of the enforcer job, the rebounding and the body. The question is who takes the last roster spot behind him.

And here, the evidence and the coach's words point in slightly different directions. The evidence from two games says Wiseman. He's played better, and he's the only backup centre who got minutes on Wednesday. The coach's words, physicality is how you make the team, point a little more towards a player like Eubanks. Monday against Minnesota is where those two things either line up or don't. If Eubanks gets real minutes and plays physical, it becomes a genuine decision. If he doesn't, Wiseman is very likely the fourteenth man.

There's one more enforcer on this team I want to mention, and he isn't a centre. Josh Hart. He rebounds like a big man. He's the emotional engine of the roster. He's playing through a damaged finger rather than getting the surgery that would end his career. When we talk about toughness on this team, Hart is the standard. He was the best Knick on the floor against Washington. If the bench wants to know what Brown means by physicality, they can watch number three.

And OG Anunoby has been crashing the offensive glass in both preseason games, three offensive rebounds in the opener, two against Washington. When your star wings are doing the dirty work, it raises the floor for everyone.

So maybe the better way to put it is this. The Knicks don't need one enforcer. They need a roster where everyone plays a little more like one. That's exactly what Brown has been asking for since the first day of camp.

Now let me give you the counterargument, because it's a fair one.

The Knicks won a title with Robinson. They know how much a true rim protector and offensive rebounder mattered. Drummond is good but older and not the same defender. Wiseman is a talented project.
