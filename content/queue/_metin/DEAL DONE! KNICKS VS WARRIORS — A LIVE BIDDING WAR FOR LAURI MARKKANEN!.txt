Now, the tells. And I want to go through these slowly, because every one of them is a lesson you can use on the next fake, and there will be a next one.

Tell number one. The Shams reporting reaches you through a television personality.

The video says Stephen A. Smith reported this, citing Shams. Then the narrator tells you Stephen A. has been repeating it on air all morning. And a few minutes later he admits he's paraphrasing Stephen A., and then he adds, in his own words, "but not by much."

That is the giveaway. A real report has a first source you can go and read. When Shams breaks a trade offer, it goes up on his own account, with his own words, and every outlet in the country copies it within minutes. You do not need a middleman. If the only place a Shams report exists is inside someone else's description of a morning show, there is a very good chance the report does not exist.

And look at the quote the narrator attributes to Stephen A. He supposedly said any team that doesn't hand over the moon for Markkanen is not serious about winning. Then the narrator spends two minutes politely correcting a sentence he told you he was inventing. That's a strange thing to do. You are arguing with a ghost.

Tell number two. The insider quote that sounds like a sales pitch.

The video reads out a line from an unnamed rival executive: the Knicks would love to get Lauri in there with OG and Brunson, he's the shooting this team desperately needs. Then it asks you to notice that this didn't come from a Knicks employee. A rival executive said it, the narrator tells you, because the fit is that obviously good.

Look at that sentence. That is not how front offices talk to reporters. Executives who go on the record, even anonymously, tell you things that happened. Who called whom. What was offered. What the other side said back. They do not describe how much they admire your roster. That's a sentence designed to make a fan nod along.

It is the same test we used on the Jamal Murray video. Insiders report actions. Fakes report feelings. When the quote tells you why somebody is excited, in language that would fit on a poster, treat it as marketing until you find the original.

Tell number three. The deadline that already passed.

Near the end, the video says that on this very same day Shams is also reporting the Knicks are monitoring Jalen Duren's sign-and-trade situation, with a deadline of eleven fifty-nine tonight.

That one is easy to check, and it doesn't hold up. A video that presents it as breaking news today is either badly out of date or deliberately recycling an old headline to make the day sound busier than it was.

The same sentence says the Knicks are, quote, the most frequently mentioned preferred destination if Orlando ever moves Paolo Banchero. When one paragraph contains a stale deadline, an unnamed destination claim, and a trade offer nobody can find, the right move is to doubt the whole paragraph.

Tell number six, and it's the quietest one. The scene.

Okay. That's the fact check. Now let me do something more useful than just knocking it down, because I don't want to be the guy who says "fake" and leaves.

Say the story were true. Say Utah really did pick up the phone tomorrow. Is Markkanen what this team needs?

Start with the claim that sits at the center of the whole video: the Knicks have a shooting hole. Let's test that.

Karl-Anthony Towns is the starting center. He is one of the best shooting big men the league has ever seen, a career forty-percent three-point shooter at seven feet. Every conversation we have had on this channel about his contract has come back to the same point: five-out centers who work next to your lead guard don't exist on the open market. The video tells you nobody on the championship roster is a seven-footer who can space the floor. The starting center is exactly that.

Then OG Anunoby. The video says he shoots thirty-six percent for his career, which is fine but not scary. That's a fair career number. But in the last video, the Murray one, we pulled the playoff numbers. In the championship run Anunoby shot fifty-six percent from the field and forty-nine percent from three. If that's a hole, it's a hole that held up the trophy.

And Mikal Bridges, remember, shot fifty-six percent in the playoffs too, with three assists for every turnover.

So the honest version of the shooting argument is narrower than the video makes it sound. The Knicks don't have a shooting problem in the starting five. They have a question about what happens when Towns sits, and about who stretches the floor in the minutes without Brunson. That's a different problem, and it has different solutions. We have been through them on this channel. A backup big who can hold up for six minutes. A bench guard who can create. A shooter or two in the camp competition.

Now the other half of the question. Could Markkanen play next to Towns?

On paper, yes, and I'll give the video that much. Two seven-footers who can shoot is a five-out look that very few teams can put on the floor. Brown could play Towns at the five, Markkanen at the four, and let Bridges and Anunoby switch everything on the perimeter. Brunson drives, the defense collapses, and every kick-out goes to a legitimate shooter. That is a beautiful lineup on a whiteboard.

But there are three problems that the video never mentions.

Problem one is money. Markkanen's salary is well north of forty million dollars a year. Towns is still unsigned, the two sides reported as tens of millions apart. If you're a front office that is fighting for a discount on your starting center, you do not simultaneously absorb another forty-plus million in salary. That isn't aggressive team-building. It's choosing to run two crises at once.

Problem two is the apron. We have explained it on this channel all month, so let me say it briefly. The Knicks have somewhere around three point two million dollars of room before the second apron, and the ownership has made clear it won't cross it. Above that line, you lose the ability to aggregate salaries in a trade, which means you can't send two players out and treat their salaries as one. You also have to send out at least as much salary as you take back.

Now do the arithmetic on the video's own offer. Josh Hart makes somewhere around twenty million dollars. Markkanen makes more than double that. Even if Dalton Knecht were a Knick, which he isn't, his rookie-scale salary would barely move the needle. To match a salary in the mid-forties, New York would have to send out another starter, someone like Bridges or Anunoby, and that is the point where the trade stops making sense for anyone. You are no longer adding a shooter. You are swapping two starters for one.

That's the idea we've been repeating all fall. Under this apron, the Knicks can't add. They can only swap. And a swap that costs you Hart and a starting wing to get a shooter who plays next to Towns is a swap that breaks the thing that won the title.

Problem three is defence, and I want to be careful, because I don't want to overstate it. Markkanen is a skilled and improving defender, but he has never been an anchor. The Knicks' structure is built so that Anunoby and Bridges can cover for limited defenders, and that works. But every time you add a high-usage scorer next to Towns, you're asking the same two wings to cover two bigs who aren't rim protectors. That's the kind of detail that looks fine in October and shows up in the second round.

None of this means Markkanen is a bad player. He's an excellent one. It means the fit is less obvious than the video says, and the price is much steeper than the video's version of the price.

Now the steelman. And I want to give it fairly, because there is a real argument here.

The argument goes like this. Nobody has repeated as champion in eight years. The reason is almost never that the champion got worse. It's that the champion stood still while every other contender spent the summer working out how to beat it. From that view, a front office that doesn't at least pick up the phone for a star is leaving something on the table. Aggression is the right instinct. Sentiment is the enemy.

I agree with the instinct. I really do. If Leon Rose called Utah to ask the price, I would not be upset about it. Calling is free. Asking is how you find out where the market is.

What I disagree with is the idea that this specific trade is the answer. There is a difference between adding a star to your core and swapping two of your best five for a star who doesn't fit the other three. This front office has been disciplined about that difference all summer. They let Mitchell Robinson leave rather than pay him a number that would have broken the structure. They spent the offseason on camp invitations and waiver claims rather than a blockbuster. They are not a team that panics.

And there's an argument on Utah's side too, which the video skips completely.

Put yourself in the Jazz's seat. You've spent four years tanking. You've finally built a young core. You've got a player who just averaged a career-high in scoring and who, if you believe the reporting, is now the centrepiece of a team you think can make the playoffs. What do you actually want in return for him?

You want young players with upside. You want multiple firsts. You want something that makes you better right now, because you've just decided you are in the competing business.

What New York can offer is a wing who rebounds like a big man, a player who isn't even on the roster, and one first-round pick, in twenty thirty-three. If you're Utah, that is not an auction. That is a polite rejection.

Which brings me to something I think is worth saying about all of these fake trades. They are almost always designed to thrill one fan base rather than to be accepted by two front offices. The test isn't "would a Knicks fan love this?" The test is "would the other team's general manager sign off on it?" When you run that test, most of these ideas fall apart in about thirty seconds.

Let me also address the Ewing comparison, because the video leans on it and I want to be fair about it.

Nineteen eighty-five, Patrick Ewing comes to New York in the draft, and an era begins. The narrator says the shape of that story rhymes with a Markkanen trade. I understand the instinct. Fans want to believe a single move can change a franchise's trajectory.

But Ewing was drafted. He was not traded for, and he wasn't a veteran who needed a fit. And the Knicks of this era have already done the thing. They won the championship. They did it with a roster built around balance and a coach who held them to a standard. The reason this team succeeded is not that it was missing a piece. It's that five players fit together and nobody needed to be a hero. A story that begins with "they need a savior" is a story about a team that doesn't exist.

Now, how do you protect yourself the next time one of these shows up? Because there will be another, probably next week.

Step one: find the first source. Not the video that quotes the report, not the video that quotes the video. The original post. If it's Shams, it's on his account. If it's The Athletic, there's a link. If you can't find it in two minutes, assume it isn't there.

Step two: look at who is named as the coach. We covered this before. If the script treats anyone other than Mike Brown as the Knicks' head coach, it's a recycled template.

Step three: read the quotes as if they were a transcript. Are people reporting events, or are they describing feelings? Real insiders say what happened. Fakes say how everyone is excited.

Step four: check the roster. If a trade includes a player the team doesn't have, you can stop reading.

Step five: check the date. A deadline that says "tonight" on a story that has already been resolved is a sign the video is stitching together old headlines.

Those five steps will save you from most of what's out there.
