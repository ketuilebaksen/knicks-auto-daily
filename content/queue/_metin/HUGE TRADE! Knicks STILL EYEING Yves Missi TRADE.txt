New Orleans wants it. Or something close to it. And until New York is willing to hand it over, Yves Missi is not coming to Madison Square Garden.

That's the real story behind this rumor that won't go away. Let's get into it.

Shout out to The Third Apron podcast, where cap expert Yossi Gozlan talked with Jake Madison of Locked On Pelicans, and to Sports Illustrated for putting the key part of it out there.

Here's the short version. Missi is still someone the Knicks could be eyeing, along with young Hornets big man Moussa Diabaté. But Madison was quick to point out the obvious problem. Missi is a third-year player on a cheap rookie contract, and he's very unlikely to be moved if New York's best offer is one or two second-round picks.

Nobody is calling him untouchable. As both of them said, anyone can be moved if the price is right. But unless the Knicks can put together a complicated pick swap, or gamble their only tradable first-rounder in 2033, this is going to be much harder than people assume.

So let's talk about that 2033 pick, because most coverage mentions it and then moves on. It deserves more than that.

Why is 2033 the only one? Because this front office spent its draft capital building a champion. The Mikal Bridges trade and the Karl-Anthony Towns trade both cost real first-round picks. And the league has a rule — often called the Stepien rule — that stops a team from trading away its first-round picks in consecutive future drafts. You have to leave gaps.

So after everything New York has already traded, 2033 is what's left. It's the last bullet in the chamber.

And here's what makes it complicated. A pick seven years out is the most uncertain asset in basketball. Nobody knows what this roster looks like in 2033. Brunson will be in his late thirties. Towns will be retired or close to it. That pick could be the fifth pick in the draft.

Which is exactly why New Orleans wants it — and exactly why New York doesn't want to give it up.

Now here's the part of this story that the reporting mentioned but nobody has really explained. And I think it's the single most important thing in this whole conversation.

There's a future extension question.

Missi is in his third year on a rookie contract. That contract does not last forever. Whichever team has him will eventually have to decide whether to pay him — first through a rookie-scale extension, and then, if that doesn't happen, as a restricted free agent.

So think about what the Knicks would actually be buying. Two more cheap seasons, and then a raise.

And when does that raise arrive? Right around the same window where this team is already trying to fit a Towns extension, a Josh Hart extension, and possibly a Deuce McBride extension under the second apron. We've done the math on this channel all month. There is barely room for one of those. Now add a young center due a real payday.

That's not a reason to avoid the trade. It's a reason to think very carefully about the total price. Because the real cost of Yves Missi isn't just the 2033 pick. It's the pick, plus what he costs when the cheap years end.

Nobody talks about that part. They should.

Alright, so what is New York actually offering? Nobody has confirmed it, but based on everything reported, the package is almost certainly Pacôme Dadiet plus a few second-round picks.

And here's the problem with that. The Pelicans don't want Dadiet. They don't particularly want second-round picks either. They're rebuilding, and rebuilding teams want unprotected firsts or pick swaps — not a young wing whose own team is trying to salary-dump him.

That's a fundamental mismatch. It isn't about negotiating harder. The two sides want completely different things.

And now let's name the bigger issue, because I think it explains this entire offseason.

New York is paying the champion's tax.

If the Knicks hadn't just won a title, I think this trade gets done. New Orleans would take a reasonable package and move on. But you just won the championship. So now every front office in the league looks at your offer and asks a very simple question: why would I make the best team in the East better without getting a lot in return?

And honestly? I understand it. If I ran the Pelicans, I'd do the same thing. You don't hand a young, cheap, defensively elite center to the defending champs for spare parts.

So the price goes up. Not because Missi got better overnight, but because the buyer's name changed.

And it's worth remembering why the Knicks keep coming back to Missi and Diabaté specifically, instead of just signing another veteran big.

Because the front office knows the answer to this problem isn't in free agency. Everybody good is already employed. What's left in September is exactly what we've been watching all month — camp bodies on non-guaranteed deals.

A trade is different. A trade gets you a young center who can grow with this team. Someone who becomes the primary backup behind Towns now, and potentially the starting center later, when Towns is in his thirties and this roster needs its next chapter.

That's the whole idea. It isn't about surviving this season. It's about not having this same conversation every single summer for the next five years.

Which brings us to the obvious question. What should the Knicks do?

My answer is the same one I gave earlier this month, and I'm not going to abandon it now that the story is back in the news. Wait.

September is the worst time to buy. Right now every team in this league is looking at its roster with the most optimism it will feel all year. New Orleans looks at Missi today and sees a cornerstone. Ask them in late January, after the standings have told them the truth, and that same question gets a very different answer.

So if the Pelicans are saying no right now — and they have said no multiple times — why keep calling? Take the same package to the deadline and see if the answer changes.

But let me be fair and give you the argument against waiting, because it's real and I don't want to pretend it isn't.

Waiting can also make the price go up. If Missi takes a leap this season — if he becomes a genuine defensive anchor in front of a national audience — New Orleans won't be more willing to trade him. They'll want more. The bargain you're waiting for might never appear.

And there's a cost to the waiting itself. If Towns or Andre Drummond misses time in December, New York is playing James Wiseman real minutes in games that decide seeding. That's the risk you accept by being patient.

So it's a genuine gamble either way. I still lean toward waiting, because I'd rather risk a few uncomfortable December games than spend the last first-round pick this franchise owns in September, on a player another team has already told you no about three times.

Now, one more thing, because I hear this a lot and I want to push back on it a little.

People say the Knicks have "mishandled" the center position all offseason. I understand the frustration. I've shared it. But I don't think that word is quite right.

Look at what actually happened. Mitchell Robinson got an offer from Boston that New York couldn't match under the apron. Ariel Hukporti got an offer from Philadelphia. Missi was pursued repeatedly and refused. Ryan Kalkbrenner, Nick Richards, other names — all explored.

That's not a front office ignoring the problem. That's a front office that attacked the problem and kept getting told no, because it's operating with almost no draft capital, almost no cap room, and a target on its back.

You can fairly criticize the outcome. The roster does have a real weakness, and it might cost them games. But "they didn't try" isn't accurate, and neither is "they got outsmarted." They got priced out. There's a difference, and it matters.

What they should be criticized for, if anything, is planning. They didn't use the draft to address the center spot, apparently confident a trade would materialize. That confidence turned out to be wrong. That's the fair critique.

And there's a lesson in that for the deadline. Confidence that a deal will come together is not a plan. If New York walks into February assuming Missi will finally be available, and New Orleans says no a fourth time, then this team plays a playoff series with Towns, Drummond, and whoever won the fourteenth roster spot in October.

So the smart version of patience isn't just waiting for Missi. It's building a real list — Missi, Diabaté, and two or three names nobody is talking about yet — and being ready to move on whichever one actually becomes gettable.

And that brings us to where this actually stands. The Knicks are still working two tracks at once — camp competition on one side, trade conversations on the other.

The camp track will most likely end with James Wiseman taking the fourteenth roster spot, as we covered in our last video. That's the short-term answer.

The trade track goes quiet until the deadline, when Missi and Diabaté both come back onto the board — and when a few teams that think they're contenders right now will have found out otherwise.

And one last note on expectations. The market currently has Oklahoma City on top for the title at plus two-forty, San Antonio at plus two-eighty, and then the Knicks tied with Philadelphia at plus one thousand.

Tied with Philadelphia. The defending champions. That's what the whole league thinks of our center depth, in one number.

Fix that, and everything else about this roster is already in place.
