The title says an incredible trade is sending a superstar power forward to the Knicks. Let’s slow down right away. There’s no trade. There’s no superstar. And there’s no power forward. What we actually have is a trade proposal from Grant Hughes at Bleacher Report, written up by Adam Taylor at Heavy. In that idea, the Knicks would send Mikal Bridges and two twenty twenty-seven second-round picks, their own and one from Washington, to the Los Angeles Clippers. In return, the Knicks would get Max Strus and Derrick Jones Jr. That’s it. Nobody has reported that the Knicks and Clippers are talking. It’s a columnist’s idea. But it’s an idea worth talking about, because it touches the two biggest questions around this team right now: what’s going on with Mikal Bridges, and how the Knicks are going to pay for this roster in the years ahead. So let’s break it down honestly. What the proposal is, why someone would suggest it, what it gets right, what it gets wrong, and whether the Knicks should even think about it.

First, let’s be fair to the idea. Why is anyone proposing this? Because Bridges has had the worst start of his career. Through the first eight games, he’s been shooting under thirty-five percent from the field and under thirty percent from three. He’s averaging about six points a game. Against Brooklyn last Monday, he played sixteen minutes, went one of seven, and was pulled with eight minutes and forty-four seconds left in the third quarter. He didn’t come back, and the Knicks won the fourth quarter twenty-eight to eleven. Two nights later against Chicago, he scored seven points in a thirty-point blowout. So yes, there’s a real basketball problem. When Bridges isn’t scoring, defenses can sag off him, the floor gets smaller for Jalen Brunson and Karl-Anthony Towns, and other players have to work harder to create.

And there’s a real money problem too. The Knicks are only about eight hundred twenty-four thousand dollars under the second apron. Above the first apron, they can’t take back more salary than they send out in a trade. And James Dolan has made it clear he doesn’t want the team crossing the second apron. He’s called it suicidal. Bridges makes about thirty-three point five million this season, and his contract keeps climbing. About thirty-six point two million next season, about thirty-eight point eight the year after, and a forty-one point five million player option in twenty twenty-nine thirty. That’s four years and one hundred fifty million dollars in total. So when columnists look for a way to create flexibility, his name comes up first. This week alone, Stefan Bondy of the New York Post called Bridges the Knicks’ most expendable big salary, and Sean Deveney at Heavy wrote that the Knicks should consider trading him. And now Grant Hughes has put a specific package on the table.

So let’s look at the package. What are the Knicks getting?

Max Strus is a shooter. He went undrafted, worked his way into the league, and became a key part of Miami’s run to the NBA Finals in twenty twenty-three. He’s been a big-shot maker in the playoffs before, and he can play the wing in a spread offense. The proposal’s argument is that Strus would give the Knicks a reliable floor spacer around Brunson and Towns. That’s a fair point. The concern is health. Strus has dealt with injuries, and you can’t assume he’ll be there every night. A defending champion can’t build its playoff rotation around a player it isn’t sure will be available.

Derrick Jones Jr. is the more interesting piece. He’s an elite athlete, he won the Slam Dunk Contest in twenty twenty, and he’s turned himself into a real wing defender. He was a key defender for the Mavericks on their run to the twenty twenty-four Finals, and he’s been with the Clippers since. Hughes argued that Jones could defend opposing wings as well as the current version of Bridges. Maybe, on some nights, given how Bridges has started. But that’s the key phrase: the current version of Bridges.

And here’s the biggest part of the proposal, at least on paper. Both Strus and Jones are described as being on expiring contracts. So if the Knicks made this trade, they’d get two useful role players for this season, and then next summer they could decide to keep one, keep both at a lower price, or let both walk. Bridges’ thirty-six point two million for next season would be gone. And the Knicks would have a lot more breathing room under the second apron.

Now, the proposal says the Knicks could remove about eighteen million dollars in salary commitments over the next three years. I want to be careful with that number, because it depends on what you count. Bridges is owed about one hundred sixteen and a half million dollars over the three seasons after this one, if he picks up his player option. If both Strus and Jones walk after this season, the Knicks would clear all of that. If the Knicks re-sign one or both of them, the savings would be much smaller. So the eighteen million figure only makes sense under certain assumptions. The bigger point stands: trading Bridges for expiring contracts would free up a lot of future money.

Let’s also do the math for this season, because that’s what matters for whether a trade is legal. Above the first apron, the Knicks have to send out at least as much salary as they take back. Bridges makes about thirty-three point five million. If the two players coming back make less than that combined, the Knicks would actually be fine. They’d be taking back less money. They’d also be sending out one player and getting two, so they’d go from fourteen standard contracts to fifteen. That works on the roster. The more complicated side would be the Clippers, who would be taking on more salary this season and a lot more in future years. So the question isn’t whether the Knicks could do it. It’s whether Los Angeles would want four years of Bridges’ contract in exchange for two expiring role players and two second-round picks.

And let’s think about it from the Clippers’ side for a second, because a trade needs two teams. Why would Los Angeles want this? The argument would be that Bridges, at his best, is exactly the kind of two-way wing a contender needs, and that his slump is a buying opportunity. If you believe he’ll be back to normal by the spring, you’d be getting a championship starter for two role players. But the risk is huge. You’d be taking on one hundred sixteen and a half million dollars in future salary for a thirty-year-old who’s in the worst stretch of his career. And you’d be giving up two players on expiring deals, which are exactly the contracts teams like to keep for their own flexibility. So even if the Knicks wanted this trade, I’m not sure the Clippers would. That’s another reason to treat this as a column, not a negotiation.

There’s also the question of how Strus and Jones would fit with the Knicks’ current rotation. The Knicks already have a crowded group of guards and wings. Brunson, Hart, Anunoby, Shamet, McBride, Clarkson, Alvarado and Kolek all need minutes. Adding two more wings, while subtracting one, doesn’t make the rotation simpler. It makes it more crowded. Brown would have to find minutes for Strus as a shooter and Jones as a defender, and someone else would fall out of the rotation. Shamet, who has been one of the Knicks’ best shooters this season, might be the one who loses the most. That’s not an upgrade. That’s a reshuffle.

So now let’s do the honest evaluation. Is this a good trade for the Knicks?

Here’s my answer. Not right now. And I want to explain why, because it’s not just about loyalty to Bridges.

Reason number one: you’d be selling at the lowest point. Eight games is a tiny sample. Last season, Bridges also had a terrible stretch. He went seven games shooting around thirty-one percent from the field and twenty-three percent from three. Then he followed it with ten games shooting about fifty-three percent. And in the playoffs, he started all nineteen games, averaged thirteen and a half points on almost fifty-six percent shooting, and scored fourteen with four assists in the Finals clincher against San Antonio. After a scoreless Game Three against Atlanta in the first round, he shot about fifty-eight percent from the field and thirty-nine percent from three over the next sixteen games. That’s the player the Knicks won a title with. Not the player from the first eight games of November. Trading him after eight bad games means you get paid for the slump, not for the player.

Reason number two: the return is two role players, not a replacement. Strus can shoot. Jones can defend. But Bridges at his best does both, and he does it every single night. He’s one of the most durable players in the league. At the end of last season, he had the NBA’s longest active streak of consecutive games played, more than six hundred thirty in a row. You’re not replacing that with one player who has had injury issues and another who is a specialist. You’re replacing it with two half-solutions.

Reason number three: the Knicks would be giving up picks to dump salary. The proposal has the Knicks sending two second-round picks. The Knicks do have a lot of second-round picks, so that part isn’t a disaster. But think about what it says. When the Knicks traded for Bridges, they gave up five first-round picks, a pick swap and a second-round pick, plus Bojan Bogdanović, Shake Milton and Mamadi Diakite. Now this proposal has them paying more picks to move him. An executive told Heavy this week that the Knicks probably wouldn’t even get one first-round pick back if they traded Bridges right now, and that it would probably be a player-for-player deal. This proposal is exactly that, plus the Knicks attaching picks. That’s the definition of selling low.

Reason number four: the locker room. Bridges, Brunson and Josh Hart won a national championship together at Villanova in twenty sixteen. They won an NBA championship together last June. That connection was a big part of why the Knicks made the trade in the first place. That doesn’t make Bridges untouchable. But you don’t break up that core over an eight-game slump.

Now, the proposal does get some things right, and I want to give it credit.

It’s right that the money is a real problem. ESPN’s Bobby Marks projected that if the Knicks pick up the options for Towns, Hart and Tyler Kolek next summer, they’ll have nine players under contract and be only about fourteen point seven million dollars under the second apron for twenty twenty-seven twenty-eight. That’s not much room to fill out a roster. And there’s the Towns question. Towns has a sixty-one million dollar player option for next season. The deadline for veteran extensions on multi-year deals passed in October, so the Knicks can’t simply extend him during the season unless he declines that option as part of a new deal. Hart has a twenty-two point four million team option. So the Knicks will have to make hard choices, and Bridges’ rising salary is right in the middle of all of it. If you cleared Bridges’ thirty-six point two million for next season, the Knicks’ cushion under the second apron would jump to around fifty million. That’s a real argument.

It’s also right that Bridges has to play better. Mike Brown has already shown he won’t hand out minutes based on reputation. Bridges is still starting, but he hasn’t been closing games. Landry Shamet, Jordan Clarkson, Tyler Kolek and Miles McBride have all finished games ahead of him at different times. That’s the right approach. Earn your minutes.

But here’s the thing. The trade deadline isn’t until February eleventh. There’s no reason to make this decision in November. If Bridges is still shooting under thirty percent in January, the conversation changes. If he bounces back, the way he did last season, this proposal will look silly. Patience costs the Knicks nothing right now. A bad trade could cost them a championship.

And let’s be clear about the title one more time. Superstar power forward? Max Strus is a shooting wing. Derrick Jones Jr. is an athletic defensive wing. Neither one is a superstar, and neither one is a power forward. They’re solid role players. The headline was written to make you think a star was coming. Nobody is coming, because nobody is negotiating.

So what should the Knicks actually do? Here’s my plan.

Step one: be patient with Bridges until at least the middle of December. Keep him in the starting lineup, but keep closing with whoever is playing well. That’s what Brown is already doing.

Step two: focus the trade market on the real need, which is a backup center who can protect the rim. On December fifteenth, Andre Drummond, Jose Alvarado, Jordan Clarkson and Mohamed Diawara become trade-eligible. That gives the Knicks more small salaries to combine. A two-for-one for a rim protector is much more realistic, and much more useful, than moving Bridges for two wings.

Step three: if the money crunch is real next summer, deal with it next summer. That’s when teams have cap space, when Bridges’ value may have recovered, and when the Knicks will know exactly what Towns and Hart are going to cost.

And here’s one more angle that nobody in the proposal talks about. If the Knicks ever do move Bridges, they should try to get something that helps the roster’s biggest hole, not just two expiring contracts on the wing. The Knicks already have Shamet, Clarkson, McBride, Kolek and Alvarado. They have plenty of guards and wings. What they don’t have is size and rim protection behind Towns. A Bridges trade that brings back two more wings doesn’t fix that.

Let me also say something about the way this story is being covered. This week, we’ve had Bondy’s column, Deveney’s column and now Hughes’ proposal, all about Bridges. That’s a lot of noise. But if you read them closely, none of them is a report that the Knicks are shopping him. Bondy even wrote that there are plenty of reasons why trading Bridges is stupid, and that a salary dump would be bad business. The Knicks themselves haven’t said anything. And nobody on the Knicks beat, not Shams Charania, not Marc Stein, not Ian Begley, not Fred Katz, has reported any calls. Columns are columns. Reports are reports. A proposal is an idea, and an idea is not a deal. Don’t let a headline turn one into the other.

Now let’s talk about what Bridges needs to do to make all of this go away. First, get to the free-throw line more. He didn’t take his first free throw of the season until the Brooklyn game. That tells you he’s settling for jumpers instead of attacking. Second, stay aggressive in transition, where he’s always been at his best. Third, keep doing the little things when the shot isn’t falling: defend, move the ball, crash the glass. He had moments like that against Chicago. And fourth, just shoot through it. Bridges has been a career thirty-seven percent three-point shooter. Shooters come out of slumps. That’s what history says.

And finally, think about what the Knicks have done without much from Bridges. Coming into the Cleveland game, they were four and four, with four wins in their last five games. They scored eighty-four points in the first half against Chicago and tied a franchise record. Brunson looked like himself again, with twenty-two points in the first quarter. The bench has carried them. Imagine what this team looks like when Bridges gets back to being the player who started all nineteen playoff games last season. That’s the upside you’d be giving away in this trade.

One more thing about Derrick Jones Jr., because I don’t want to undersell him. He’s exactly the kind of player Mike Brown likes: athletic, willing to guard the other team’s best wing, and happy to do the dirty work. If the Knicks could get a player like that without giving up Bridges, I’d be all for it. But that’s the point. Players like Jones are role players you add around your core. They’re not the players you trade your core for.

So here’s my verdict. The proposal is interesting as a thought experiment. It’s right about the money. It’s right that Bridges has to be better. But as an actual trade, right now, it’s selling a championship starter at his lowest value, for two role players, and paying picks to do it. I’d say no. Ask me again in January, when we have twenty-five or thirty games of evidence instead of eight, and when the Knicks know a lot more about what this roster really needs.

A more honest title would be something like, Bridges for Strus and Derrick Jones Jr.? Why the Knicks should say no for now. Or, the Bridges proposal that saves money but costs a title.

So let me ask you. Would you trade Mikal Bridges for Max Strus and Derrick Jones Jr. today? If not, how long would you give Bridges to turn it around: until December fifteenth, until the deadline, or all season? And if the Knicks do make one move before February, should it be about Bridges, or about finding a backup center? Let me know what you think, and I’ll see you in the next one.
