Alright, let's get started.

First, the reporting. Two pieces, and both matter.

Marc Stein and Jake Fischer have both indicated that Mike Brown is a fan of James Wiseman. Not a scout. Not a front office executive. The head coach — the man who decides every rotation minute of this title defense.

And piece two is the structural part. The Knicks are expected to open the season with only fourteen standard contracts unless another salary-clearing move happens. They're at thirteen right now.

So that's the whole picture. Five Exhibit 9 veterans — Wiseman, Drew Eubanks, Bruce Brown, Ochai Agbaji, John Konchar — and one job. Unless the Pacôme Dadiet domino falls, there is exactly one golden ticket, which makes the identity of the coach's favorite very important.

Now, why does a coach's opinion matter more here than it usually would?

Because of what he actually saw.

Every other evaluator in this league is judging Wiseman on two data points — a six-year-old draft slot and a September workout. Mike Brown is judging him on years of proximity. He was in the Golden State building when Wiseman arrived. He saw the practices nobody filmed, how the kid took coaching, how he processed schemes, what the athleticism looked like at full health from ten feet away.

And crucially, he also saw everything that went wrong. The rushed timelines. A dynasty roster with no patience for a project. Confidence eroding in real time.

So when a coach who witnessed the entire rise and fall comes out the other side still believing, that's not a press conference pleasantry about a new signing. That's an informed opinion from the best-positioned evaluator on the planet.

It doesn't guarantee anything. But it means the belief survived contact with the truth. Most belief doesn't.

And there's something real about coach-player reunions generally. A coach inherits hundreds of players over a career. The ones he goes out of his way to bring back are usually the ones whose work habits he trusted behind closed doors.

Think about what a reunion actually buys the player. A coach who can explain his mistakes in a vocabulary he already speaks. A system taught by the same voice that taught it the first time. And the most valuable thing of all for a player on his last NBA life — patience. Not unlimited patience. Champions don't have that to give. But a coach who believes will hand a struggling big a third and fourth chance in an October practice that a stranger never would.

For a player whose entire career problem has been never getting consecutive healthy months inside one stable system, that might be the only thing that matters.

Now let's read the medical file with clear eyes, because belief without context is just wishing.

A genuinely promising rookie year, ended by a torn meniscus. The entire following season gone. Then the bouncing — Golden State to Detroit, some flashes in Detroit, then a fade. And then the cruelest one. First game of his Indiana chapter, a torn Achilles.

Total NBA games over the last two seasons: five.

That's the case against, and it is not a small case. A knee, a lost year, an Achilles. Bodies that have been through that sequence don't owe anybody a comeback. Anyone selling this as an obvious decision is selling something.

So here's my framework, and I want it on the record before a single preseason game.

James Wiseman makes this roster if and only if he passes three tests.

Test one: health. Not "medically cleared" — visibly whole. Back-to-back intense practices. Real preseason minutes. Contact, with no protective handling.

Test two: defense. Nobody needs him chasing stats. They need him protecting the paint, calling coverages out loud, and not leaking possessions through mental errors. In this scheme, communication is ability.

Test three, and this one is about the soul: the role. There will be nights he doesn't play at all. Nights Drummond eats every backup minute. Can a former number two overall pick — a man who was supposed to be a franchise — accept being the third center on somebody else's championship and stay ready in the dark?

Swallow that, and the redemption story writes itself. Choke on it, and no amount of athleticism saves the seat.

And here's the useful part. Every one of those three tests is visible to you during preseason if you know where to look.

Health shows up in the second half of back-to-backs. Does the explosion survive fatigue, or does he start finishing flat-footed?

Defense shows up in the scramble — when a guard rejects the screen and the coverage breaks. Does Wiseman point, talk, and recover? Or does he freeze for half a second? That half second is the whole evaluation.

And the role test shows up on the bench. Watch him during the minutes he doesn't get. Coaching staffs absolutely notice who stays engaged when the towels are waving.

Now let me make the basketball case, because there's one specific thing he'd bring that this roster genuinely does not have.

This offense generates some of the most open rim opportunities in basketball. Brunson's drives pull in two defenders. Towns' pick-and-pop drags the opposing center twenty-five feet from the basket. OG Anunoby and Mikal Bridges bend the corners.

And what's missing from that buffet? A true lob threat.

Drummond catches and finishes, but at this stage he's a below-the-rim craftsman. And that matters more than it sounds, because it changes how teams are allowed to guard us. If there's no vertical roller, an opposing center can sit back in drop coverage, protect the paint, and dare Brunson to finish over him. Put a six-eleven, twenty-five-year-old athlete rolling into that vacated space, and drop coverage suddenly has a price.

That's the dimension this roster hasn't fielded since Mitchell Robinson left. Ten minutes a night of that against second units, and by January it might be real rotation value.

And there's one more thing worth saying about his age, because it separates him from everybody else in this competition.

He's twenty-five. Which means there's a live possibility that the player you have in March is meaningfully better than the one you kept in October.

Think about how rare that is at the bottom of a championship roster. The fourteenth man is almost always a finished product — you know exactly what he is on the day you sign him, and that's it for the season. Wiseman is the only name in this camp where development is still on the table.

And this organization has made a skill out of exactly that. Look at what happened with Landry Shamet last year, and Mohamed Diawara. These aren't teams that hand out camp deals and hope. They develop.

So the honest version is that Eubanks is the safer October decision and Wiseman is the better March bet — and a season is played in March, not October.

Now the counterargument, stated with full respect, because champions don't get to romanticize.

This is not a rebuilding team with twenty spare minutes for a project to find himself. If Wiseman misses defensive rotations, botches positioning, or simply isn't physically right after everything his body has been through, Mike Brown cannot keep him for what he might become in 2028. Trust is the currency of a title defense, and Eubanks earns trust faster by default.

Because look at what Eubanks is. Twenty-nine years old, eight NBA seasons, screens, physicality, competent reserve minutes. No assembly required. There's nothing left to discover — and that's both the criticism and the entire point.

And hovering over both of them is the question that won't go away. What if Bruce Brown is simply the best basketball player in this camp? All eighty-two games last season, thirty-nine percent from three, a championship ring, and four positions of defense.

Then the front office faces the oldest dilemma in roster building. Need, or best player.

And here's the contradiction I keep circling, which I think is the most interesting thing in this whole story.

Every single thing this front office has done since June has been an upside bet. Young bigs with tools. Camp invites with something left to prove. Rebounding and length over polish. That's a doctrine, and Wiseman is the purest expression of it.

But a title defense argues for the opposite. Championship teams usually buy floor, not ceiling. They buy the guy who will not hurt you in April.

So the Wiseman decision isn't really about Wiseman. It's this front office deciding whether its own summer philosophy survives contact with a championship defense.

Here's where I land.

If he passes all three tests, take him. Not for nostalgia, not for the draft slot, but because the need is real, the upside towers over the field, and the one coach qualified to know says there's something left.

If he fails health or defense, no sentiment. Take Eubanks and sleep fine.

And if Bruce Brown just outplays everybody in the room, take Bruce Brown, and go solve the center problem with a trade instead — which is what this front office probably should have done in July anyway.
