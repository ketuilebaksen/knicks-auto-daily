So when the video says the Knicks can increase their extension offer, that’s not quite how it works right now. The negotiation isn’t over. But the next real decision point is next June.

Now let’s talk about the trade talk, because that’s what makes headlines. The video cites Sean Deveney at Heavy and a site called HITC, saying the Knicks have discussed the possibility of moving Towns if it becomes clear he might leave in twenty twenty-seven. Let me be careful here. Deveney writes columns. He talks to executives, and those quotes are worth hearing, but a column is not the same as a report that the Knicks are shopping Towns. And honestly, the trade math tells you how unlikely this is. Towns makes about fifty-seven million dollars. The Knicks are above the first apron, so they can’t take back more salary than they send out. Any team trading for Towns would have to send back players making close to fifty-seven million, or less, and the Knicks would need to get a star-level return to stay a contender. There just aren’t many deals like that in the league. We looked at one a few weeks ago, a Towns for Bam Adebayo idea. The salary math worked. But no one has reported anything like it, and the Knicks would be trading their most consistent player in the middle of a title defense.

So what’s the real risk? It’s this. If Towns declines his option next summer and walks as a free agent, the Knicks could lose him for nothing. That’s why people keep bringing up a trade. The idea is, if you think he’s leaving, you trade him before he can walk. But here’s the thing. We have no evidence that Towns wants to leave. He was disappointed about the extension talks, sure. But he’s played great, the team is winning, and he’s part of a championship core. Being disappointed about money is not the same as wanting out. Players get frustrated in negotiations all the time. Most of them still end up signing. The ones who really want out usually find a way to let everybody know, and Towns hasn’t said or done anything like that. He’s just kept playing, and playing well.

And the Knicks have one huge advantage if this goes to free agency: Bird rights. Because Towns is already on the Knicks, they can re-sign him even though they’re over the cap. Other teams would need actual cap space to sign him. Back in October, Bondy quoted an anonymous executive saying Chicago and the Clippers had the cleanest paths to cap space in the twenty twenty-seven free agency class, and that the best free agent offers would probably be around forty-five to fifty million dollars a year. Think about what that means. If the best outside offer is around forty-five to fifty million a year, the Knicks’ offer of about two hundred million over four years, around fifty million a year, isn’t actually that far off the market. That’s a big reason why I think this eventually gets done. The Knicks can offer more years, more total money, and a team that just won a title. Very few teams can match all three of those things at once. ESPN’s Brian Windhorst has said he expects an eventual deal too.

Now let’s talk about Jalen Duren, because the video makes a fair point here. And the video quoted SNY’s Ian Begley citing an executive who suggested the Knicks would probably have to offer Towns more than Duren got.

I understand that argument. Towns is a multiple-time All-Star with a long track record, and he’s one of the best offensive big men in the league. From his side, why take the same total as a young center who hasn’t done what he’s done? But there’s another side. Duren is much younger. He’s at the start of his prime, and Detroit is paying for the next five years of his career. Towns turns thirty-one this month. A four-year deal for Towns would take him into his mid-thirties. Teams pay for the future, not the past. So the Duren comparison works for Towns’ agent in a negotiation, but it doesn’t mean the Knicks are wrong.

They’re trying to fit Towns, Brunson, Bridges, Hart and Anunoby under a hard line.

So what are the real options? Let me lay them out simply.

Option one: Towns picks up his sixty-one million dollar player option. He stays through twenty twenty-seven twenty-eight, and the Knicks deal with the long-term question a year later. This is the simplest path, but it pushes the money crunch into next season.

Option two: Towns declines his option and signs a new long-term deal with the Knicks next summer. This is probably the most likely outcome if both sides want to stay together. It could even lower his number next season, giving the Knicks a little more room, in exchange for more years.

Option three: Towns declines and leaves in free agency. That’s the nightmare scenario, but based on what we know about the market, it’s not the most likely one. With the best outside offers projected around forty-five to fifty million a year, and the Knicks holding his Bird rights, the Knicks are in a strong position.

Option four: the Knicks trade him before the February eleventh deadline. This is the one in the headlines, and I think it’s the least likely. You don’t trade your most consistent player during a title defense unless you’re sure he’s leaving, and there’s no sign of that.

Let me also put the money in simple terms, because the numbers can get confusing. Two hundred million over four years is about fifty million a year. Towns makes fifty-seven this season and can get sixty-one next season by picking up his option. The max would be around sixty-nine million a year over four years. And the outside market, according to that executive Bondy quoted, might top out around forty-five to fifty million a year. So the Knicks’ offer is roughly at the top of the outside market, but well below what Towns is making right now and well below the max. That’s the gap. And that’s why both sides have reasons to wait.

So here’s my take. Trading Towns should be the last resort, and right now, it’s not even close to that point. The Knicks are winning. Towns is playing at an All-Star level. And the money gap between the two sides isn’t as huge as it sounds when you look at the market. The original offer was two hundred million over four years. The max was around two seventy-six. Somewhere in between, there’s probably a deal that works for both sides next summer, maybe with more years, maybe with a slightly lower first-year number, maybe with a different structure.

Remember how Towns got here. Right before the twenty twenty-four twenty-five season, the Knicks traded Julius Randle and Donte DiVincenzo to Minnesota to get him. At the time, a lot of people questioned whether Towns and Brunson could work together, and whether Towns could handle the physical side of playing center in the East. Two seasons later, the Knicks are champions, and Towns has been one of the biggest reasons why. He’s averaged a double-double, he’s spaced the floor for Brunson, and he’s held up in the biggest games. That trade worked. The Knicks shouldn’t let a contract standoff undo it.

What could a compromise look like? I don’t know the exact numbers, and nobody outside the room does. But you can imagine a few ideas. More total years, with a lower number each season. A deal that starts a little lower and rises over time. A shorter deal with a player option at the end, so Towns gets flexibility and the Knicks get a lower price in the early years. Every one of those ideas gives each side something. And the best time to find that kind of deal is next summer, when both sides know exactly where the cap is going and how this season ended.

And I want to say something about Towns as a player, because the money talk sometimes makes people forget. He’s a seven-footer who can stretch the floor, score from anywhere, and rebound at an elite level. His shooting forces defenses to guard him at the three-point line, and that opens the paint for Brunson. Take him out of the lineup, and the whole offense changes. That’s why the Knicks went and got him. And that’s why, after a title, they should be very careful about letting money push him out the door.

There’s also the human side of this. Towns has been through a lot in his career. He waited a long time to win, and he finally did it in New York. A championship builds a bond between a player and a franchise. I’m not saying that means he’ll take less money. But I don’t think he’s looking for the exit either.

Now let’s be fair to the Knicks too. They’re not wrong to be careful. A max contract for a thirty-one-year-old center, on a team right up against the second apron, is a real risk. If Towns gets hurt, or if his game starts to decline in his mid-thirties, a huge deal could make it impossible to keep the rest of the core together. Leon Rose has to think about the next four years, not just this one. So both sides have a fair argument. And when both sides have a fair argument, the usual answer in the NBA is a compromise, not a trade.
