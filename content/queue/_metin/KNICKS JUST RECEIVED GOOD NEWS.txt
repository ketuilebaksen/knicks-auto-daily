Good news number one is about the mood of the building, and I know that sounds soft, but stay with me, because it's the most important thing on this list.

The Knicks are champions. And the thing that ends most championship runs is not a trade or an injury. It's what happens in the room after the parade is over. So the first question at media day wasn't about the roster. It was about whether this group could stay hungry.

Josh Hart was asked about the weight of the second apron and all the history that says teams like this get broken up. And he waved it off. He said, in effect, that we write our own history, that the apron and the CBA are the past, and that none of it has any precedent for what happens next. We're not worried about it, he said. Then Jalen Brunson, who gave probably the most useful answer of the day. Bad news number one is Josh Hart's finger.

Let me tell you what's actually been reported, because the headline version of this story is a lot scarier than the real one.

Steve Popper of Newsday noticed in training camp that Hart had the same finger that he injured last year heavily taped up again. It's the ring finger on his right hand. He had surgery on it last offseason, and it was reaggravated early in the season. Someone on social media asked about it, and Hart replied directly. His words were, "Just so I don't got to answer this question anymore. There is no other surgery for me to get besides a joint replacement. If I get a joint replacement, I can't play with it and my career is over. So it will be wrapped until I retire."

So let me be exact about the headline. The injury isn't ending his career today. It's that the only remaining fix would end it. He's choosing not to have that fix, and he's choosing to play on a finger that's damaged.

SNY described it as an enlarged joint at the knuckle, with the finger angled off to one side. Hart's own description is more matter of fact. It's not the prettiest to look at, he said. I would love to have a straight finger. But it works. Some days it's fine. Some days it hurts to catch the ball. It varies. And on the worst days, it hurts just to close his hand into a fist.

Then he said the part that stuck with me. When he was asked about the options, he said the choices are to wrap it up until he retires, or get the joint replacement and let it go, or, quote, "I guess, amputation." And he said he'll probably do the joint replacement when he retires, in the hope that it makes the finger a little straighter.

I want to pause on how remarkable this is. This is the player who, with that finger, shot forty-one point three percent from three last season, the best mark of his career. He did that while the injury, in his own words, was getting worse.

So here is how I'd hold two thoughts at once.

The first is respect. A player is deciding to put his body on the line, and as far as I can tell the only thing driving that is wanting to win. You can say whatever you like about Hart, but that is not a player who is coasting after a championship.

The second is the part the video I watched mostly skipped, and that's the business side. We are in the middle of a period where Hart's contract is a live conversation. Ian Begley reported weeks ago that extension talks were active, and I haven't seen a finished deal. This kind of injury doesn't change what Hart is on the court. His shooting last year says it hasn't. But it does change what a team thinks about long-term risk on a player who has chosen to manage a chronic problem rather than fix it.

And I think that's the right way for a front office to look at it. Not as a reason to doubt him, but as a reason to design the contract carefully, with the numbers and the years matching a player who relies on his hands every night.

Now, the steelman for the worried side. A finger doesn't sound like a big injury, but this is a player whose game is built on rebounding, loose balls, catching passes in traffic and absorbing contact. A hand that hurts to catch the ball on a bad day is a real handicap. And the team's whole philosophy this season is no slippage. If Hart's shooting dips by even a few points, or if he loses a step on the glass, the cost shows up in the margin that a repeat requires.

The counter is that he has already played an entire season this way, and played it at the highest level of his career. He isn't asking anyone to take a leap of faith. There's a full year of evidence.

So my answer is that this is a real concern, but a manageable one. What I'd watch in the preseason is simple. Whether the finger is taped, which we already know. Whether he's catching in traffic without hesitation. Whether his minutes are managed in the early weeks, which is consistent with what Brown said about easing people in. And whether he looks like the same defender and rebounder who made the last year work.

If those four things look normal, I'd stop worrying.
