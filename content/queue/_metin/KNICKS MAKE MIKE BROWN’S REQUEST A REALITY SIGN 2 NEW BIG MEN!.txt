Mike Brown walked into media day and asked for adversity. Early. On purpose. Because he wants to know, before it matters, whether this group can take a punch.

Alright, let's get started.

Media day was Monday. The first official practice was Tuesday. The preseason opener is October fifth. And the defending champions are back.

Brown opened by saying that no matter what this team accomplished last season, the standard it holds itself to and the way it approaches its daily process will be critical going forward. And then he added the line that set the tone for everything.

The Knicks cannot afford any slippage in that process.

Notice what he didn't talk about. No banners. No rings. No highlight reel. He talked about habits — the small details that separate a champion from a team that just had one great year.

And he's getting ahead of something before it starts. Every team that reaches the top faces the same danger. Players get comfortable. The hunger that carried them through a playoff run quietly fades. People call it the championship hangover, and it has wrecked plenty of talented rosters.

By putting the word "slippage" on the table on day one, Brown is telling his players he knows the risk — and that he expects them to know it too.

Now here's why it means more coming from him than it would from almost any other coach in this position.

Before New York, Mike Brown spent several seasons as an assistant with Golden State, where he was part of multiple championship teams. He watched a group successfully defend a title and win back-to-back. And he watched that same group fall short chasing a third.

So he's seen both halves of this. The repeat that worked, and the one that didn't. That's a complete picture, and very few coaches can offer it.

Then he was asked about adversity, and his answer is the part I keep thinking about.

He said most people try to avoid adversity, especially early in a season, because it brings scrutiny. He said he actually wants it. He wants it early — because he wants to know his group can handle it.

That is not a normal thing for a coach to say out loud. Most of them spend October trying to protect their team from noise. Brown is asking for the test while the stakes are still low, so that nothing in April is the first time this group has been uncomfortable.

And remember what we talked about recently — every opponent is going to circle this team on the calendar. Teams that coast against other opponents will show up with playoff effort against the champions. Brown would rather find out in November who flinches.

He also pushed back on the thing most of us have been leaning on all offseason. Continuity.

A lot of people believe that bringing back most of the same core protects the Knicks from the usual early-season bumps. Brown agreed that keeping a core together is exciting, especially one that's already won — and then immediately added that this is a new season and a new team, and that New York will face new challenges.

And he's right, even though it's not what fans want to hear.

The faces are familiar, but a locker room is never the same twice. Some players are entering contract years. Others just signed new deals. Some roles are about to grow, and some are about to shrink. Every single one of those changes touches chemistry, motivation, and the rotation.

So Brown isn't pretending continuity solves everything. He's preparing his team for the fact that it doesn't.

Put all of it together and you get a coach who isn't riding the high. He named the three threats to a repeat — complacency, the target on their back, and changing roles — and he sounded eager to go solve each one.

If the biggest risk to a returning champion is a step back from the bench, that does not look like a concern here.

Alright, now the practice floor. Five things from day one.

Number one, and it's the most important. Deuce McBride could not miss.

Christian Winfield of the New York Daily News reported that McBride was red-hot in three-point drills — that he hadn't missed from the moment reporters walked into the gym.

Now, a shooting drill in October is not a playoff game. But look at the position he's in. He's in the final year of his deal, he wants a new contract here, and the apron plus the Towns negotiation has turned him into the most discussed trade chip in the league.

Almost none of that is in his control. What is in his control is exactly what he did on Tuesday.

And a hot start opens three different doors for him. One, his trade value rises, which helps New York land the backup center it has been chasing all summer. Two, he plays well enough that the front office finds a way to keep him. Three — and this is the uncomfortable one — he plays so well that he prices himself out of what the Knicks can afford.

But notice that all three of those outcomes are good for the franchise. A breakout season from your sixth man is never a problem.

Number two. Andre Drummond made the best first impression available.

At the end of practice, with the whole team facing conditioning sprints, Drummond stepped to the free throw line with everyone's legs on the line. He made it. Nobody ran. His teammates lost their minds.

Light-hearted moment — but it points at something real, and I want to correct something I said about him recently.

Drummond struggled badly at the line early in his career, and that reputation has stuck. But he's improved a lot. He shot sixty-two point two percent and fifty-nine point two percent from the line in recent seasons. And last year he attempted ninety-three three-pointers, a career high, making thirty-five point six percent of them.

I previously described his three-point volume as a handful of attempts. That was wrong — ninety-three attempts at thirty-five point six percent isn't a novelty, it's a real part of his game now.

And here's why both of those numbers matter for this specific team. Opponents hunted Mitchell Robinson at the free throw line in the playoffs, and there were moments where New York simply had to take him off the floor because of it. A backup center who can be trusted at the line in a close fourth quarter gives the coaching staff flexibility it did not have.

Number three. James Wiseman got the private session.

Five veterans came to camp on tryout deals. When practice ended, Mike Brown pulled Wiseman aside for what looked like a hands-on, one-on-one coaching conversation.

We covered the Brown-Wiseman history from their Golden State years, and the reporting that Brown is a fan. This is that relationship showing up on the floor rather than in a quote. Combine it with the need at center, and there's a growing sense he's the favourite among the tryout group.

But the decision still depends on what he shows over the next three weeks. Belief gets you the extra session. It doesn't get you the roster spot.

Number four, and this one I genuinely love. Mikal Bridges spent time one-on-one with rookie Tyler Nickel.

And there's a detail that tells you who Bridges is. During the championship parade, he wore Pacôme Dadiet's jersey — putting the spotlight on one of the least-known players on the roster, on the biggest day of all of their lives.

Nickel already has the shooting half of the three-and-D profile. Bridges has built an entire career on the other half. If you were designing a mentor for that rookie, you'd design Mikal Bridges.

And number five, the unexpected one. Rajon Rondo was in the gym watching practice.

Rondo has been exploring a coaching career — he worked as a guest assistant with Doc Rivers in Milwaukee, and he interviewed for the head coaching job in New Orleans. Jalen Brunson said afterwards that he got to know Rondo over the summer, calling him an icon from when he was younger and praising his creativity as a player.

Nobody has said this points to a job. But a mind like that around a team defending a title is never a bad thing.

Alright, last thing. Karl-Anthony Towns finally spoke.

For weeks, the biggest unanswered question around this team has been his contract. At media day, he addressed it publicly for the first time.

Let's restate the numbers, because they're the whole story. Towns has two years left on the four-year, a hundred and twenty point four million dollar deal he signed before arriving in New York. The final season of it is a sixty-one million dollar player option for 2027-28. And he's eligible for a four-year extension worth up to two hundred and seventy-six million.

His case is strong. In the playoffs he averaged fifteen point nine points, ten point six rebounds, and four point nine assists on a championship team. He wasn't along for the ride — his rebounding and his passing from the frontcourt gave this offense a dimension it doesn't otherwise have.

At the same time, the front office has to think past one contract. Every dollar committed to one player changes what's possible for everybody else on a title-winning roster.

And here's my read on him speaking up, which might be counterintuitive.

Frustration expressed publicly, in measured terms, is usually a good sign. The dangerous version of this situation is silence — a player who stops engaging and lets his representatives handle everything while the relationship quietly cools. A player who stands at a podium and talks about it is a player who still wants it resolved.

So no, I don't think media day made a deal less likely. I think it made it more likely.
