Let's begin with a rule that shapes everything from here, because almost nobody mentions it and it changes the entire calendar.

Players who sign as free agents in the offseason generally cannot be traded until December fifteenth. That covers Andre Drummond. That covers every single one of those Exhibit 9 camp guys — Bruce Brown, John Konchar, Ochai Agbaji, Drew Eubanks, James Wiseman.

So if you're imagining the Knicks winning a camp battle in October and then flipping somebody in November, that's not available. Between now and mid-December, the only players New York can actually move are the ones who were already here.

That's why every rumor keeps landing on the same two or three names. It isn't lazy reporting. It's the rulebook.

Okay. The board. Tier one: genuinely gettable.

Name number one, Moussa Diabaté. Twenty-four years old, six-ten, two and a half million dollars, seven point nine points and eight point seven rebounds a night. Charlotte just went to eighteen guaranteed contracts and has to be at fifteen by opening night.

He's at the top of this board for one simple reason. He's the only target whose team has an actual incentive to talk right now. Everybody else is a "no" waiting to be re-asked.

Name number two, Johni Broome. The young big Philadelphia dumped on the Clippers with a pick attached. Los Angeles has seventeen players under contract and can keep fifteen, and they've been stripped of draft capital, which means a second-round pick actually means something to them.

Two different teams. Same squeeze. And that squeeze is the only reason either of these names is realistic.

Now tier two: possible, but expensive.

Name number three, Yves Missi. Twenty-two, six-eleven, elite per-minute rim protection, and honestly the best long-term fit on this entire board. He's also been asked about repeatedly and refused every single time. New Orleans wants an unprotected first or a swap, and New York's only tradable first is in 2033.

Name number four, Ryan Kalkbrenner. Now here's the part I actually wanted to get to. Because we spend all our time talking about who the Knicks want, and almost none talking about what they can pay with.

Let's rank the currency.

Asset number one: Deuce McBride. He's the most valuable trade piece this team has after Jalen Brunson, full stop. Twelve points a game, forty-one percent from three, and legitimate point-of-attack defense on a cheap contract. Every rebuilding team in the league would take that call.

But there's a complication we've covered. The league raised its cap projection, which made keeping him realistic again. So spending McBride isn't just a trade anymore — it's choosing to give up a player you can now afford.

Asset number two: the 2033 first-round pick. The only one left. Everything else went into building this champion. And a pick seven years out is both the most valuable thing on this list and the scariest thing to give away, because nobody has any idea what this roster looks like in 2033.

Asset number three: Pacôme Dadiet. Two point nine million, with a team option for 2027-28 worth about five point three million. Multiple insiders have reported the Knicks exploring a move to clear his salary. He isn't a headliner. He's the salary filler that makes bigger trades legal, and the move that opens the second roster spot.

Asset number four: second-round picks. New York has a real stockpile, and they're perfect for exactly one thing — convincing a team that's over the roster limit to hand you a player they were going to lose anyway. That's the Broome scenario. It is not the Missi scenario.

So look at that list honestly.

One genuinely valuable player you'd rather keep. One draft pick you're terrified to spend. One salary filler. And a pile of seconds that buy small things.

That's the whole wallet. That's why this has been so hard all summer, and it has almost nothing to do with the front office being asleep.

Now let me put the two lists together, because that's where it gets clear.

If you want Missi or Kalkbrenner — the two best long-term answers — you're spending McBride or the 2033 pick. Maybe both.

If you want Diabaté or Broome — the two realistic ones — seconds plus salary filler probably gets it done.

So the real question of this offseason isn't "which center." It's "how much of the future are you willing to spend to fix seventeen minutes a game?"

And here's my honest answer.

I'd spend Dadiet and seconds on Diabaté or Broome right now, today, because the cost is genuinely low and the roster spot problem gets solved along the way.

I would not spend the 2033 pick in October. Not for anybody on this board. If New Orleans finally says yes in February, after their season has told them the truth, then we talk seriously. In October, a team that just won a championship has no business burning its last first-round pick to solve its fifteenth roster spot.

And I'd hold McBride unless a genuine difference-maker comes available — someone clearly better than everyone on this board. Because trading your best asset for your fourth-best target is how teams end up with neither.

And let me answer the question I know is coming in the comments, because somebody always asks it. Why not just keep everybody and roll with what we have?

Honestly? That's a defensible position, and I want to give it a fair hearing.

You won a title with this group. Towns is one of the most skilled offensive centers alive. Drummond still rebounds at an elite rate for his minutes. Whoever wins the camp battle gives you a third body. And the biggest risk to a championship defense usually isn't a thin bench — it's health and chemistry, neither of which improves by adding a new face in October.

There's also the version where the answer is already in the building. Mohamed Diawara is six-nine and shot well from the corner last season. OG Anunoby has played center in stretches. Mike Brown has shown he'll go small when the matchup calls for it.

So the case for standing pat is real, and it isn't lazy.

Here's why I still don't buy it. Standing pat assumes Towns and Drummond both stay available. Drummond is in his fifteenth season. And going small isn't a plan you choose — it's a plan you're forced into, usually in a playoff game, usually against the one team built to punish it.

You don't buy insurance because you expect a fire. You buy it because the cost of being wrong is enormous.

One more thing before we go, and it's the piece I think people underrate.

This board changes in December. Not because the Knicks get smarter, but because the calendar opens up. On December fifteenth, everybody signed this summer becomes tradable — ours and everybody else's. Suddenly there are hundreds more movable contracts across the league, salary matching gets far easier, and teams that started six and fifteen begin to accept who they are.

That's when the Missi conversation gets real. That's when a name nobody has mentioned becomes the answer.

So if nothing happens in the next few weeks, don't read it as failure. Read it as a front office that knows which month it's in.
