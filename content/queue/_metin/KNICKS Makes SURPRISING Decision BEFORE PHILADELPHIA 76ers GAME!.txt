Welcome back to Ketuil KNICKS. It's ring night at Madison Square Garden, and there's a lot to cover before tip-off. Let's get into it.

First, the report. Marc Stein wrote that, according to a source briefed on the talks, the Knicks "did not shop reserve guard Tyler Kolek to try to make roster room for Bruce Brown before Brown's release Saturday." And he added that Kolek "would only have been dealt before Tuesday's season opener" if "the deal brought a coveted center to New York."

So let me be precise about what that means. The Knicks weren't calling teams saying, "take Kolek, we need the roster spot for Bruce Brown." They were open to moving Kolek only in one kind of deal: one that brought back a center they actually wanted. A roster-clearing dump? No. An upgrade at center? Yes, they'd listen.

Now, how does that fit with what we heard last week? Ian Begley of SNY reported that the Knicks would explore other deals, including ones involving Kolek, over the weekend and Monday, as they tried to keep Bruce Brown. At first glance, those two reports seem to contradict each other. But I don't think they do. "Exploring deals involving Kolek" and "not shopping Kolek" can both be true. A team can listen to offers, ask what something might bring back, and think through scenarios, without actively trying to give the player away. Begley described the possibilities on the table. Stein described what the Knicks were and weren't willing to do. That's how front offices work: lots of conversations, very few offers.

Actually, this is a good moment to explain how to read trade language in NBA reporting, because it'll help you all season. When a reporter says a team is "exploring" deals, it usually means the team is having conversations and asking what's possible. "Gauging value" means asking other teams what a player might bring back, often without any intention of moving him. "Shopping" a player means actively trying to trade him, calling teams and pitching him. "Active discussions" means two specific teams are talking about a specific framework. And "close" or "finalizing" means a deal is basically done. Those words are chosen carefully by the best reporters. When the headline turns "exploring" into "trading," that's where most of the misinformation starts.

And here's where I owe you a correction. In the last few videos, I said the market had said no to Kolek, and that no one would take him or Dadiet on the Knicks' terms. I also quoted a Post line that Kolek had little value around the league. For Dadiet, the reporting that teams weren't biting still stands. But for Kolek, Stein's report suggests something different. The Knicks may never really put him on the market for a salary dump at all. So it's not that nobody wanted Kolek. It's that the Knicks weren't willing to give him away for nothing. That's a meaningful difference, and it says the front office values him more than some of the coverage, including mine, suggested.

So the real decision before tonight wasn't "Kolek or Bruce Brown." It was this: the Knicks would rather keep a twenty-five-year-old point guard on a cheap deal with a team option than trade him to keep a thirty-year-old veteran for one season. And if they're going to move Kolek, they want something real back. Specifically, a center.

And that tells you the most important thing in this report. The Knicks are still looking for one more center.

Think about where they are. Karl-Anthony Towns is the starter. Andre Drummond is the backup, and Mike Brown has been raving about his communication. James Wiseman made the team as the third center, but he's played only a handful of NBA games in the last two seasons and is coming back from an Achilles tear. There's no two-way center. And Mitchell Robinson, the best rim protector they had, is in Boston. So a young, athletic, cost-controlled center is the one thing the front office keeps circling back to, all the way back to the offer sheet for Moussa Cissé in July and the attempts to trade for Yves Missi.

And think about why a center is the priority, not a guard or a wing. Mike Brown has hinted that Towns could play a little more power forward this season. That only works if there's another big who can handle the center spot for real minutes. Drummond is thirty-three. Wiseman is talented but hasn't played a full season in years. And look at who the Knicks might face in the playoffs. Joel Embiid in Philadelphia. Mitchell Robinson and Neemias Queta in Boston. Jakob Poeltl in Toronto. Victor Wembanyama if they get back to the Finals. That's a lot of size. A young, athletic rim protector on a cheap contract would help in all of those matchups, and it would give the Knicks a long-term answer behind Towns as the core gets older.

So who might count as a "coveted center"? There's been no reporting naming a target, so let me be careful. Fans and writers have floated names. Dan Favale of Daily Knicks has suggested Moussa Diabaté in Charlotte. The Missi idea from the summer still comes up. And Cissé himself may become available if Dallas waives him to keep Dwight Powell. Those are ideas and situations, not reported talks.

But here's the math that matters, and it shows why Kolek alone usually isn't enough. The Knicks are above the first apron, so they can't take back more salary than they send out. Kolek makes about two point three million. Diabaté makes about two point four six million. So even a one-for-one Kolek-for-Diabaté swap wouldn't be legal on its own, because the Knicks would be taking back about a hundred and sixty thousand dollars more than they send. Adding draft picks doesn't fix that, because picks don't count as salary. You'd need to add a contract. Dadiet, at about two point nine eight million, would work alone for Diabaté. And McBride, at just under four million, could bring back a bigger salary.

So realistically, a "coveted center" deal probably looks like Dadiet alone, or Kolek with something else, or McBride plus one of the young players, with second-round picks attached. That's why the same combinations keep coming up.

Let me lay out the calendar, because it shapes everything. Right now, the Knicks' tradable non-starters are mostly Kolek, Dadiet and McBride. On December 15th, players who signed or re-signed this summer become tradable, including Drummond, Clarkson and Diawara. Around the middle of January, Wiseman becomes tradable, three months after signing his standard deal. January 10th is the league-wide guarantee date, when non-guaranteed contracts around the league lock in, which tends to shake loose some players. And the trade deadline is in early February. So the Knicks' options grow in steps. If they want a center before Christmas, the pieces are limited. If they wait until the deadline, they'll have a lot more ways to make the salaries work.

And timing matters. Most teams wait until mid-December, when this summer's signings become tradable, to make real moves. And as some analysts have pointed out, teams that are struggling by the deadline, possibly a team like Charlotte or New Orleans, sometimes accept offers in February that they turned down in July. So if the Knicks land a center this season, I'd expect it closer to the deadline than to opening night.

Now, Deuce McBride, because his name is always in this conversation.

Stefan Bondy of the Post has predicted that McBride will be traded at some point before February's deadline, noting there's been no traction on his extension. His colleague Jared Schwartz made the bigger point: under owner James Dolan's insistence on staying under the second apron, it will be extremely difficult for the Knicks to have all five starters on big contracts and still have room to give a significant contract to a bench player. That makes McBride's expiring contract the most likely to be moved, rather than risk losing him for nothing next summer.

And the numbers behind Schwartz's point are simple. This season, the five starters alone make about a hundred and ninety-two million dollars: Towns at about fifty-seven, Anunoby at forty-two and a half, Brunson at about thirty-seven point seven, Bridges at about thirty-three and a half, and Hart at about twenty-one. And Towns and Hart are both looking for new deals. If the Knicks pay them, there's almost nothing left for a sixth man on a bigger contract under the second apron. McBride's next deal, at market value, might be worth eight, ten, maybe twelve million a year. That's not a lot for most teams. For this one, it's the difference between staying under the apron and crossing it.

And there's precedent for that kind of thinking with this front office. They traded Immanuel Quickley to Toronto in December of 2023 as part of the deal for OG Anunoby, rather than paying him a big contract in restricted free agency. And they traded Julius Randle in the deal for Karl-Anthony Towns in 2024. Leon Rose has shown he'll move a popular player when the long-term numbers don't work. And both of those trades worked out. Quickley and RJ Barrett went to Toronto, Anunoby came back and became one of the best defenders on a title team. Randle and Donte DiVincenzo went to Minnesota, and Towns came back and became the second-best player on a champion. So when Knicks fans worry that McBride could be next, the honest answer is that this front office's history with these kinds of moves is pretty good. That doesn't mean McBride is gone. But it does mean the Knicks won't sentimentally hold onto him and watch him walk away for nothing.

But remember what his agent, Mark Bartelstein, said this weekend. "If the Knicks aren't ready to do extensions right now, then we just gotta buckle up and go have a great season." And: "There may be opportunities during the season, maybe in the near future." And McBride is the only one of the four extension-eligible Knicks who can sign a new deal during the season without an option being declined first. So there are two real paths. A trade before the deadline, or an extension if the front office decides he's worth it. I'd still keep him for now. He's one of the best perimeter defenders on the bench, and the Knicks need that tonight against LeBron, Jaylen Brown and Tyrese Maxey.

As for Kolek himself, let me remind you what he actually did last season, because his value isn't just theoretical. Sixty-two games, about twelve minutes a night, four point four points and two point seven assists, shooting almost thirty-nine percent from three. And in the NBA Cup final, with a trophy on the line, he had fourteen points, five rebounds and five assists. That's a player who has already produced on a big stage, on a contract of about two point three million, with a team option for next season at about two and a half. No wonder the Knicks didn't want to give him away for a one-year veteran.

Now, let me push back on one thing I heard in some coverage this weekend. A few fans and channels said Pacôme Dadiet has "no future in the NBA," that he'll end up overseas, that he has no value. I think that's too harsh. He's twenty-one. He's six-nine, long and athletic. He was the twenty-fifth pick in 2024. Yes, he's raw, and yes, he shot poorly in the preseason. But plenty of players his age look exactly like this before something clicks. The Knicks have until October 31st to decide on his fourth-year option for 2027-28, worth about five point three million. Declining it wouldn't mean he can't play. It would mean the Knicks don't want to commit five million dollars to finding out. And his contract, as we covered, is actually one of the most useful trade pieces they have, because his salary is the right size to match a cheap center. So I'd stop writing him off. He might not be part of the Knicks' future. But his career isn't over at twenty-one.

And this connects to a bigger theme. The Knicks have one of the oldest cores in the league. Brunson is thirty. Towns is thirty and turns thirty-one next month. Hart is thirty-one. Bridges is thirty. Anunoby turns thirty next summer. Under the second apron, the Knicks can't keep buying veterans. They have to develop younger players: Kolek, Diawara, Dadiet, two-way players like Tyler Nickel and Jaden Akins, and Dink Pate in Westchester. That's why keeping Kolek instead of trading him for a one-year veteran makes sense. If he's traded, it should be for a younger player who helps more, not for an older one who helps less.

Now, let me give you the honest counterargument to everything I just said. The Knicks are defending champions. Their window is right now. Bruce Brown is a proven, tough, versatile defender who played every game last season and knows how to win. Kolek played eight playoff games last season, mostly in garbage time. So you could argue that by refusing to give up Kolek, the Knicks chose a backup point guard's future over a real veteran's help in a title defense. I don't agree, mostly because Brown would have been the eleventh or twelfth man when everyone's healthy. But it's a fair argument, and if the bench defense struggles in November, people will make it.
