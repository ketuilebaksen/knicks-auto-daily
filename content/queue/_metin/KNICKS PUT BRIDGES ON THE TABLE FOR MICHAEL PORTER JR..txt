That didn’t happen. There is no report from Shams, from ESPN, from The Athletic, from the New York Post, from SNY, or from anyone else that the Knicks are trading Bridges for Porter. And if this sounds familiar, it should. We covered a Michael Porter Jr. to the Knicks story a few weeks ago, and it was fake then too. Same player, same First Take scene, same quote: I can confirm this trade is going to happen. So today, let’s do it right. Let’s go through how you can tell this story is made up, what’s actually true underneath it, and then let’s do the real math, because the real math is way more interesting than the fake quote.

Let’s start with the tells, because once you see them, you’ll see them everywhere.

Tell number one: the First Take scene. Every one of these videos has Shams sitting next to Stephen A. Smith, calmly saying, I can confirm, this trade is going to happen. That’s not how Shams breaks news. He posts it. He reports it with details, the teams, the players, the picks. He doesn’t go on a debate show and say a trade is going to happen without saying it’s agreed. If Shams had actually reported a Knicks trade for Michael Porter Jr., it would be on every sports site in the country within five minutes. It isn’t anywhere.

Tell number two: the personal story. This video spends almost a minute on the narrator sitting on his back step, fixing his niece’s kite, with the string flying over the fence when he heard the news. It’s a nice bit of writing. But that’s exactly what it is: writing. When a video spends more time on how the narrator heard the news than on the news itself, it’s because there’s no real news to talk about. The same goes for the running joke, here a lawn sprinkler, that gets repeated all through the video. That’s a storytelling device, not reporting.

Tell number three: the video tells on itself. Listen carefully and the narrator says the status is expected, not official, and that nobody has reported the Nets said yes. That’s the video admitting there’s no deal. Then it goes right back to talking like there is one.

Tell number four: Stephen A.’s verdict. The video has Stephen A. telling the Knicks to close the deal today, and saying Brooklyn has sold this kind of player before. Even inside the video, those are presented as his words and his urgency, not as a report. That’s a debate segment wrapped around a trade that doesn’t exist.

And here’s why these videos keep coming back with the same structure. They work. A big name, a fake quote from the most trusted reporter in basketball, a funny story about how the narrator heard it, and a running joke to keep you watching. Then, buried in the middle, a line saying it’s not official. By the time you hear that line, you’ve already clicked, watched and maybe shared. That’s the business model. And the best defense is simple: if Shams really reported it, you’ll see it everywhere. If you only see it in one video with a kite and a lawn sprinkler, it didn’t happen.

So, no trade. But here’s the thing. The reason these videos keep working is that they’re built on real facts. So let’s separate the real facts from the fake trade.

What’s real? Mikal Bridges has had the worst start of his career. He’s averaging around six points a game on about thirty-one percent shooting, and about twenty-seven percent from three. The video says SNY’s Ian Begley noted that Bridges has sat out the fourth quarter in three games. That fits what we’ve seen. He sat the final eight and a half minutes in Boston. He didn’t play in the fourth quarter in Chicago. And against Brooklyn, he was pulled with eight minutes and forty-four seconds left in the third and didn’t come back.

Also real: the money columns. Stefan Bondy of the New York Post wrote that Bridges is the Knicks’ most expendable big salary. Sean Deveney at Heavy wrote that the Knicks should consider trading him. But Bondy also wrote that there are plenty of reasons why trading Bridges is stupid, and that a salary dump would be bad business. Those are opinions, not reports of talks.

Also real: the contract situation. Karl-Anthony Towns turned down a four-year, two-hundred-million-dollar extension offer, according to Bondy. The talks have stalled. Towns has a sixty-one million dollar player option for next season. Josh Hart has a team option. And James Dolan doesn’t want the Knicks to cross the second apron. So yes, the Knicks have real money questions.

And also real: Michael Porter Jr. is on the Nets, he won a championship with Denver in twenty twenty-three, and he’s on an expiring contract worth about forty point eight million dollars this season. That’s the piece that makes this fake trade sound smart.

Let’s also remember how Porter ended up in Brooklyn in the first place. In the summer of twenty twenty-five, Denver traded him to the Nets for Cam Johnson, and Denver attached a future first-round pick to the deal. Think about that. A year and a half ago, a team was willing to give up a first-round pick just to move Porter’s contract. Now the contract is expiring, which makes it more attractive. But it also tells you that teams have had mixed feelings about Porter for a long time, mostly because of his history of back injuries. He had back surgeries early in his career. When he’s healthy, he’s a terrific shooter at six-ten. The question has always been whether he stays healthy.

Now let’s fix the numbers the video gets wrong, because a couple of them matter a lot.

The video says Bridges’ contract rises to forty-one and a half million dollars in twenty twenty-seven twenty-eight. That’s not right. Bridges makes about thirty-three and a half million this season. Next season, twenty twenty-seven twenty-eight, he makes about thirty-six point two million. Then about thirty-eight point eight. And the forty-one and a half million is the player option in twenty twenty-nine thirty, the last year of the deal. So when the video says a Porter trade would clear forty-one and a half million off next year’s books, it’s using the wrong number. The real savings for next season would be about thirty-six point two million.

The video also says the Knicks are zero million under the second apron. The real number is about eight hundred twenty-four thousand dollars. That sounds like a small difference, but in a trade like this, it decides everything.

And the video says the Knicks have few draft picks left after giving up five first-round picks for Bridges. That’s partly true, and there’s a twist the video completely misses. The Knicks owe their twenty twenty-seven, twenty twenty-nine and twenty thirty-one first-round picks to Brooklyn. To the same Nets team in this fake trade. So the only first-round pick the Knicks can trade right now is in twenty thirty-three. They do have a lot of second-round picks. But here’s the twist: Brooklyn owns the Knicks’ future picks. The worse the Knicks are, the better those picks are for Brooklyn. So why would the Nets send the Knicks a player who makes them better? That’s the opposite of what Brooklyn wants. Every win Porter helps the Knicks get makes Brooklyn’s picks a little worse.

Now, the real math. Could the Knicks actually do Bridges for Porter? Let’s check.

The Knicks are above the first apron, so they can’t take back more salary than they send out. Porter makes about forty point eight million. Bridges makes about thirty-three point five million. So Bridges alone is about seven point three million short. Straight up, the trade is illegal for the Knicks.

So you add players. Bridges plus Miles McBride plus Pacôme Dadiet comes to about forty point four two million. That’s still short. By about three hundred eighty-four thousand dollars. Three hundred eighty-four thousand. That’s how close it gets, and how close is still not enough. In a normal trade, a team would just throw in a minimum player or a second-round pick and move on. But above the first apron, the Knicks can’t take back even one extra dollar, so close doesn’t count.

Add Tyler Kolek, and you’re at about forty-two point seven million. Now the salary works. But it’s four players for one. The Knicks would drop from fourteen standard contracts to eleven, and they’d need to sign three players to get back to the minimum. With only about two point seven million dollars of room under the second apron after the trade, they can’t afford three minimum contracts. So that version fails too.

After December fifteenth, Jose Alvarado becomes trade-eligible. Bridges, Alvarado and McBride come to about forty-one point nine million. The salary works. But it’s three for one, so the Knicks need two minimum signings, and they’d only have about one point nine million in room. Still not enough.

The first version that really works is after January fifteenth, when Landry Shamet becomes trade-eligible. Bridges, Shamet and McBride come to about forty-two point nine million. That clears Porter’s salary. The Knicks would need two minimum signings, and they’d have about two point nine five million in room. Two rookie minimums cost about two point seven two million. That leaves about two hundred thirty thousand dollars to spare. Legal. Barely. And by then, minimum contracts are prorated for the rest of the season, so the math gets a little easier.

So the honest answer is: Bridges for Porter can’t happen today. The earliest clean version is in mid-January, and it costs the Knicks Bridges, Shamet and McBride. That’s three rotation players, two of whom, Shamet and McBride, have been some of the Knicks’ best bench players this season. Shamet had twenty-two against Brooklyn. McBride had one of the best plus-minus numbers on the team in Cleveland.

There’s also one more detail that makes this harder for Brooklyn. Bridges has a five point six nine percent trade bonus in his contract. That makes him a little more expensive for any team that trades for him. And Brooklyn would be taking on four years of Bridges, with his salary rising every season, in exchange for a player whose contract ends in June. For a rebuilding team, that’s a big commitment.

Now let’s talk about the basketball side, because the fake video skips it.

Porter is a great shooter. He’s one of the most efficient scorers at his position when he’s healthy. He’d give Brunson and Towns more spacing. That’s the appeal. But Bridges is a defender. Even in this slump, he’s still guarding the other team’s best wings, and in Cleveland, he had season highs of six rebounds and two blocks with four assists. Swapping Bridges for Porter would make the Knicks better on offense and worse on defense. And if the deal costs Shamet and McBride too, it also makes the bench a lot thinner.

Think about how Porter would actually fit, too. He’s a forward. The Knicks already have Anunoby at one forward spot and Towns at the other, with Towns sometimes sliding to the four when Drummond plays. So Porter would most likely play Bridges’ role on the wing, which means guarding the other team’s best perimeter scorer some nights. That’s not his strength. On a team that has given up big scoring nights to Jayson Tatum, Tyrese Maxey and Donovan Mitchell already this season, losing your best wing defender is a big risk. You’d be fixing one problem, outside shooting, and creating another.

And here’s the other thing. Shooting hasn’t really been the Knicks’ biggest problem lately. In the last two games, they scored one thirty-five against Chicago and one-oh-nine on the road in Cleveland, with only seven turnovers. Brunson is back. Towns is rolling. The bench is producing. The biggest hole on this roster is still the backup center spot, a rim protector behind Towns. Porter doesn’t fix that. A trade that uses three rotation players to get another scoring forward doesn’t touch the real need. If the Knicks are going to spend their limited trade assets, they should spend them on size, not on another wing.

And there’s the chemistry piece. After the loss in Cleveland, Cavaliers coach Kenny Atkinson talked about the Knicks’ continuity. He said three of them played in college together, talking about Brunson, Hart and Bridges, who won a title at Villanova in twenty sixteen and an NBA title together last June. Opposing coaches see that chemistry as a strength. Trading Bridges breaks it up, and you can’t rebuild that in a month.

And let’s be honest about the Nets’ side too. Brooklyn is rebuilding. The video says they’re two and eight. They lost to the Knicks one-oh-three to ninety at the start of last week. And they’re stacking young players and picks, including the Knicks’ own picks. A rebuilding team usually wants to move veterans on expiring deals for young players or draft picks. The Knicks have very little of either to offer. Dadiet is young, and Diawara is young, but neither is the kind of prospect that headlines a deal for a player like Porter. And the only first-round pick the Knicks can trade is in twenty thirty-three. So even if the Knicks wanted Porter, they’d be competing with teams that can offer what Brooklyn actually wants.

If the Knicks ever do trade Bridges, the more realistic market looks like the proposal Grant Hughes wrote at Bleacher Report last week: Bridges to the Clippers for Max Strus and Derrick Jones Jr., two players on expiring deals. Even that one was just a columnist’s idea, and it had the Knicks attaching second-round picks. The executive Deveney quoted said the Knicks probably wouldn’t get even one first-round pick back for Bridges right now, and that any deal would likely be player for player. That’s the honest market. Not a former champion like Porter falling into the Knicks’ lap from the team that owns their picks.

So what about the argument that the Knicks need to clear money for Towns? Let’s look at it honestly. If the Knicks traded Bridges for an expiring contract like Porter’s, they’d clear about thirty-six point two million for next season. ESPN’s Bobby Marks projected the Knicks would be about fourteen point seven million under the second apron next season if they pick up the options for Towns, Hart and Kolek. Add Bridges’ money back, and the cushion would be around fifty million. That’s real flexibility. So the money logic isn’t crazy. It’s the reporting that’s fake, and the timing that’s wrong.

Because here’s the thing. The Knicks don’t have to make this decision now. They’re five and four. They’ve won three straight and five of six. Brunson has back-to-back thirty-point games. Towns just had twenty-nine and thirteen in Cleveland. The trade deadline is February eleventh. Bridges has slumped before and come out of it. Last season, he had a seven-game stretch shooting around thirty-one percent, then followed it with ten games at about fifty-three percent. And in the playoffs, he started all nineteen games and shot about fifty-six percent. If the Knicks trade him now, they’d be selling at the absolute bottom.

One more note on timing. Even the earliest legal version of this trade, in mid-January, would happen just a few weeks before the deadline. By then, Bridges will have played forty games, not nine. If he’s still shooting under thirty percent, the conversation changes. If he’s back to normal, nobody will be talking about trading him at all. Either way, January is when the Knicks will have real information. November is when you get kites, sprinklers and fake quotes.

So let me give you the honest scorecard.

Is there a Bridges for Porter trade? No. There’s no report, and the First Take quote is made up.

Could it happen? Not today. The Knicks would be about three hundred eighty-four thousand dollars short with Bridges, McBride and Dadiet. The first legal version that keeps the roster intact is after January fifteenth, with Bridges, Shamet and McBride.

Would Brooklyn want it? Probably not. The Nets own the Knicks’ future first-round picks. Making the Knicks better makes those picks worse.

Would it make the Knicks better? On offense, maybe. On defense and in the locker room, probably not.

Is the money problem real? Yes, it is. But it’s a next-summer problem, not a November emergency.
