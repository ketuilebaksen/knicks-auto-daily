The money is where it gets hard. Turner's deal works out to roughly twenty-eight million dollars a year over the remaining years. And there's the trade kicker, a full fifteen percent, which can raise his cap number for the team that acquires him. So the real cap figure for the Knicks could be higher than his current salary.

The Knicks are roughly three point two million dollars below the second apron, and ownership has made clear they won't cross it. Above the first apron, a team can't take back more salary than it sends out. So to take on Turner, the Knicks would have to send out at least what he'd count for on their books, probably close to thirty million.

Let me explain the trade kicker for a second, because it's a detail most fans skip, and it matters here. A trade kicker is a bonus a player receives when he's traded. Turner's is the maximum, fifteen percent. The bonus is paid by the team that trades him, but for cap purposes it gets added to his salary on the books of the team that acquires him. The exact amount depends on how much is left on his deal and on the league's maximum salary rules, so I'm not going to give you a precise figure. But the direction is clear. Turner would count for more on the Knicks' books than he does on Milwaukee's. For a team with three point two million dollars of room, that's not a small detail. A player can agree to waive part or all of his kicker to help a trade happen, and that sometimes happens. But you can't assume it.
