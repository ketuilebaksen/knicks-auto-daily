Here's what happened. Charlotte traded veteran forward Dorian Finney-Smith to Atlanta and got back Buddy Hield and Ryan Nembhard. A two-for-one. And that pushed the Hornets to eighteen guaranteed contracts.

NBA rosters max out at fifteen on opening night. So Charlotte has to get rid of three.

And here's the detail that makes this our story. Per Daily Knicks, New York already reached out to Charlotte earlier this offseason to ask about Diabaté.

So the interest is established. What's new is the pressure on the other side.

Now let's look at the player and the price, because both are attractive.

And camp invites are fine for practice reps — but you cannot defend a championship with non-guaranteed contracts as your primary insurance policy.

The fit is obvious too. Diabaté is a mobile, hyperactive rim runner who crashes the offensive glass and can switch onto a guard without getting cooked. In Mike Brown's scheme, having a big who can step out on pick and rolls while Towns operates from the high post gives this team real flexibility.

So how would it actually work? Because this is where it gets technical, and I want to explain it properly.

Daily Knicks suggests New York could package second-round picks or a first-round swap, and bring in a third team to absorb Pacôme Dadiet's two point nine million dollar contract alongside smaller Charlotte salaries — Nembhard at two point two million, or Pat Connaughton at three point eight.

Why does a third team need to be involved? Because of the apron. New York can't simply take back more salary than it sends out. So you need somewhere for the money to go. A third team with space becomes the dumping ground, and everybody gets what they want.

And Daily Knicks even floats the bigger version — if New York isn't planning to re-sign Deuce McBride long-term, he could be dangled too.

Now let me give you the sober counterweight, because I don't want to get carried away and neither should you.

A roster crunch forces cuts. It does not force trades.

Charlotte has to get to fifteen. The cheapest way to do that is to waive the guys on small deals and move on. Nobody is obligated to trade a productive twenty-four-year-old just because the spreadsheet is full.

What the crunch really does is make Charlotte willing to have the conversation. That's genuinely valuable — it's the difference between this and the Yves Missi situation, where New Orleans has flatly said no. But "willing to talk" and "desperate" are not the same thing, and the price will reflect that.

There's also the contract-year problem, and it's the same one we discussed with Missi. Two and a half million is cheap right now. But he's a free agent after this season. So you'd be paying a trade price for a rental, and then paying again to keep him. That's not a dealbreaker — but the true cost is the trade plus the next contract, and people keep forgetting the second half of that sentence.

Now — hold on. Because two days ago on this channel, we covered Marc Stein reporting that James Wiseman was the favorite for that spot, largely because of his relationship with Mike Brown.

So which is it?

Here's my read, and I think the disagreement is actually the most useful information in this whole story.

The two reports aren't measuring the same thing. They're answering two different questions.

If the question is "who's the best player available," the answer is Bruce Brown. It isn't particularly close. A championship connector wing who defends three positions and shoots thirty-eight percent is simply better than a third-string center.

If the question is "what does this roster actually need," the answer is Wiseman. Because we have four guards and wings for every spot and two centers total.

So the argument inside that building right now isn't Brown versus Wiseman. It's best player versus biggest need. And everything we know about how this front office has operated all summer — every camp invite has been a big who rebounds — suggests they lean toward need.

Which means, if I had to guess, Stein's version is closer to the truth. But Wildstein isn't wrong about the talent, and if Bruce Brown dominates camp, that calculus can change fast.

Sporting News adds one more detail worth noting. Drew Eubanks faces an uphill battle specifically because Mike Brown prefers the familiar option in Wiseman, whom he coached in Golden State. So the center race may be narrower than we thought.

And Wildstein floated something else — that New York could trade another depth player just to clear room if Brown outplays his contract in camp.

Which brings us right back to the same name. Again.

Daily Knicks points out that New York is officially running back last year's open tryout approach. And if it feels like déjà vu, that's because it is. In the 2025 offseason, Leon Rose brought in a blend of rookies and veterans on Exhibit 9 and Exhibit 10 deals. Now here's my honest take on the "lightning twice" idea. Last year's camp worked — but let's remember how much of it was luck. Malcolm Brogdon retired unexpectedly, which opened a roster spot nobody planned for. Shamet was a proven NBA shooter who needed a home, not a lottery ticket.

Running the same process is smart. Expecting the same outcome is not a plan.

So let me tie all three of these stories together, because they converge on one move.

Story one needs a salary to send out in a Diabaté trade. Story two says only one roster spot exists because of the apron. Story three has five players fighting for that single spot.

Move Dadiet, and you open the second roster spot, so you can keep both a big and a wing. Move Dadiet into a three-team Diabaté framework, and his contract becomes the salary filler that makes the trade legal.
