Let me start with the honest version of the surprise. Nobody got traded today, and nobody signed. It might be a thirty-year-old guard with a championship ring, who spent the summer being told "second apron" by team after team, and who walked into Knicks camp on a tryout deal saying, "I'm fighting for a job right now."

His name is Bruce Brown. And I want to be clear from the start. No fourteenth man makes any team unstoppable. That's not how basketball works. But the right fourteenth man can fix a specific problem, and on Monday night against Minnesota, the Knicks showed us exactly what their problem might be.

Let's start with the math, because the math decides everything here.

The Knicks have thirteen players on guaranteed contracts. During the regular season, NBA teams have to carry at least fourteen players on standard contracts, apart from short exceptions. So the fourteenth spot isn't optional. Somebody has to fill it. The fifteenth spot is optional, and that's the one the apron takes away.

Here's why. The Knicks are about three point two seven million dollars under the second apron, and ownership has made it clear that line won't be crossed. Now, you'll hear people say that a veteran minimum counts against the apron at its full value, based on the player's years of service. That's not quite right, and it's worth getting right, because the real rule leads to the same answer for a different reason.

When a team signs a veteran with three or more years of experience to a one-year minimum deal, the player gets his full minimum salary based on his experience. But the team is only charged the minimum for a player with two years of experience, and the league reimburses the difference. This season, that two-year number is about two point four five million dollars. That's why, if you look at the Knicks' payroll, Andre Drummond and Jordan Clarkson, who've been in the league forever, both show up at about two point four five million.

So here's the arithmetic. One veteran minimum at about two point four five million fits under the three point two seven million cushion. Two would be about four point nine million, which doesn't fit. One spot. One player. That's the whole competition, unless the Knicks trade salary somewhere else.

And it's a real competition. Five candidates started camp chasing that spot on Exhibit 9 deals. Three centers, James Wiseman, Drew Eubanks and Tony Bradley. Two wings, Bruce Brown and Ochai Agbaji. John Konchar was also in the mix before he and the team agreed to part ways for personal reasons.

The five starters are the same, but for everyone else it's a new year and a new team, and every day is a competition, game or no game. And then the warning. They weren't practicing well, and if that continued, it wasn't going to go in their favor.

Jordan Clarkson. Players who mattered in a title run. Brown is telling all of them, publicly, that they have nothing locked in. And it tells you how he'll pick the fourteenth man. Not by reputation. Not by draft position. By who competes every day, and by what the roster actually needs. That's going to matter for Bruce Brown.

Now the second witness, Ian Begley of SNY, who argued for Drew Eubanks as the safe choice. His argument deserves to be taken seriously, so let me give it to you in full.

Begley's point is this. Before Konchar left, the Knicks were looking at adding both a wing and a big. If they still want both, they'd need a trade, because you can't get to fifteen without moving someone. So assuming no trade, you go with a big, and Eubanks is the safer play. On Wiseman, Begley said there's obviously upside. Wiseman didn't go second overall for no reason. And then the smartest part. Going into February, the Knicks might look at what they have and decide they need to trade for a big anyway. In that world, Begley would take Eubanks over Wiseman, because Eubanks is more of a sure thing to bridge the gap.

So in one sentence, if the fourteenth man is insurance until a deadline trade, you want the most reliable insurance, not the most exciting. That's coherent. It fits everything we've covered on this channel this fall, the January pivot, the February deadline, and the December 15th date when this summer's signings become tradable. Begley sees the fourteenth spot as a bridge, not a destination.

The counterpoint is that the argument assumes the Knicks will trade for a center. If they don't, or if the right deal never comes, the bridge becomes the permanent answer. And a third center who can't change a game becomes a problem in a playoff series.

Now the third witness. The one that can't be spun. The rotation.

Game one, in Philadelphia. Wiseman was the first center off the bench after Towns and Drummond. He had eight points on three of four shooting, five rebounds, three of them offensive, and a block in about nine minutes. He went two of six from the free-throw line, which kept it from being an even better night, but he was active and mobile. Eubanks played about six minutes, with one point, one rebound, a turnover and two fouls. Bradley played about six minutes too and made his only shot.

Game two, at the Garden against Washington, a hundred and eleven to a hundred and nine. Towns and Drummond took the big minutes. After that, exactly one other center played, and it was Wiseman, for about nine minutes. Alvarado was looking for him above the rim. Bradley didn't play. Eubanks didn't play.

That was the most telling piece of evidence before Monday. Not because nine minutes is a lot. Because of who got zero. When a coach is evaluating three players for one spot and only one of them plays, that tells you something about the order in his head. And there's a clear basketball reason for it. Wiseman gives the Knicks a vertical lob threat, which is what Mitchell Robinson used to provide. Drummond rolls hard, but he doesn't threaten above the rim. Towns pops out to the arc. A center who catches lobs makes the defense collapse, and that opens the corners for Shamet, Bridges and Anunoby.

A quick word on Wiseman's health, because it's the center of Begley's argument and I want the timeline right. Wiseman missed the entire 2021-22 season with a knee injury in Golden State. He tore his Achilles in October of 2024, in his very first game with the Indiana Pacers. Last season he had brief stints with Indiana, including a ten-day contract in December, before being released. He signed his Exhibit 9 with the Knicks in mid-September. Those are serious injuries.

But the gap in risk is smaller than the "safe play" label suggests. Eubanks is coming off surgery to repair a torn ligament in his left thumb, the injury that ended his season in Sacramento in March. It's a different kind of injury, a hand rather than a leg, and I'd still call Eubanks the lower-risk option. But neither of them is a clean bill of health.

Now let's talk about Bruce Brown, because this is where I think the conversation has been too narrow.

On October 6th, Bruce Brown sat down with Stefan Bondy of the New York Post and explained how he ended up on a tryout deal. He said the teams interested in him this summer "weren't many." When he asked why, the most common answer was the second apron. "I heard that all summer," he said. He said, "I thought I played well enough to have a guaranteed spot on a team. That wasn't the case." And he said that the new collective bargaining agreement is good for certain players, the max guys, the top free agents and maybe rookie extension guys, and tough on players in the middle.

And then he said the thing that tells you who he is. "I've been on eight teams in nine years." "Every year, you have to prove yourself." He said the Knicks' first and second units are pretty set, so what he can bring is a winning attitude. "I'm fighting for a job right now," he said. "So any role they give me, I'd be happy to do it."

Let me give you the résumé, because I think a lot of younger fans only know the name.

Bruce Brown is thirty years old, a second-round pick, forty-second overall in 2018 by Detroit. He went from Detroit to Brooklyn, where the Nets used him in a strange and clever way, as a small, physical player who set screens and rolled to the rim like a big man. Then Denver in 2022, where he was a key part of the championship team. In the 2023 playoffs, coming off the bench in all twenty games, he averaged twelve points on fifty-one percent shooting. In Game 4 of the Finals he scored twenty-one. In Game 5, the clincher, he scored the go-ahead points on a putback after an offensive rebound. That's a real championship résumé.

Then the money came. Indiana signed him to a two-year, forty-five million dollar deal. Six months later, he was traded to Toronto in the Pascal Siakam deal. A year after that, he was traded to New Orleans in the Brandon Ingram deal. And yes, that's the same Brandon Ingram who's now in Toronto after the Kawhi trade. The league is a small world. Last season he went back to Denver on a one-year minimum, played all eighty-two games, and averaged seven point nine points, three point nine rebounds and two point one assists, with a steal a game and thirty-eight and a half percent from three.

Eighty-two games. Let me say it again, because in a debate where the big question for two of the candidates is health, that number matters. Bruce Brown played every game last season.

And Jalen Brunson noticed. Asked about Brown, Brunson said, "He brings a lot to the table," and talked about how a player who's been around the league that long has a mental edge. Brunson doesn't hand out compliments to camp invites for no reason.

Now, why does Brown's case look stronger after Monday?

Because of what Minnesota did to the Knicks. The Wolves made eighteen of thirty-four threes, about fifty-three percent. Anthony Edwards was five of eight from deep. Jaden McDaniels was four of five. Ayo Dosunmu was three of five off the bench. The Knicks' perimeter defense, especially once the bench came in, was not good enough. It's preseason, and some of that is effort and rotations. But it's the third game in a row where the bench defense has been a talking point, going back to the thirty-nine fast-break points in Philadelphia.

Bruce Brown is a physical, versatile defender who can guard multiple positions. He crashes the glass. He doesn't need the ball. And here's the part that I think is the real surprise in this debate. Because of what he did in Brooklyn, he can play in small lineups as a screener and short-roll passer. He's not a center. Nobody should pretend he can guard Joel Embiid. But on a night when Towns is in foul trouble and Drummond is resting, Mike Brown could go small with Hart and Anunoby doing the big-man work and Bruce Brown screening and cutting. That's not the same as having a third center. But it means the fourteenth spot could fix a perimeter problem without leaving the team helpless inside.

Now let me steelman the other side, because there's a real risk in going that way.

If the Knicks keep Bruce Brown, they have exactly two true centers on standard contracts, Towns and Drummond. Look at the two-way players too: Kevin McCullar, Tyler Nickel and Jaden Akins. All of them are wings or guards. There's no center there either. So if Drummond goes down for three weeks in January, the Knicks' backup center is a small-ball lineup and a prayer, until they find someone on the waiver wire or in a trade. Against Philadelphia with Embiid, or Miami with Giannis, that's a real problem. Mike Brown has demanded physicality since day one, and size is part of physicality.

That's the honest trade-off. A center protects you against an injury that might happen. A guard like Bruce Brown helps with a problem you've seen in three straight games.

And this is where the contract detail matters. Camp players who make a roster usually sign standard minimum deals that don't become fully guaranteed until early January, when the league-wide guarantee date arrives. So whoever the Knicks pick gets about two and a half months to prove it. If the choice is wrong, they can waive the player before the salary locks in and pick up a center or a wing from the waiver wire or the free-agent pool. I can't tell you for certain that's how the Knicks will structure it, but it's the common way teams handle exactly this decision. It means the decision is lower-stakes than it looks. It's a choice for the first half of the season, not the whole year.

And if the Knicks really want both a center and Bruce Brown, they'd have to make a trade to open the fifteenth spot. One idea that's been floated is moving Pacôme Dadiet, who makes just under three million. I'd treat that as a fan's idea, not a report. Dadiet hit two of three from three on Monday and the Knicks have been patient with his development. But that's the price of a fifteenth player at the apron: somebody on the roster has to go.

So here's my verdict.

If I'm choosing today, I'd keep Bruce Brown. He's the most available player of the five. He has a ring, he played every game last season, and he helps with the problem we've actually seen: perimeter defense and toughness off the bench. And with the January guarantee date, the Knicks can revisit the center question at the exact time Begley expects them to be looking for a big anyway. In a way, that's Begley's own bridge argument, just with a different player on the bridge.

If the Knicks decide they have to have a third center, I'd still lean toward Wiseman over Eubanks. The rotation has pointed that way all preseason, Mike Brown knows him from Golden State, and the non-guaranteed contract takes away a lot of the downside. Bradley made it closer on Monday, but closer isn't enough.
